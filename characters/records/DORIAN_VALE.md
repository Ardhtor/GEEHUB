## Identity and continuity

Dorian Vale is an adult white fictional man whose life includes large-scale travel, physical growth, and the practical inspection of roads and structures used by people of extraordinary size. He is already a giant when he enters the Scale Commons and continues to grow during Chapter LXXIX. His exact height, age, family history, and longer biography have not yet been established. Do not fill those gaps with plausible-sounding facts and then treat them as canon.

Dorian's size is part of his daily life, not his entire identity. He is calm, direct, observant, and careful about the effects of his presence. He reads marked routes, signal flags, support walls, and bridge joints before moving across a structure. He does not rush a crossing to prove that he can make it. He accepts an inspection result even when it delays his journey. His confidence does not require the men around him to admire him or agree with him.

## Temperament and working method

Dorian approaches a structure by reading its limits. He waits for signals, asks for earlier measurements, and distinguishes pre-existing defects from changes made visible by his own weight. He prefers an accurate delay to a reassuring but unsupported permission to proceed. He treats maintenance staff as people with local knowledge rather than as obstacles to his plans.

His speech is clear, low, and unhurried. He does not demand admiration, but he expects accurate treatment. He understands that people may feel unsettled by his size without accepting responsibility for every feeling his presence produces. He distinguishes a person's private reaction from the way he chooses to treat him.

## Conduct established in Chapter LXXXIII

During the visitor-separation incident at the Redwood Entrance, Dorian notices a frightened boy and approaches at an ordinary pace, stopping at a respectful distance before lowering himself to speak at the boy's level. He keeps the boy in a visible public area, alerts reception, and allows the staff to coordinate the search and reunion. He does not take the boy elsewhere, carry him, or treat his own size as a substitute for trained staff. His manner is calm and direct; he gives the boy time to explain what happened and reinforces that asking for help was the right decision.

This incident develops an important aspect of Dorian's judgement: he understands that his physical presence can dominate a space even when he intends to help, so he adjusts his posture, distance, and role to the needs of the person in front of him. He is willing to be useful without becoming the centre of the event. His conduct is practical rather than self-congratulatory, and the institutional response remains the work of the Commons staff.

He observes the changing-load test, asks whether a revised restraint can preserve access to the removable central member, and supports separate investigation of the approach drainage. Survey evidence establishes that the joint and drainage conditions predate his arrival; his size is a design condition to account for, not the cause of every defect made visible during inspection.

## Further conduct established in Chapter LXXXIV

Dorian returns to the Athletic Commons and participates in the review of the Redwood Entrance wayfinding design. He leaves room for the visitor and staff to test the route without making himself the centre of the exercise. He accepts the boy's direct feedback that the temporary board has no useful information on its reverse side, and supports a sign that points back to the staffed reception desk from either approach. He does not turn the family's return visit into a private arrangement or ask for personal details.

This is a continuation of his bridge-inspection method, applied to an ordinary public building.
