Joey is the Teal Titan incarnate: a monumental adult male figure whose Stage Four body stands thirty feet tall. His size is a physical fact in the novel's world, not a metaphor, camera trick, forced perspective, or exaggeration of an ordinary body. The climax is the six men's recognition of this fact, not a sudden transformation that begins at the end of the book.

## The observation rule and Joey's control

Joey can unfold into his full thirty-foot Stage Four form whenever nobody is looking directly at him. When the men look directly at him, he can make himself appear ordinary-sized. The apparent restriction is not a weakness, involuntary curse, or limitation imposed by the hotel. Joey controls the effect and can reveal his actual form at any moment. He chooses when to show it and when to restrain the men's perception.

The unobserved expansion is the first outward sign of the truth. It produces weight, pressure, shadows, displaced furniture, strained beams, low-frequency sound, altered reflections, and architectural distortions. When a man turns to look, Joey may return to ordinary visible scale. The evidence remains. The men initially use direct sight as the final authority and dismiss what they saw indirectly. Joey understands this habit and uses it to keep the week repeating.

The climax changes the rule by changing the men's recognition. Joey does not lose control of his form, nor does the hotel accidentally release it. He decides to reveal the whole thirty-foot figure openly. He can do so earlier, but withholds the revelation until the sentence has reached its intended reckoning. The first moment in which the men see and accept his full form at once is therefore an act of judgment and disclosure, not an escape from a spell.

## Joey as the devil of the hotel

Joey is the infernal authority behind the Grand Meridian. The hotel is his instrument: a luxurious, carefully managed world in which men can eat, sleep, train, swim, attend performances, admire fine rooms, and continuously postpone the truth. He controls the recurring week and decides when it begins, what traces survive, when a corridor opens, and when his true size is visible. He is responsible for the men's sentence and has a direct part in the crimes for which they are held.

His responsibility is deliberately complicated. Joey arranged the temptations, built the conditions, offered convenient excuses, and helped turn each man's weakness into an opportunity to commit or conceal a wrong. The men retain agency for the choices they made; Joey is not a device that absolves them. But neither is he an impartial judge. He is the devil who engineered the conditions and then made himself the keeper of the punishment. The story must eventually confront his own culpability: he has used their crimes to justify an elaborate system of control, and he takes satisfaction in their failure to recognize him. His sentence may contain truth, but it is also a form of domination.

The men must therefore confront two linked facts. They have committed acts for which they are responsible, and Joey has manipulated those acts into a prison from which he benefits. Recognition of Joey's size is the key that breaks the loop, but it does not automatically absolve anyone. Release begins the harder work of acknowledging what each man did, what Joey arranged, and what must be repaired.

## The repeating week

The visible week runs from Monday to Sunday. At the weekly boundary, ordinary episodic memory resets, while metaxic traces survive. The men repeat because they refuse to accept the whole truth of Joey's body and the truth of their own participation in the crimes. They encounter evidence of his immense scale but repeatedly explain it away as perspective, building failure, optical distortion, a faulty sensor, a dream, or a strange feature of the hotel. Every denial renews the sentence.

The loop is not a mechanical accident and is not self-sustaining without an authority. Joey maintains it deliberately. The metaxy is the medium through which he lets evidence persist while withholding complete recognition. It gives the men enough proof to approach the truth, but not the ordinary memory that would make the work easy. Joey can break the weekly recurrence at will, yet he withholds that release until the men have recognized what they have refused to see and begun to face why they are there.

The hotel is luxurious because comfort is one of the instruments of the sentence. Excellent service, elaborate meals, beautiful architecture, immaculate presentation, exercise facilities, entertainment, and social hierarchy keep the men occupied. Tomorrow always seems like the appropriate time to confront a difficult fact. Tomorrow is repeated. Their captivity is maintained as much by postponement as by locked doors.

## Character temperament and voice

Joey is composed, perceptive, confident, and capable of deliberate cruelty. He can be charming and attentive, but his hospitality is not proof of benevolence. He understands what each man wants to hear and can present the hotel as a reward even while using it as a prison. He does not need to shout or threaten. His authority is most frightening when he is calm, courteous, and exact about what a man has done.

His devilish character must not flatten him into a one-note villain. He is capable of genuine creative attention, humour, patience, pride, pleasure in craft, and perhaps an attachment to the men he has condemned. Those qualities do not excuse his manipulation. His own contradiction is central: he demands that the men acknowledge him truthfully while refusing to acknowledge his own role in creating their crimes and their punishment.

## Narrative progression

The early book presents Joey as an ordinary-sized man in direct view. The men encounter indirect signs of a much larger body when they look away. This pattern escalates through pressure marks, altered room dimensions, reflections, low-frequency sound, overloading sensors, and displaced architecture.

The middle book makes clear that Joey is aware of the phenomenon and controls it. The men begin to suspect that the hotel is not protecting him from observation; he is protecting his sentence from being understood too soon. The Director of Standards, a fictionalized Stanley Tucci and the sole *Devil Wears Prada* carryover, knows how the luxury and presentation of the hotel conceal the moral machinery. He is not the devil; Joey is.

The climax reveals Joey's full thirty-foot Stage Four body. The men recognize the entire physical fact at once and understand that the weekly loop has been sustained by their denial and by Joey's deliberate control. They must then face their crimes and Joey's responsibility for engineering the circumstances. Recognition breaks the time loop; it does not erase the past or grant instant absolution.
