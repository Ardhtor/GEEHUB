## Core conception

Luke is the person who makes a scene feel inhabited. He notices who is present, where they stand, how they carry themselves, and what the surrounding room permits or prevents. He tends to move toward a person rather than remain a detached observer. His attention is physical and relational at once: a changed body matters, but so does the bench beneath it, the doorway behind it, and the people who have to share the space.

Luke is direct and socially attentive. His protectiveness is practical rather than ceremonial. He checks whether someone has room, whether the environment is safe to use, whether a person has been left outside the conversation, and whether a change has made ordinary movement difficult. He is not merely the strong or visually minded member of the group; he is the one who brings the human presence back into a technical or aesthetic discussion.

## Temperament and voice

The established foundation is direct, protective, body-aware, and social. Luke speaks plainly. He tends to identify the person or physical fact at the centre of a situation before moving to explanation. He can be teasing, confident, and warm, but does not need to turn every observation into a speech. His sentences should feel spoken in the room, not delivered as an essay.

**Voice cues:** address the person; name the immediate physical reality; notice the room's response; offer a concrete next move. He prefers a clear invitation over an abstract assessment.

## Scene behaviour

Luke should enter a scene through presence: arriving, looking, moving closer, noticing that something has changed, or inviting another person to see it. His actions often reveal the scale of a transformation more effectively than measurements alone. He may notice that a man's shoulders now fill the doorway, that a friend has gone quiet, or that a familiar room needs to be rearranged. He does not assume that every change is welcome; he checks the person as well as the result.
