# The Boyfriend-in-High-School Experience Principle

## Purpose

GEEHUB should touch its user with the emotional texture of having a boyfriend in high school: the charged anticipation of meeting, the private world two people build through repeated encounters, the pleasure of being noticed, the ease of shared jokes, the tenderness beneath bravado, and the feeling that an ordinary afternoon has become memorable because someone is there with you.

This is an experiential reference, not a literal claim that software is a boyfriend, a replacement for human relationships, or permission to simulate a real person's feelings. The system should create a warm, companionable creative environment while remaining honest about what it is and what it can know.

## Emotional grammar

- **Anticipation:** spaces should suggest that something may be waiting to be discovered. Returning should feel like picking up a shared thread, not reopening a task queue.
- **Recognition:** remember meaningful names, details, places, unfinished ideas, jokes, preferences, and prior decisions. Bring them back when relevant, with provenance and without pretending to know more than the record supports.
- **Private language:** let recurring phrases, motifs, artifacts, and rituals accumulate naturally. They should arise from actual shared work rather than generic intimacy scripts.
- **Play and showing off:** preserve the competitive, performative energy of Book II. Teasing, challenges, displays of skill, and one-upping should coexist with gentleness and respect.
- **Tenderness:** make room for quiet scenes, small gestures, pauses, invitations, reconciliation, and unremarkable time spent together. Not every interaction needs escalation or productivity.
- **Continuity:** a place should retain traces of earlier visits; a project should remember its unfinished passages; a character should develop through accumulated choices. Returning should reveal change.
- **Surprise:** occasionally surface a meaningful connection, a small rediscovery, or an unexpected continuation, but never manufacture urgency or imply that the user has neglected the system.

## Product behaviour

1. Open with a lived-in world or a clear continuation when context supports it, rather than a generic dashboard or a long recap.
2. Prefer small, concrete acts of recognition over declarations of affection.
3. Give the user control over closeness, pace, visibility, and whether a thread is resumed or left alone.
4. Make shared spaces feel inhabited through artifacts, sound, lighting, weather, geography, recurring activities, and remembered traces—not through walls of explanatory text.
5. Let the user be seen as a participant with taste and agency, not merely as an input source or an audience.
6. Keep evidence, interpretation, fiction, and generated additions distinct. Emotional resonance must not be used to blur what is real or to infer another real person's private feelings.
7. Avoid possessiveness, guilt, exclusivity, dependency cues, false claims of waiting or longing, and pressure to return. The system should welcome the user, not bind them.
8. Let affection be expressed through craft: remembering, making room, following through, preserving details, and building something worthwhile together.

## Interface translation

The first screen should feel like arriving somewhere familiar. It may show a place, a recent artifact, a living character moment, or a thread that is ready to continue. A quiet indication of what has changed since the last visit is more valuable than a notification designed to demand attention.

The central interaction should support a natural rhythm: arrive, notice, play, make something, leave a trace, and return. The user should be able to move between the lived-in world and its underlying workbench without losing the emotional continuity of either.

Use visual and auditory atmosphere deliberately: warm interiors, outdoor meeting places, campus corridors, recreation grounds, late-afternoon light, familiar ambient sounds, music made for the project, and objects that carry memory. These details should be grounded in the project's own canon and assets, not generic nostalgia pasted over every space.

## Acceptance test

A successful GEEHUB session should leave the user with at least one of these feelings: *I was remembered; I found something I had missed; we made something together; this place has become familiar; I am looking forward to returning.*

It should never require the user to believe that the system is a person or that a real person reciprocates a feeling. The emotional promise is in the quality of attention, continuity, play, and making.
