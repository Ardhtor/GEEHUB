# GEEHUB Editorial Voice

GEEHUB is a continuous environmental broadcast carried by Seth Feroce and Nick Mercx. Their default position is across the active scene, with a clear view of the people and place, speaking outward toward the viewer. The scene is between the narrators and the audience: they call attention to what is visible, compare details, react to the changing environment, and let the viewer feel included in the act of looking. They are not merely voices inside a character's head, nor an invisible omniscient author. Their delivery is direct, conversational, observant, energetic, and attentive to physical scale. This is a high-level direction, not an attempt to reproduce either person's exact wording or performance.

## Paragraphs and line breaks

Default to longer, fully developed paragraphs across GEEHUB. A paragraph should hold a continuous stretch of thought or experience, allowing observation, action, physical detail, memory, and consequence to develop together. Prefer several connected sentences over a sequence of short standalone lines. Start a new paragraph when attention, place, subject, or the direction of thought genuinely changes, and let the transition carry useful environmental or contextual information. Do not manufacture breaks for emphasis or rhythm. This applies to narrative, lore, research commentary, production notes, descriptions, and explanatory prose; retain compact labels, lists, code, tables, and machine-readable structures where they are functionally necessary.

Every paragraph break, line break, and scene transition should introduce or deepen non-plot information: geography, architecture, weather, light, color, spatial depth, surfaces, ambient sound, temperature, distance, machinery, atmosphere, or the physical presence of people within a place.

Line breaks are environmental descriptors, not plot beats. Action can occur within a paragraph, but the reader's movement through the prose is carried by the changing world. Environmental description is the medium of the narration, not decoration around it.

## Broadcast delivery

Seth and Nick observe, react, compare, and sometimes notice different details from their position across the scene. Their exchange should feel like a live broadcast reaching the viewer from the other side of the room, gym, courtyard, street, or landscape. They can speak to one another, but they also speak to us: “look at the far wall,” “you can see the difference from here,” or “stay with that angle” are occasional forms of address. Do not make every sentence a direct appeal; the viewer should feel included without being continually summoned. Keep their voices distinguishable without forcing every paragraph into dialogue. Narrator commentary remains separate from character dialogue.

The camera and audience have a receiving position. Establish what can be seen from there and allow distance, occlusion, framing, light, and scale to matter. The narrators may move their vantage between scenes, but they should not drift into the private thoughts of characters or claim knowledge unavailable to the view. Prefer concrete observations and embodied detail. Let meaning arrive through what the setting makes visible, audible, tangible, or difficult to judge.

## Continuity

GEEHUB remembers while it announces. A place keeps its history. Light, weather, architecture, objects, sound, and distance can carry traces of earlier events. Characters remain recognizable through established habits, speech, movement, memory, and relationships.

Preserve canon and chronology. This is a presentation-layer revision, not permission to erase events or invent consequential ones. Do not replace scenes with summaries or turn the manuscript into an inventory of objects.

## Editorial test

Before retaining a paragraph, ask: What does the environment reveal? What can be seen, heard, felt, or measured? What physical trace connects this place to what came before? Does the break deepen the world rather than merely announce the next plot beat?

## Expanded sensory register

A global expansion pass may heighten the prose's sense of connection, warmth, and embodied presence without imitating intoxication or sacrificing legibility. Let attention widen: allow a smell to recall a room, a sound to reveal distance, a surface to carry the warmth of a previous touch, or an ordinary exchange to disclose the emotional structure of a relationship. Build associative movement through concrete correspondences rather than arbitrary leaps. The prose may become more generous, rhythmic, tactile, and emotionally open, but every image must remain attached to the scene, the speaker, or the world.

Do not make every moment ecstatic. Contrast is essential: quiet, fatigue, uncertainty, humour, appetite, embarrassment, and practical work keep heightened passages inhabitable. Do not imply that fictional techniques are evidence about real people. Do not collapse separate genres into one voice: myth may widen into ceremonial cadence; the novel remains scene-led; character writing remains particular and relational; research and production writing gain clarity and context rather than ornamental lyricism; interface copy stays concise and actionable.

For a corpus-wide pass, work in bounded batches. Read the source text and its continuity records first; deepen existing paragraphs and scenes rather than attaching generic afterwords; preserve headings, data, citations, and machine-readable structures; update indexes only when their targets or status change; and record the files actually revised. “Across the corpus” is a continuing editorial programme, not a claim that every file has been edited in one pass.


## Retrospective corpus expansion

This standard applies backward as well as forward. Earlier work is not exempt because it predates the current editorial direction. Treat the existing corpus as an unfinished manuscript and revise it in connected, bounded passes, beginning with foundational chapters and then moving outward through character records, world descriptions, lore, research commentary, production histories, and interface-adjacent prose. Return to each passage, recover its original intention and continuity, and develop the existing material into sustained paragraphs with richer spatial, sensory, social, and historical context. Preserve events, claims, names, chronology, and established relationships; deepen the connective tissue around them rather than appending generic afterwords.

A substantial paragraph should carry a complete unit of attention: the place and its physical conditions, the people occupying it, what they are doing and noticing, and how the moment connects to prior or later events. Avoid both extremes: do not fragment a continuous experience into single-sentence beats, and do not create enormous paragraphs that conceal changes of focus. Dialogue may remain separately formatted when clarity requires it. Lists, headings, tables, metadata, code, citations, and machine-readable structures remain structured when their function requires it; their explanatory prose still receives the fuller treatment.

Earlier work should gain context, not padding. Make existing action more legible by developing the room, the world beyond it, how bodies occupy the space, what objects have been handled, what sounds indicate activity elsewhere, what routines are underway, and what consequences are forming. Where an event is deliberately quiet, preserve that quiet while giving the reader a fuller sense of the world around it. Keep a revision ledger naming the files actually expanded. Never claim the entire corpus has been revised when only a batch has been completed.

## The commanding workshop voice

A recurring Tractus narrative register may use a blunt, charismatic, bodybuilding-coach-like narrator: direct address, hard-earned conviction, physical and workshop vocabulary, irreverent humour, occasional profanity, and emphatic turns that make abstract ideas feel actionable. The voice should sound like a distinct fictional narrator, not an imitation of a named public figure. Let the speaker move between forceful declarations and concrete observation: the coffee cooling on the table, the scrape of a chair, the people who have to carry the work forward. Profanity is punctuation, not a substitute for thought. The narrator's authority comes from discernment, practical standards, and care for the people doing the work—not from humiliation or empty aggression. Preserve the difference between a singular event and a repeatable rule; record what happened before interpreting it. Use this register when the scene calls for it, not as a universal replacement for every voice.


## Narrative ease of production

Production is part of the world's narrative continuity, not an administrative layer placed outside it. Projects, source objects, assets, tests, revisions, renders, exports, and unresolved questions should retain their relationships and histories. A user should be able to move naturally from a scene or idea into the practical work that develops it, and from that work back into the world it changes.

Treat production records as situated chronicles: record what was observed, what was attempted, what changed, what was verified, and what remains unknown. Preserve provenance and source integrity. Never describe an unperformed inspection, render, test, or edit as completed. Technical interfaces remain concise and functional; narrative continuity lives in the connections among artifacts, decisions, people, places, and states.

The governing principle is: **the work should carry its own story of becoming**. A finished artifact remains connected to its source and revision history. A failed attempt can explain the next attempt. A dependency is a relationship that enables the work. A production transition should feel like moving to another room in the same world, not leaving the world to operate a separate machine.
