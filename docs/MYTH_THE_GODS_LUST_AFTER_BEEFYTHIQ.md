# The Gods Lust After Beefythiq
## A Divine Growth Myth

This is a living myth within the GEEHUB fiction archive. It treats Beefythiq as a transformative principle whose expression is shaped by the identity of each host. The gods remain themselves through change; their bodies, domains, relationships, and ambitions acquire new possibilities. The myth follows the continuity rule of Berit: transformation may be radical without erasing the person who undergoes it.

## I. The First Transmission

Before the temples had names, the gods believed they had already discovered the final shapes of power. Zeus understood power as dominion, Neptune as command of the sea, and the other immortals each guarded a domain they considered complete. Then a new principle entered the divine world. It did not arrive as a weapon or a rival claimant. It appeared as a pressure within the old order, a capacity for growth that could use whatever it encountered as the beginning of something further.

Zeus noticed it first. His body had begun to feel insufficient for the force it contained. The change was gradual but unmistakable: his shoulders carried more breadth, his chest acquired depth, and his arms took on a density that made the old proportions of the king of Olympus seem like an earlier draft. The transformation did not replace his familiar face, temperament, or authority. It made those things more physically present. He remained Zeus, but the body that expressed Zeus was being revised from within.

He called the phenomenon Beefythiq. The name pleased him because it sounded less like a sickness than a force with an appetite for possibility. He descended from Olympus to find Neptune, not to announce a conquest but because his brother was the one god whose response he genuinely wanted to witness. Neptune received him in the ocean palace, where the currents moved slowly through enormous columns and the light from the surface arrived in broken blue bands.

Neptune recognized the change immediately. Zeus's frame had become heavier and more imposing, but the familiar impatience in his eyes remained. “What storm has touched you?” Neptune asked. Zeus answered that it was not a storm, but a beginning. He offered his forearm, where a faint golden pulse moved beneath the skin. Neptune took hold, and the first transmission passed between them.

The ocean answered before Neptune spoke. Currents reversed in the outer halls. The pressure of the deep gathered around his body, and his own transformation began to take shape. His shoulders broadened, his torso thickened, and his arms gained a new, compact power. Where Zeus's growth carried the character of thunder—sudden, radiant, commanding—Neptune's developed like the sea: immense pressure accumulating into quiet force. His domain supplied the language of his change.

The brothers studied one another. Zeus was delighted by the result; Neptune was more cautious, but neither mistook the event for an ordinary contest. Their transformations were connected. When one moved, the other felt a faint answering pulse. Their powers had not merged, and neither had become subordinate. Beefythiq had established a relationship between them without dissolving their differences.

They tested the connection. Zeus called lightning down through the palace's open crown while Neptune raised a column of water to meet it. Gold travelled through the blue depths, illuminating the architecture from within. The palace trembled, then settled. The two gods laughed—not at a defeated opponent, but at the discovery that something had happened to them which neither could yet explain. The first transmission had succeeded. Beefythiq had found a way to grow through relationship.

## II. The Gathering of the Immortals

The news travelled without a messenger. Olympus felt warmer; the sea became restless; old statues seemed suddenly to belong to an earlier conception of divinity. Zeus and Neptune began meeting often, comparing the different ways Beefythiq expressed itself. Zeus's shoulders and chest continued to develop, giving his movements the weight of an approaching storm. Neptune found that the pressure of the deep intensified his own growth. Neither could predict the final form, and this uncertainty became part of the attraction.

Hercules arrived expecting another test from his father. He found Zeus and Neptune in the courtyard, their altered frames making the columns appear narrower than before. He asked what had happened. Neptune replied that Zeus had been sharing things. Zeus corrected him with a look, but did not deny it. Hercules had spent his life proving himself through labours, and he recognized at once that this was not simply another contest of strength. The two gods seemed to have entered a process that made the old measure of achievement inadequate.

Zeus offered him the golden pulse. Hercules accepted. Beefythiq met a body already accustomed to extraordinary exertion, and the response was immediate. His shoulders broadened, his chest gained depth, and his arms grew heavier. Yet the change did not turn him into a copy of Zeus or Neptune. Hercules retained the directness and stubborn courage that had carried him through the labours. His growth expressed endurance under pressure: the body of someone who had repeatedly met the impossible and discovered another effort beyond it.

He laughed when he understood that the process was still underway. Zeus asked whether he understood what had happened. Hercules answered that he did not, but intended to. Neptune approved of the answer. The three stood together beneath the columns, speaking long after the attendants had withdrawn. They considered whether Beefythiq possessed an intelligence, whether it selected its hosts, and whether the transmission could be directed. None of them could yet distinguish intention from adaptation.

Athena had been watching from the upper gallery. She was not impressed merely by the increase in physical strength. She was interested in the continuity of identity across transformation. Three distinct divine bodies had changed, but none had lost its particular character. The phenomenon was not producing identical forms. It was finding the latent grammar of each host and developing it beyond its former limits.

She descended and asked to examine the pulse. Zeus joked that she could inspect the phenomenon but not take measurements of his chest. Athena ignored him. She studied the faint golden undertone moving beneath Hercules's skin and noticed that it responded to the presence of the other transformed gods. “This is not simply an infection,” she said. “It is an adaptive principle. It takes the nature of its host and develops it beyond its previous limitations.”

Zeus asked whether she could stop it. Athena looked at the three of them. “That is not the interesting question. The interesting question is what happens when it reaches someone who does not want to stop.” For the first time, Zeus had no immediate answer.

Far away, Ares had begun hearing the rumours. He cared little for Athena's theories or Hercules's enthusiasm. He wanted to know whether Beefythiq could make him stronger than his father. He did not intend to wait for an invitation.

## III. Ares Comes to Olympus

Ares arrived at dusk, when the light across Olympus had turned the marble rose-gold and the clouds below the mountain were gathering into a dark belt. He came armed, not because he expected a battle, but because he wanted the others to understand that he had not come as a petitioner. His armour fitted differently from the last time he had worn it; he had spent the journey imagining what Beefythiq might do to him, and each imagined possibility had sharpened his impatience.

He found Zeus, Neptune, Hercules, and Athena in the courtyard. His eyes moved over them, pausing at the breadth of Zeus's shoulders, the massive calm of Neptune, and the changed proportions of Hercules. He did not conceal his interest. “So it is true,” he said. “You have found another way to make yourselves feared.”

Zeus answered that fear had nothing to do with it. Ares laughed. To him, every power was eventually tested by conflict. He demanded the transmission, but Zeus refused to hand it over as though it were a weapon from the armoury. Beefythiq was not a prize that could be claimed by the loudest god. Ares stepped closer. The air tightened around him, and the old instinct of Olympus—challenge answered by challenge—began to gather in the courtyard.

Athena intervened before Zeus could respond. She told Ares that the phenomenon would not simply obey his ambition. It would take the whole of him: his impatience, his appetite for confrontation, his courage, and the part of him that had never learned to distinguish recognition from victory. Ares said that those qualities were precisely what he wanted strengthened.

Neptune watched him for a long moment. “Then understand that growth will not make you less yourself.” Ares turned toward him. “I have no intention of becoming less.” The answer was honest, and that honesty mattered. Zeus extended his hand, but this time the transmission was not a gift passed between brothers. Ares had to choose to accept the uncertainty that came with it.

When he clasped Zeus's forearm, the pulse moved through him like a drumbeat. His body responded with a different rhythm from the others. His shoulders thickened, his chest expanded, and his arms acquired a dense, forceful mass. The transformation did not make him serene. It intensified his presence. Even standing still, he seemed ready to move; the floor beneath his feet registered each shift of weight. His armour began to strain at the seams, and he removed it piece by piece, unwilling to let metal dictate the shape of his new body.

Ares looked toward Hercules. For an instant the old rivalry returned, but it no longer had the same meaning. Hercules did not lower his eyes or prepare to defend his place. He looked at Ares with frank curiosity, as though the next stage of the story had just become more interesting. Ares felt the difference and did not know what to do with it.

“Try me,” he said.

Hercules smiled. “Tomorrow.”

The answer unsettled Ares more than a challenge would have. Tomorrow meant that the transformation was not a single spectacle to be settled in one contest. It meant there would be time to learn what his new strength could do, and what it might require of him. For the first time, Ares considered that Beefythiq might create a future rather than merely furnish a victory.

That night the four transformed gods remained in the courtyard. Athena recorded the changes; Neptune listened to the currents beneath the mountain; Zeus watched the storm-front gathering over the sea. Hercules and Ares spoke in low voices about the coming test. The golden pulse passed faintly between them, not as a command but as a sign that the new connection had been established.

## IV. The Measure of a God

The contest took place the following morning on the broad training ground above the clouds. The gods assembled without ceremony. No mortal audience had been summoned, and no herald announced the competitors. Athena wanted to observe how Beefythiq responded to exertion, while Zeus wanted to see whether the new strength could be used without turning every encounter into a struggle for supremacy.

Hercules and Ares began with familiar movements. They circled, tested distance, and exchanged measured blows. Their bodies carried new weight, and the ground answered each impact. But the most striking change was not the force of the strikes. It was the way each man adapted. Hercules learned to distribute his strength through his stance; Ares discovered that his first impulse—to overwhelm—could be made more effective by waiting half a beat and reading his opponent.

The contest grew more demanding. Sweat darkened their clothing; their breathing deepened; the muscles of their backs and shoulders worked visibly under the strain. Beefythiq responded to exertion, but it did not simply reward the harder blow. It seemed to register attention, recovery, and adjustment as well. Every correction became part of the body's new understanding.

At the edge of the ground, Zeus watched with his arms folded. Neptune stood beside him, the sea wind moving through his hair. “You thought it would make us stronger,” Neptune said. Zeus replied that he had expected no less. “But it is making us more capable of learning.” Athena, listening nearby, recorded the distinction. Strength was an immediate effect; adaptation was the deeper event.

Eventually Hercules stepped back and offered Ares his hand. Ares hesitated. The old rules told him that accepting help would make the contest incomplete. The new experience told him that the contest had already changed them both. He took Hercules's hand, and the golden pulse travelled between them. Neither became the other's copy. Each retained his own character, but the connection gave them access to a shared momentum.

Athena understood then that Beefythiq did not spread like an ordinary contagion. It established a living network of transformations. Every host became a distinct expression of the principle and a possible point of transmission. The network could accumulate differences without losing its common source. It could grow through rivalry, but it did not require rivalry. It could grow through affection, instruction, imitation, challenge, or the simple recognition that another being's development opened a new possibility.

She named the emerging pattern the Brotherhood Cycle. Zeus accepted the name because it sounded suitably grand. Neptune preferred to call it the tide. Hercules said that names could wait until they knew what it did next. Ares, still breathing hard from the contest, looked toward the distant halls of Olympus.

“Who else knows?”

Athena turned toward the mountain. Beyond its familiar temples lay the older houses of the gods, the forgotten shrines, the caves where the Titans had once been confined, and the territories of powers who had never cared for Olympus. The transmission had reached only a handful of immortals. The divine world was much larger than their courtyard.

“Enough to begin,” she said.

And beneath Olympus, far below the roots of the mountain, something ancient stirred in answer.

## Canon Notes

- **Beefythiq** is a transformative principle, not merely a disease or a strength-enhancing substance. The word “infection” remains part of the gods' initial vocabulary because they do not yet understand what has entered their world.
- **Continuity:** Zeus remains Zeus; Neptune remains Neptune; Hercules remains Hercules; Ares remains Ares. Their transformations amplify and develop their existing identities rather than replacing them.
- **Individual expression:** Zeus manifests storm-like expansion and authority; Neptune manifests deep pressure and tidal mass; Hercules manifests endurance and adaptive exertion; Ares manifests force, momentum, and the discipline he has not yet learned to value.
- **Transmission:** Contact establishes the first bond, but acceptance and adaptation determine what follows. The principle spreads through relationships and encounters.
- **The Brotherhood Cycle:** A network of distinct beings whose growth becomes mutually generative. Rivalry may initiate contact, but shared development changes the meaning of strength.
- **Unresolved:** Athena has not determined whether Beefythiq has an originating intelligence. Something beneath Olympus has now responded to its presence.
