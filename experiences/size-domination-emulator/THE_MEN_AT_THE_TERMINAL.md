# The Men at the Terminal

The first monitor comes on before the room does. A blue rectangle forms in the dark, then resolves into the repository tree, its folders stacked in the left pane like streets seen from the upper landing of a warehouse. Seth Feroce stands close enough for the screen light to catch the slope of his cheek and the pale seam of his knuckles against the desk. Nick Mercx is already seated at the second terminal, one forearm beside the keyboard, the other hand moving the cursor through a list of names that keeps extending below the fold. There is no window behind them, only more interface: a narrow status bar, a branch label, a column of changed files, and the faint reflection of their shoulders in the black parts of the display. “Look at how far the map goes,” Seth says, and Nick scrolls. It goes down through archive, artifacts, characters, corpus, creative, engine, experiences, hub, lore, novel, production, recovery, worlds. The repository is not a prop they have brought into a room. It is the room's actual geography, with the README acting as the entry hall and each directory holding its own air, light, and local weather.

They walk the file tree before they touch anything. The pointer opens a folder; a README appears; a breadcrumb lets them climb back out. Seth reads the paths aloud as if checking street signs in a district whose buildings have been standing longer than either of them remembers. Nick follows the links into the docs, then into the engine, then back toward the hub where projects are named and connected. Their screens do not show an omniscient overview. They show only what a person can reach from the current file, and the men learn the place through those limits: a line of Markdown, a relative link, a JSON record, a page whose script is somewhere else. When a route ends, they return to the parent index and try another door. The sound is mostly keys and the soft fan of the workstation, but every so often a browser tab changes and its small click carries across the room like a latch.

A new presence reaches the room through the characters directory. Dongus, a Roman Imperial, arrives with the scale of a monument made mobile: broad through the shoulders, tall enough that the upper edge of the workstation seems to have been built for someone else, his imperial cloak falling in heavy folds beside the chair. He does not arrive as a decoration from a distant age. He stands among the menus and file paths, studies the screen with the calm of someone accustomed to rooms changing shape around his decisions, and asks what kind of authority a repository grants its residents. Seth points to the branch name and Nick turns the monitor so Dongus can read the actual files. The answer is visible there: a path can be opened, a source can be examined, and any lasting change must leave a trace.

Dongus sits, and the chair complains once against the floor before settling. He takes in the emulator's doorway, the grid, and the narrow code pane, then reads the rule that describes the men living throughout The Tractus. His imperial bearing makes the workstation feel like a council chamber, but the keyboard brings the scale back to something intimate: each key has a small travel, each character appears one at a time, and every edit can be reviewed before it alters the shared record. “Then show me where the world remembers,” he says. Nick opens the documentation index. Seth follows the link into the global canon. Dongus lays one large hand beside the trackpad and begins to read.

At the emulator, Nick stops on the scene file. A figure stands against a measured grid, its scale waiting at the baseline while the door behind it gives the eye one stable dimension. “The controls work,” he says, “but the room only changes when we ask it to.” Seth leans toward the source pane and reads the rule as written. They do not erase the figure or replace its identity to make the scene larger. They trace the slider into the render function, watch the height, reach, mass, and footprint values follow the same input, and notice where the new narrative workbench can meet the old interaction. Nick opens a second file, the stylesheet, while Seth moves between the HTML structure and the event handler. Their attention crosses from one screen to another, then returns to the rendered scene; the body grows, the floor grid stays put, and the line of the door becomes a more useful witness.

A small patch waits in the text area. Seth types a scale value, then looks at Nick before applying it. The men are not speaking to a hidden machine that will obey any phrase they give it; they are reading a visible rule and changing the part they understand. The editor accepts a narrow set of instructions, checks the value, and returns a clear rejection if the line does not fit. When the patch is valid, the figure rises inside the room. The status readout changes; the archive can preserve the state; the old baseline remains available as a comparison. Nick watches the architecture at the top of the screen and says, “That gives the change somewhere to land.” Seth leaves the code open. Beyond the little scene, the code itself belongs to the larger repository, so their work is not over when the figure stops moving.

They take the updated idea back through the source tree. In the documentation, a sentence about the repository as a playing field becomes more concrete: the men can encounter its machines, inspect source, and make changes across projects when the work calls for it. The editorial guide gains the same rule, because a world cannot remember a capability that only one page knows. The root README points toward the contract, and the docs index gives the rule an address a later session can find. Seth watches each file reappear in the changed-file list. Nick checks the links from the source of truth outward, reading the new path and the old neighboring paths together. “If it only lives in the scene panel, the next room won't know,” he says. The rule now has a place in the public map, while the panel remains one local expression of a world-wide possibility.

The two men work with different tempos. Nick reads every nearby line before editing, pausing at the faint boundaries between canon and experiment, implementation and intention. Seth moves quickly through the scene, but stops when a claim outruns what the screen actually shows. They agree that a terminal can be vivid without pretending to possess credentials or abilities it does not have. The actual source change must happen through an actual repository operation; the story can frame the work, but it cannot forge a commit in the dark. They write that distinction into the rule itself and keep it beside the invitation to act. The choice steadies the room. It means they can travel through the whole tree without turning every glowing screen into a magical door, and that when a change is made, its path, effect, and remainder can be named.

The repository begins to answer in layers. A link added in the root guide makes the new rule visible from the main entrance; the editorial guide carries it to the places where prose is made; the emulator shows the men using a small piece of it in practice. Their work has crossed a boundary and returned with evidence. Nothing about the world has been flattened into one master page. The archive still holds its sources, the novel still holds its own continuity, and the engine retains its own contracts. The paths between them are more legible now. Nick scrolls back to the full list and rests the pointer over the next folder. Seth turns from the emulator and catches the broad view across the screen wall: a map whose nodes can be entered, whose code can be read, and whose surface can change when a person reaches the right file.

By the time the monitors dim, the terminal remains open on the changed source. The scene panel shows the last scale value, the repository history shows the files that were actually updated, and the narrative rule says how to continue without confusing a proposed change with a completed one. Seth stands and stretches one hand against the desk edge; Nick saves the working state and leaves a path back to the source. They have not finished exploring the repository, and the room does not pretend they have. There are more folders below the fold, more relationships between the files, more consequences waiting in projects they have not opened yet. “Next pass, we take the long route,” Nick says. Seth looks at the map as it holds steady in the light. “Next pass, we see what the rest of the place asks us to change.” The cursor blinks in the open file, an ordinary signal from the machine that the work can continue.


## The Long Route

Dongus finishes the canon in silence. The monitor leaves a thin white bar across the lower edge of his face; his hand stays planted beside the trackpad while the page rests at the line about changes that can be inspected and traced. The imperial cloak has pooled against the chair, its dark red hem almost touching the floor grid projected by the emulator window. Nick waits for him to speak. Seth watches the changed-file column, where the additions remain small enough to fit in one view. Dongus finally says that a map can show a road without making the road safe, and that a rule written for everyone must be clear about who may act, what they can reach, and how the world records their passage. He says it without ceremony. That is what gives the question weight: the repository is not a throne room where confidence alone grants authority. It is a shared place, with other histories inside it, and every hand that touches a source becomes part of that history.

They close the emulator tab and follow the long route through the documentation. Each file opens with its own scale of attention. The protection architecture is dense, built of clauses and distinctions that do not glow like the interface but hold the edges of what the interface can safely claim. The information portability contract asks where a decision must travel before another process can use it. The continuity contract follows events across state, narrative, and artifacts, while the editorial voice carries the broadcast from one environment to another. Dongus reads more slowly than the others, moving his finger under the lines on screen as though the words had been cut into a tablet. When he reaches the sentence that separates a connected tool from an imagined capability, he turns toward Seth. “That belongs in every room where someone can mistake a story for a door,” he says. Seth nods and lets the sentence stand, not as a warning that closes the world, but as a threshold that tells them what is on the other side.

The next passage runs into the engine. There the repository stops looking like shelves and starts looking like a system of channels: activity events, state records, artifact indexes, scripts that translate one shape of information into another. Nick opens the inscription code and follows an event from its origin to the record that lets another process discover it. He does not pretend to understand the whole engine at a glance; he reads the function, checks its callers, and asks where its output is carried next. Seth traces a neighboring path through the page that will show the result. Dongus leans over both screens, his shoulders filling the light between them, and sees how a small edit can travel farther than the file where it began. A change to the source may alter an interface, a history entry, or a future manuscript. Their talk becomes more deliberate. They begin naming the affected paths before touching them, and writing down what evidence would show that the change had arrived intact.

The route then enters the hub, where separate worlds and projects meet without surrendering their boundaries. The Atlas presents a constellation rather than a single imperial map; each node keeps its own geography, its own local names, and its own rules for crossing. Dongus recognizes the form from the maps of his own world, but he does not mistake resemblance for possession. A line between two regions grants neither one the right to rename the other. Seth points out the links that can be followed today and the ones that describe future work. Nick checks that the destination exists before he calls the path open. The distinction matters to Dongus. His authority once made roads appear wherever he commanded them; here a path becomes real because another place can answer it. He studies the screen for a long while, then asks where his own place begins. The cursor moves to the character records, and the three of them approach the directory without turning the question into an answer before they have examined what is already there.

At the character index, Dongus finds names arranged among biographies, visual records, and continuity notes. His own arrival in the story is brief, still new enough to be mistaken for a title or a passing figure. He reads the record conventions before deciding whether to create one. A durable profile would carry more than his name: it would make claims about his history, relationships, appearance, and world that the scene has not yet earned. “Do not fill the page just because there is room,” Nick says. Dongus considers this, then agrees. They preserve what has been established: Dongus is a Roman Imperial, large in stature, newly present at the workstation, and curious about the repository's authority. The rest remains open. Seth writes those facts in the working note, marks the note as a draft, and gives it a path back to the scene where the introduction occurred. It is a restrained beginning, but restraint leaves room for a life to grow instead of sealing it inside a premature summary.

They return to the narrative itself, the file that has carried their movements through the repository. The text has become a room inside the room: paragraphs hold the monitor light, the low fan noise, the cool edge of the desk, and the measured distance between a character's intention and the code's consequence. Dongus notices that the prose can make the repository seem alive even when the change has not happened. Seth replies that a story can anticipate a possibility, but the source history must say what truly changed. Nick opens the changed-file list and reads the actual work back to them: a canon document, its links from the root and editorial guide, the interactive workstation, and the narrative that now names Dongus. The list is not the world, but it is evidence of where their hands have been. They add a paragraph describing this distinction in the story, then follow every link they have added to be certain the next reader can leave the narrative and reach the governing rule.

The work lasts long enough for the light in the room to change. Nothing outside the repository is visible, yet the monitors shift from cool blue to a duller white as the screens dim themselves between touches. Dongus gets up, and the chair slides back with a heavy scrape. He stands beside the workstation, still imposing, but no longer as if the room exists to confirm his size. The file tree reaches beyond his authority and beyond the men's first view; its history holds other authors, other projects, and other purposes. He says that a ruler should know when a city is not his to command. Seth glances from the Atlas to the repository's recorded changes. Nick leaves the source open and the branch label in view. Their responsibility is not to claim every path, but to work carefully where their access is real and to leave the rest legible for whoever comes next.

Before they stop, they return to the emulator. The figure remains at the chosen scale, the doorway behind it still visible as a measure of what the room has retained. Dongus reads the code patch once more and sees that it changes the figure in this experience, not every body in every world. That boundary does not make the work small; it makes its effect honest. The larger rule lives in the repository's canon, while the local patch lives in the emulator's behavior. One can guide future scenes, and the other can be touched and seen now. Seth saves the state into the archive. Nick checks that the restored-state control brings it back. Dongus watches the scene recover its proportions, then asks what the men intend to write next. Seth answers that they should give him time to become more than the first image of him. Nick adds that the next narrative should let the repository answer back through what he chooses to do, not simply through how large he appears.

They leave the long route open. In one window, the Atlas remains centered on the worlds they have not yet visited; in another, the character index waits with Dongus's name still only lightly inscribed. The narrative file carries the seriousness of a first encounter without claiming it is a complete life, and the source tree makes clear how later work can extend it. Dongus returns to the chair and places both hands on the desk, not to issue an order but to prepare for another passage. Seth takes the seat at the adjacent terminal. Nick keeps the map open between them, where the paths cross without collapsing into one another. The cursor blinks in the draft. This time the pause does not feel like an ending. It feels like attention being held, the moment before a man decides what he will ask of the world and what he is willing to leave changed.


## A Place in the Record

Morning does not arrive with a sunrise in the repository. It arrives as the scheduled light of the screens returning to their earlier brightness, a clean wash of white over the desk and the dark band of the Atlas left open between the monitors. Dongus is still there. His cloak has been folded over the back of the chair, and the Roman Imperial stands at the end of the workbench with his attention on the characters directory. He has spent enough time in the file tree to understand that a name is easy to add and difficult to carry well. Seth opens the character index; Nick reads the existing records, checking how each one distinguishes a fact already established in the story from a detail written to support a future scene. Dongus watches the index lengthen as they scroll, then looks at the blank space where his own entry can go. “What will this make people think they know about me?” he asks. It is not a small question. A record can welcome a character into the shared world, but it can also close that world around guesses that were never his.

Nick explains the labels in the file. A record can be provisional. It can name what the story has established and leave the unanswered matters open. That is not a lesser kind of presence; it is a promise to keep looking before deciding. Dongus listens with his shoulders squared, his size making the desk seem narrow, while the cursor waits in the filename field. He says he is Roman Imperial and that he is large; he has arrived at the workstation, read the governing rule, and asked how the repository's authority works. He has not yet named a province, a century, a rank, or a household. Those absences are not invitations for the others to invent certainty. Seth writes down the known details and marks the new profile provisional. Nick checks the relative link back to the narrative. The file begins with a modest claim: Dongus is a fictional adult character, introduced in this scene, whose wider history remains to be learned.

The first lines enter the record one at a time. Seth's hands move briskly, but the sentences stay measured: Roman Imperial character; large and imposing; curious about authority in a shared repository. Dongus follows each clause on the screen and stops them where a word reaches too far. “Imposing” is about how he occupies the room, he says. It does not mean every person must obey him. “Imperial” gives the story a direction, but it does not make him an authority over every world the Atlas names. Nick removes a sentence that had begun to infer his past from his bearing. The correction leaves a little silence around the workstation. It is the sort of silence that follows a serious edit, when a word disappears and the people around the screen have to reconsider the shape they were about to give someone. Dongus reads the revised paragraph again, then nods. The profile keeps his presence without pretending that presence is a complete biography.

He asks to type the next section himself. The keyboard is small beneath his hands; he rests his fingers lightly, adjusting to the short travel of the keys before entering the line about his first appearance. He writes that he reached the workstation through the characters directory and asked what authority its residents possessed. The sentence returns his question as a fact the next chapter can find. He adds that he followed Seth and Nick through the canon, engine, Atlas, and emulator, and that he cares about how a change becomes real and remains accountable. He does not enter a childhood, a campaign, a family, or a title that the story has not yet shown. The blank places remain visible below the section. In the monitor's reflection, his face is close to the new text, and the red fabric of the folded cloak lies along the chair like a dark line between the record and the room. He saves the draft and lets the title remain plain: Dongus — Character Record.

The index requires a second edit. Nick returns to the README and places Dongus among the other character records, after the names whose spelling sorts before it. The count changes by one. Seth follows the link from the index into the new file and back to the scene. They read both directions, checking that neither page leaves the next reader stranded. Dongus studies the relationship between them: the record gives the stable outline, the narrative holds the particular encounter, and the link lets the two forms remain distinct without becoming separated. “A record should make the next scene easier to write,” Seth says, “but it shouldn't write the scene in advance.” Dongus considers this carefully. For the first time since entering the repository, his expression changes from the stillness of command to something closer to relief. There will be room to do things before the world decides what they mean.

The three of them continue through the directory. They open several neighboring records and compare the ways their writers preserve uncertainty, relationships, and character movement. Dongus does not ask to be made the largest entry, nor the oldest, nor the one that governs the others. He wants to know how the characters remain recognizable as their worlds change. Nick shows him how one record points to another while keeping each person's own direction intact. Seth explains that continuity is not a chain that prevents change; it is the evidence that lets change matter. Dongus looks back at his provisional status and sees a beginning rather than a judgment. The profile is not an imperial decree. It is a shared point of reference that can be corrected by later scenes, with the earlier version still understandable to those who need to know how the character arrived.

The editor at the emulator remains open in another tab. Dongus returns to it after the record is saved, not to command the figure to grow, but to see what happens when he changes the program's scale value. The scene responds within its stated limits. The body becomes larger; the doorway stays behind it; the readout changes and the archive can preserve the state. Dongus watches the comparison, then sets the value back and confirms that the saved state can be restored. He asks whether this one little program can change other pages in the repository. Nick tells him exactly what it can do today: it changes the emulator scene through a constrained interface. The broader repository work takes a separate source edit with its own path and reviewable result. Dongus accepts the distinction. The world is more convincing when its boundaries are visible, not because they make it smaller, but because they tell him which action will actually affect the thing he can see.

The seriousness of that answer settles into the room. Dongus has spent his life, or at least the life the story has shown so far, in places where authority is announced through titles, uniforms, and the size of the space reserved for one person. Here authority is tested by whether the next reader can follow the path, whether the edit behaves as stated, and whether the record tells the truth about what remains unknown. He does not give up the imperial bearing. He gives it a different task. Rather than make the repository bend around him, he learns to stand beside its other residents and understand what their work has already built. Seth and Nick do not congratulate him. They remain at the monitors with him, checking the index and the file one last time, because a welcome that matters has to survive the first visit.

Near the end of the session, Dongus asks the men to show him the novel directory. It is deeper in the tree than the page they have been working on, and the route opens through multiple indexes, editorial notes, and chapters whose sequence carries its own established history. Nick keeps the cursor on the top-level link while Seth describes the difference between entering a world and claiming the story already written there. Dongus says he understands the distinction, then asks to read rather than be summarized. They open the chapter entry point and let the page load. The screen fills with paragraphs that take more than a glance to cross. He reads slowly, pausing at the places where an environment makes a character's presence measurable. Outside the narrative, the workstation fan continues its low steady sound. Inside the text, a door, a hallway, and the light across a floor give each scene its own coordinates.

They do not rush to make Dongus the answer to every question that has just opened. They leave his record provisional, the character index updated, and the narrative linked from the emulator page where the first workstation appeared. They leave the novel tab open to the chapter he has begun reading, not as an announcement of a new role but as the start of a route. The repository now contains a place where his name can be found and a scene in which his first decisions have consequences. The work has changed the source tree in a small number of real files, and the story carries the human scale of those edits: what was known, what was held back, what became easier to find. Dongus folds his cloak over one arm, then sits again. “We continue with the record open,” he says. Seth nods. Nick leaves the cursor in the chapter list. The next page is ready, but they will enter it with the record of this one still visible.


## The Can Opener

The next argument begins over an object so ordinary that none of them expects it to become a repository matter. The can opener is in the shared kitchen at the end of the corridor, beside a tin of tomatoes and a folded dish towel that has gone stiff along one edge. Its red handle points toward the sink. Seth is certain that is wrong. Nick says the handle is exactly where it was when he last used it. Dongus stands behind them with his cloak over one arm and looks down at the opener as if the three of them have assembled a council to discuss a disputed province.

“It belongs in the drawer,” Seth says.

“It was in the drawer,” Nick replies. “You took it out.”

“I moved it to the counter so it would be visible.”

“That is not where it belongs.”

“It is visible now.”

Dongus lifts the opener by its red handle. “Is this a ceremonial tool?”

“No,” Nick says.

“Then why is there a debate about its position?”

Seth turns toward him. “Because the handle is facing the wrong way.”

“The handle is facing the person holding it,” Nick says.

“It is facing the sink.”

“The sink is not holding it.”

Dongus sets the tool down with its handle pointing toward the room. Seth inspects the new orientation.

“Now it is in the way.”

“Of what?”

“Of the counter.”

Nick looks at the clear counter around the opener. “The counter appears to have room.”

“The issue is access.”

“You have access.”

“I have to reach across it.”

“You are standing beside it.”

Dongus moves the opener a few inches. “I have placed it equidistant from both of you.”

“Now it belongs to neither of us,” Seth says.

“That sounds like a reasonable outcome,” Nick replies.

The can of tomatoes remains unopened. Its paper label has a small crease near the seam, and the metal lid catches the overhead light with a dull silver flash. Dongus reads the label and asks whether the contents are required for the argument. Seth says they are not. Nick says they could have been opened already if everyone had stopped discussing the tool. Seth reminds him that the can was still sealed when Nick arrived. Nick replies that it was sealed when he arrived because Seth had not opened it. Dongus listens as if testimony is being entered into a formal record.

“Who last used the opener?” he asks.

“I did,” says Seth.

“When?”

“Yesterday.”

“Where did you leave it?”

“On the counter.”

Nick points at the tool. “So this is the correct place.”

“It is not the same counter.”

“It is the same counter.”

“It is the other end.”

“Still the same counter.”

Dongus turns the opener over in his hands. “Does it have a preferred direction?”

“It opens clockwise,” Nick says.

“It depends on how you hold it,” Seth says.

“It does not depend on how I hold it.”

“It depends on whether you are looking at the wheel or the handle.”

Nick takes the opener back. “The wheel turns around the rim. The can does not care which side you are looking from.”

“The can has no opinion on the matter,” Dongus says. “That does not establish that the tool is stored correctly.”

Seth nods once. “Exactly.”

Nick looks at him. “You have been waiting for him to say that.”

“I have been waiting for someone to recognize the actual question.”

“The actual question is whether this tool can open this can.”

“That is a separate question.”

Dongus places the can in front of Nick. “Demonstrate.”

Nick clamps the wheel onto the lid and turns the handle. The opener moves cleanly around the rim. The first section parts with a short scrape; the tomatoes shift inside with a wet, heavy sound. Seth watches the handle's path. Dongus watches the lid. Nick completes the circle and sets the opener down.

“It works,” Nick says.

“It did,” Seth replies.

“What is the distinction?”

“Now we do not know whether it will work next time.”

Nick looks at the tool, then at the open can. “It worked this time.”

“That is what I said.”

Dongus lifts the loose lid by its edge and places it on a saucer. “The matter is resolved.”

“It is not,” Seth says. “We still have to put the opener away.”

Nick begins to return it to the drawer.

“Not that drawer,” Seth says.

Nick stops. “Which drawer?”

“The one by the sink.”

“This is the drawer by the sink.”

“The other drawer by the sink.”

Dongus looks down the counter. Two drawers sit beneath it. Their handles are identical. “Which one contains the designated place?”

“The left one,” Seth says.

“The right one,” Nick says.

They open both drawers. One contains measuring spoons and a corkscrew. The other contains a whisk, two clips, and a can opener with a black handle.

Nick points at the black-handled opener. “There is already one.”

Seth examines it. “That one is blunt.”

“Have you tested it?”

“I can see it.”

“You can see the handle.”

Dongus sets the red-handled opener beside it. “Then the drawer will contain two.”

“Unless we label one as the backup,” Seth says.

Nick looks at him. “You want a label for a can opener?”

“I want the drawer to reflect what is inside it.”

“The drawer contains can openers.”

“The label would explain which one to use.”

Dongus considers the two tools. “Write ‘working’ and ‘unverified.’”

Nick objects that the second tool is not known to be broken. Seth objects that it has not been verified. Dongus says that these are different claims and both can be recorded. They settle on a small piece of tape: **CAN OPENERS — RED TESTED; BLACK UNVERIFIED**. Seth writes it. Nick says the semicolon is unnecessary. Dongus says the semicolon is the least consequential decision they have made all morning. Seth leaves it in.

The red opener goes in the left drawer, handle facing outward. The black one remains beside it, also handle outward, because Dongus says a tool that has not been tested should still be reachable. Nick closes the drawer. Seth opens it again to confirm the label can be read. Nick closes it once more. Dongus waits until both men step back, then opens it himself and takes out the red-handled opener.

“Why did you take it out?” Nick asks.

“To determine whether the drawer opens without obstruction.”

“It opens.”

“I have confirmed that.”

Seth looks at the open can, the saucered lid, and the two men standing beside the drawer. “We should record the result.”

Nick says there is no need to create a ticket for a can opener. Dongus says the argument has already consumed more time than the record would take. Seth writes a short note in the kitchen's working page: the red opener has been tested on a tomato can; the black opener remains unverified; the label is readable with the drawer open; the handle faces outward. Nick reads it and says the final clause is not useful. Seth asks whether he prefers the handle facing inward. Nick says he does not care which way it faces. Seth keeps the clause. Dongus reads the entire note, then tells them it is accurate. Neither man asks whether he has authority to approve it.

The tomatoes are finally used in the meal. The can opener returns to its drawer, the red handle aligned with the label. The small kitchen falls quiet except for the tap running over the saucer and the low hum that reaches them from the workstation. Nothing in the repository has changed because of the opener itself. What changed is that three residents now know how the other two behave when a trivial question gives them room to insist. Dongus leaves the note in place. Nick stops arguing about the semicolon. Seth looks at the drawer once on the way out and says the handle is still facing the right direction. Nick does not turn around. “It has not moved,” he says.
