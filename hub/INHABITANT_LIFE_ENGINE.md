# The Tractus — Inhabitant Life Engine

The Tractus is populated by people who continue living when the camera, narrator, visitor, or central character leaves. An inhabitant is not a decorative presence, a reaction generator, or a task waiting to be assigned. Each has a life with its own momentum. The purpose of this engine is to make that momentum visible in scenes, records, maps, schedules, and the living interface.

## The minimum life pattern

Every recurring inhabitant should gradually acquire:

- **A personal direction:** something he wants to learn, make, repair, discover, protect, experience, or change. It need not be grand.
- **A recurring practice:** work, training, study, craft, maintenance, sport, cooking, music, volunteering, collecting, or another activity that gives shape to ordinary time.
- **A current concern:** a problem, decision, responsibility, curiosity, or unfinished matter that can develop between appearances.
- **A social field:** people he seeks out, people he avoids, people he works beside, and relationships that change through reciprocal action.
- **A private interval:** rest, solitude, entertainment, wandering, boredom, or an activity that does not need to become a scene for someone else.
- **A next action:** a concrete thing he may do after the present scene, including a choice to wait, decline, or change direction.

These are fields to discover through continuity, not boxes to fill with invented biography. Record what is established, what is provisional, and what remains open.

## Time does not stop off-screen

When a scene ends, the world does not freeze. Other inhabitants continue with their work and plans. A repair can be completed, a practice missed, a friendship strained, a meal prepared, a route tested, a new idea abandoned, or a decision postponed without the central character witnessing it. When the story returns, show the consequence rather than pretending the intervening time did not pass.

Use overlapping activity. Several people may be doing different things at the same time, in different places, for reasons that do not all lead back to the protagonist. Their paths can cross naturally through shared work, public places, meals, local events, friendship, rivalry, or coincidence.

## Activity is more than a quest

Useful activity includes practical work, skilled craft, sport, study, civic participation, creative work, maintenance, teaching, learning, care, recreation, travel, conversation, celebration, and rest. Do not make every activity a dramatic mission. A man can spend an afternoon repairing a hinge because the hinge needs repairing. The scene becomes meaningful through his judgement, company, small frustrations, competence, humour, and the result that remains afterward.

A good activity has an owner, a reason, a place, a duration or rhythm, and some consequence. It may produce an artifact, a changed relationship, a new capability, a public improvement, a memory, or simply a satisfying afternoon. It should not exist solely to make another character look impressive.

## The open invitation rule

Shared schedules and noticeboards are invitations, not compulsory quest logs. Each event should make clear who is organizing it, what will happen, where it takes place, and whether someone may join, watch, help, or decline. A character's refusal is a valid choice. Do not turn every declined invitation into a conflict or a punishment.

A community becomes credible when its members can participate at different levels. One man organizes; another contributes a skill; another watches; another chooses to spend the afternoon alone. The group remains intact.

## Scene construction

When an activity becomes a scene, establish:

1. What the character intended to do before anyone else arrived.
2. What practical or social condition complicates it.
3. What choice the character makes.
4. Who else contributes, disagrees, teaches, or changes the plan.
5. What is different when the scene ends.
6. What remains unfinished or becomes the next possible action.

Avoid repetitive scenes in which characters merely gather, admire one another, discuss future plans, and disperse. Let them make, learn, play, decide, repair, travel, cook, compete, rest, and return.

## Weekly continuity record

For a recurring ensemble, maintain a lightweight activity ledger. Each entry can use this shape:

| Field | Record |
|---|---|
| Inhabitant / group | Who owns the activity |
| Activity | What is actually happening |
| Place | Where it happens |
| Rhythm | One-time, daily, weekly, seasonal, or irregular |
| Motivation | Why the person chooses it |
| Participants | Who joins, assists, observes, or declines |
| State | Proposed, scheduled, underway, completed, paused, or abandoned |
| Consequence | What changed or was produced |
| Next action | What may happen next |

Do not create an activity ledger that simply repeats character biographies. Update it when something changes. Completed activities remain part of history; they do not need to recur forever.

## Independent worlds and local culture

Each world keeps its own occupations, customs, calendar, institutions, social expectations, and material conditions. The Colossus Chronicles can have the Residents' Cup and Crownmere's rowing clubs; Veyrthalis can have civic duties, processions, markets, and its own local mysteries; Mexiancas can narrate its own work and decisions; the Facility can have departments and routines; the Sacred Corridor can develop room-based habits and social rituals. Do not copy one world's schedule into another merely because the interface is shared.

Where a setting is designated as an all-male fictional social world, preserve that premise in its local population and institutions. Do not use the premise as a substitute for individual characterisation: the men still need distinct work, tastes, disagreements, friendships, boundaries, and private lives.

Cross-world contact follows the Tractus passage rules. A method, artifact, visitor, or motif may travel when a scene establishes the passage. The receiving world interprets it through its own history; its inhabitants are not absorbed into another world's plot.

## Interface translation

The living workspace should show more than a list of character names. Useful views include:

- **Now:** what is happening across places at this moment or during the current period.
- **Coming up:** open invitations and scheduled activities.
- **In progress:** work, practices, journeys, and projects already underway.
- **Recently changed:** completed actions and their consequences.
- **Personal threads:** each inhabitant's current direction and next action.
- **Quiet:** people who are resting, unavailable, alone, or choosing not to participate.

A person should not be marked inactive simply because he is not in the current scene. If his status is unknown, show it as unknown rather than inventing an activity.

## Continuity safeguards

- Do not invent exact employment, family, location, measurements, or personal history when the record leaves it open.
- Do not infer real people's private lives from public accounts used as creative references.
- Keep fictional characters distinct when names, visual references, or production lineages overlap.
- Let characters disagree with the narrator's assumptions and with one another.
- Preserve the ability to change direction. A long-standing ambition can be completed, revised, refused, or replaced.
- Do not keep every character permanently busy. Rest and uneventful time are part of a life.
- Make consequences persist in the next scene, record, location, or relationship.

The measure of a populated world is not how many names it contains. It is how much meaningful life continues when no one is looking.
