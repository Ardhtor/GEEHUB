# GEEHUB // THE ATTENTION ENGINE

## Core relational engine

The men's arcs do not proceed independently of one another. A central relational force connects them: **competition for the Teller's attention**.

This is not reducible to sexual conquest. The competition begins with attraction, but it becomes a broader struggle over recognition, intimacy, memory, presence, loyalty, spectacle, and the desire to become unforgettable.

Joseph is the husband and therefore occupies a distinct continuity position. He does not enter the same contest as a stranger attempting to establish a place. His bond with the Teller is already historical, embodied, and recognized. That fact becomes structurally important whenever other men enter the field of attention.

The other men may seek attention through different means:

- transformation of the body;
- humor, defiance, or charisma;
- quiet presence;
- acts of loyalty;
- rivalry;
- vulnerability;
- shared danger;
- discovery;
- artistic or spiritual creation;
- becoming indispensable to a particular place or experience.

The important distinction is:

**attention → desire → transformation → identity → conflict → history**

Attention is therefore not merely a romantic subplot. It is an arc generator.

## The contest of recognition

A man can obtain attention without becoming important.

A man can become important without demanding attention.

A man can win a moment and lose the memory.

Another can barely speak and nevertheless remain present in the Teller's mind.

The narrative should preserve these differences rather than reducing every relationship to a single hierarchy.

The question underneath the competition is:

> **What does a man become when he wants to be remembered by someone who is already loved by another?**

That question generates divergent arcs.

One man pursues spectacle and discovers that being seen is not the same as being known.

Another pursues loyalty and discovers that wanting someone does not entitle him to possess them.

Two rivals may discover that their competition makes them understand one another more intimately.

A man may initially compete against another man and eventually become his ally.

Joseph may confront the meaning of being the husband while the surrounding world continually produces new forms of attraction, rivalry, and attachment.

The competition therefore produces relationships between the men as well as relationships with the Teller.

## Narrative consequence

The Hub should treat attention as a relational resource, not a numerical score.

There is no canonical leaderboard.

There is instead a changing field of:

`recognition / desire / memory / loyalty / rivalry / intimacy / transformation`

Each new relationship can alter the field.

Each new transformation can change how a man is perceived.

Each remembered encounter can retroactively change the meaning of an earlier one.

This makes the men's arcs path-dependent. They do not simply accumulate events; they accumulate significance.

## Novel rule

`GEEHUB UPDATE → RELATIONSHIP FIELD UPDATE → NOVEL UPDATE`

When a new person, attraction, rivalry, bond, transformation, or recognition event becomes durable enough to affect the world, the novel is eligible to metabolize it.

The Hub preserves the mechanism.

The novel dramatizes the consequences.

The visual layer shows what the consequences look like.

The experience layer determines how they are encountered.

The resulting world is not a collection of men competing for a prize. It is a living social field in which wanting to be seen changes the people doing the looking.


## Collective male hysteria — when the room catches sight of a growing giant

The phrase **collective male hysteria** names a fictional social phenomenon, not a diagnosis. It describes a gathering of adult men whose fascination with another man becomes so intense that it circulates between them, gathers momentum, and begins to alter the physical world around them. In this setting, the focus is not simply a handsome or impressive visitor. It is a man whose body is becoming enormous: a continuous transformation in which height, reach, breadth, muscular mass, and physical presence advance together. The crowd sees him growing, sees the room trying to accommodate him, and discovers that its own excitement is part of the event. The spectacle is social and physical at once.

The first reaction may begin before anyone says a word. A giant man returns to the Institute after training, moving through the covered arcade with his head close to the high timber beams. He has grown since the previous gathering. His old training jacket no longer closes across the chest, his sleeves stop above his wrists, and his stride covers more of the courtyard than the others expect. He knows the building and its people; he is not making an entrance. Yet the men at the long table notice that the familiar doorway frames him differently. A conversation pauses. A chair scrapes backward. Someone looks from the man's shoulders to the reinforced lintel and then to the friend beside him, silently checking whether the change is as great as it seems.

At first, each man believes his response is private. Then he sees that the others have noticed too. One remarks on the new height; another points out how far the visitor's reach extends when he takes a cup from the far side of the table. A third remembers that only a few days earlier the giant had stood comfortably beneath the old arch. Their different observations confirm one another. A glance becomes a signal; a joke becomes permission to admit what everyone has been trying not to say; a comparison brings the room's attention back to the same changing body. They are not watching a finished monument. They are watching a man whose scale is still increasing.

The first visible sign of collective hysteria is a failure of ordinary conversation. Two men lose the thread of the repair they were discussing. A third repeats a question because he has been following the visitor's shoulders as he passes the doorway. Somebody tries to estimate the distance between the man's hand and the tabletop, then abandons the attempt when the giant reaches down and lifts an entire stack of programme folders without changing his stance. Another laughs too loudly, embarrassed by how quickly he agreed with the first observation. They return to the speaker they were repairing, but now the workbench seems unusually small. The visitor leans beside it to inspect the loose connection, and the broad span of his back changes the room's proportions. The table has not moved; their sense of its scale has.

Then the visitor grows again. The change is gradual enough to be watched rather than guessed: his posture rises, his legs lengthen beneath him, his shoulders spread and settle, and the mass through his chest and arms increases in proportion. His hands become larger around the small screwdriver, so he sets it down and asks for the longer-handled tool. The ordinary act of choosing a better tool makes the transformation undeniable. One man stands, then another, not because they have been ordered to but because sitting suddenly feels inadequate to the height of the conversation. The giant looks down at them with the same familiar expression he brought into the room. His face has not become a mask of power. He is still the man who returned a jacket, remembered the speaker's repair, and thanked the person who moved a chair for him.

The group begins to organize itself around the fascination. Men who were strangers a moment ago exchange knowing looks. The quietest one offers the most precise account of the change, remembering the height of the previous door mark and the length of the visitor's old sleeves. The joker becomes an announcer, describing the increasingly impractical furniture as though the evening has become a public exhibition. Someone imitates the giant's careful way of turning through the doorway; someone else objects that the imitation misses how his stride has changed. The correction is funnier than the original joke. A third man, who had been trying to finish his food, puts down his fork and admits that he has also been watching. The confession releases another round of laughter, because the room can now acknowledge its fascination together.

The phenomenon becomes stranger when the giant's growth begins to affect the others. In this fictional setting, shared attention can produce a **scale echo**: not a compulsory transformation and not an identical enlargement, but a temporary pressure toward greater physical presence among men who become deeply involved in the encounter. One man's shoulders broaden; another discovers that his sleeves have shortened; a third stands taller and feels his reach extending beyond the familiar edge of the table. Some remain unchanged and become the careful witnesses of the event. Others welcome the change, testing their new reach or laughing at the way a chair now feels too small. The scale echo is uneven and personal. It does not erase their bodies or give the crowd a single prescribed ideal. It allows the giant's ongoing transformation to become a social weather system in which the room's occupants may also discover new baselines.

This is the contagious interval. Each man receives evidence that his fascination is shared, and that evidence intensifies the encounter. One man's admiration gives another permission to speak; the second man's candour makes the first more confident; their exchange draws in a third. A few become theatrical, a few uncharacteristically shy, and one or two competitive, suddenly conscious of their posture, clothing, strength, or changing size. They may straighten as they grow, adjust a sleeve that has become too short, volunteer to move the heavier furniture, or compare the new length of their arms. Attraction, admiration, envy, curiosity, amusement, identification, and the wish to be noticed can coexist. The spectacle comes from their different motives converging on one man, while their bodies and the room continue responding to the pressure of scale.

The central man experiences the other side of this change. He notices the broken conversation, the extraordinary courtesy, the men measuring the doorway against his height, and the sudden number of people offering to move furniture or fetch a stronger chair. He may find it flattering, funny, bewildering, or tiring. He can enjoy being admired without agreeing to become the evening's entertainment. He can answer a compliment, return a joke, ask what everyone is measuring, or say that he would rather finish his meal in peace. His growth remains his own: the crowd may react to it, encourage it, or participate in a scale echo, but it cannot dictate his next transformation. His response determines whether the room settles into fellowship, becomes a playful exhibition, or needs to give him space.

A useful scene begins in the Institute's common room, which has a purpose other than watching him. The men have gathered after a long day: some still in training clothes, others carrying notebooks or food from the dining hall. A long oak table holds cups, a half-repaired speaker, programme sheets, and a bowl of oranges. The visitor arrives to return a borrowed jacket. He has grown since the previous evening, and the jacket now hangs from two fingers rather than fitting over his shoulders. He must duck beneath the raised lintel. When he straightens, his head nearly reaches the upper crossbeam. He sets the jacket carefully on the table and asks whether the speaker has been fixed.

The answer takes too long. One man begins explaining the repair and forgets the part's name. Another whispers a comment about the giant's new proportions, but half the table hears him. The friend beside him looks up, then looks again, measuring the distance from the visitor's hand to the edge of the bench. A serious man who rarely joins in opens his notebook and sketches the changed silhouette from memory. The joker asks whether the room has become a surveying office. Three men laugh; a fourth says the measurements are actually overdue. The giant smiles, and the effort of holding back another joke makes his chest rise beneath the training shirt. The fabric, already stretched across his shoulders, tightens another degree as the next small phase of growth passes through him.

That movement sends the group into a new round of excitement. One man moves a chair, then finds that his own frame has broadened enough to make the gap awkward. He laughs and asks for help. Another takes the heavy end of the table, surprised by how easily he can now lift it. A third, still unchanged, directs them so that the speaker and the oranges are not knocked to the floor. The giant bends to help, but his reach now carries his hand across almost the full length of the table; the men watch with the absorbed attention of spectators who have forgotten they are also participants. He asks them to stop staring and hold the other end. They do, and the scene becomes practical again: a shared lift, a shifted table, a clear route through the room.

When the visitor finally sits, the old chair gives a warning creak. Nobody is hurt. He rises immediately, and the men replace it with a reinforced bench that the workshop had been preparing for another resident. The giant is amused by the ceremony and asks whether the bench will survive dinner. The carpenter, whose own arms have become visibly thicker during the scale echo, says that it will if everyone stops treating the new bolts as decoration. The line earns another burst of laughter. The men are no longer only observing the giant; they are building the room that will let them spend time with him. The architecture becomes part of their collective response, and their changed bodies become part of the work.

The visitor looks around and recognizes that the men have become almost as excited by one another's changes as by his own. He gives them a choice: continue the measurements and make an evening of it, or finish the repair and eat while the food is warm. The group hesitates, then chooses both in the order he suggests. They complete the speaker repair, test it with a low piece of music, extend the table, and bring out more food. The giant takes the reinforced bench, his knees and long legs occupying the space beneath it in a way that makes the earlier furniture look miniature. He remains gentle and unhurried. His size changes the room, but it does not change the way he listens when a man across from him speaks.

Later, after the visitor has gone, the collective reaction becomes its own subject. The men compare accounts. Each remembers a different moment as the beginning: the jacket that no longer fit, the raised doorway, the hand crossing the table, the bench, or the moment when several of them discovered that they had grown too. Their memories do not quite agree. One says the visitor was already enormous when he arrived; another insists the real shock was the second growth phase, when the room seemed to contract around him. The quiet man studies his sketch and marks the difference between the first and final silhouette. The joker claims that the entire evening was a coordinated enlargement programme, though nobody can establish who coordinated it. They laugh, check the new furniture, and make plans for the next gathering. The giant's return has become an event in the common life of the Institute.

The phenomenon can develop in several directions. It may remain a comic disturbance, a roomful of men becoming ridiculous together as one man grows beyond the room's assumptions. It may become an affectionate social myth, with his ordinary gestures retold alongside each new physical stage until the group distinguishes direct memory from embellishment. It may sharpen into rivalry, as men compare their own growth and try to become equally noticeable. It may produce friendships between men who admire the same giant for different reasons. It may also create practical obligations: stronger furniture, higher doorways, revised training equipment, new clothing patterns, and a shared record of each person's changing baseline. Or it may become uncomfortable when the crowd mistakes shared excitement for permission to pressure him. If attention turns into persistent surveillance, unwanted pursuit, invasive speculation, or refusal to accept a boundary, the scene has changed into a breach of trust. Growth does not cancel consent, and giantness does not make a person public property. The repair must be shown, not assumed.

For the Attention Engine, the mechanism is:

**individual notice → recognition of shared notice → public admission → mutual amplification → visible growth and scale echo → adaptation of bodies and place → boundary or reciprocation → remembered event**

The central man is not merely the object around which the others arrange themselves. He remains a participant with his own interpretation and next action. The group is changed by what it sees, but it is not allowed to define him completely. His body may become monumental; his identity remains continuous. The crowd may grow in response, but it remains plural, with different temperaments, ambitions, limits, and choices.

The strongest version is neither a hostile mob nor a chorus of identical admirers. It is a room full of distinct men whose composure fails in different, revealing ways while a giant continues becoming larger among them. One is funny, one analytical, one competitive, one openly admiring, one quietly affected, one embarrassed by the intensity of his attention. Their height, reach, and strength may change at different rates; their friendship and rivalry change through what they do with those changes. The man at the centre may smile, tease them, join the work, invite a comparison, or leave; whatever he chooses becomes part of the event. The hysteria is memorable because an ordinary room becomes a place where bodies, furniture, conversation, and social order all have to make room for a growing man—and because the men discover that their own scale is changing in the act of looking.


## The everywhere error — local event mistaken for universal condition

A defining feature of collective male hysteria is that the men repeatedly confuse **what is happening here** with **what must be happening everywhere**. Their inference outruns their evidence. A single giant grows in one room, and the men begin speaking as though the entire world has entered a growth phase. One doorway has to be raised, and they start discussing architecture as if every building is already changing. Three men experience a scale echo, and the group treats it as proof that every man, in every city, must be feeling the same pressure. The mistake is not merely verbal exaggeration; in this fictional world, the mistaken belief can itself become a force that spreads attention, expectation, and sometimes further growth.

The men do not necessarily believe they are exaggerating. Their local evidence feels so vivid that it becomes a model for reality as a whole. They see the giant's shoulders rise above the common-room lintel and conclude that ordinary ceilings are becoming obsolete. They notice their own shirts tightening and assume that wardrobes everywhere are being outgrown. A friend says that he has never seen anything like it; another hears this as confirmation that something unprecedented is happening to everyone. A man checks his phone for reports from elsewhere, finds no evidence, and decides that the rest of the world simply has not noticed yet. The absence of confirmation is folded into the theory.

This creates a comic and sometimes unnerving feedback loop: **local event → universal claim → expectation everywhere → selective noticing → apparent confirmation → larger universal claim**. Each man repeats the others' interpretation until the room's shared certainty feels more substantial than the facts. Their language expands faster than the giant's body. They say “everywhere” when they mean “in this room,” “everyone” when they mean “the men at this table,” and “the whole world” when they mean the handful of people who have just watched a man grow through his shirt. The giant may be the only person who notices the difference.

The scale phenomenon gives the confusion physical consequences. When the men expect growth to be universal, they begin to carry themselves as though their own bodies are already changing. Some do begin to grow, through the established scale echo; others remain at their previous baselines, and the contrast becomes another source of argument. A man whose shoulders have broadened insists that the whole group is changing. His friend, whose measurements have not moved, replies that he is simply standing differently. A third man has grown in height but not breadth and claims the distinction proves that the transformation is spreading in different forms. The group turns each exception into another reason to believe the rule.

Keep the error plural and character-specific. The joker announces that the town will need taller streetlights by morning. The practical carpenter starts calculating beam spans, partly as a joke and partly because he can see that the central giant has grown another inch while they have been talking. The analytical man insists on recording which changes were directly observed and which were merely reported. The most excitable man has already told someone in another building that the city is getting bigger. A quieter man looks out through the window at the unchanged street and wonders whether the world is actually changing, or whether the group has become unable to imagine a place untouched by what they have witnessed.

The central giant should not automatically correct every claim. He may find the exaggeration affectionate, ridiculous, flattering, or exhausting. He can let the men enjoy their theory for a while, ask whether they have personally inspected the next town, or point out that the only confirmed new measurement is his own. His continued growth complicates the correction: while they are arguing about whether the phenomenon is universal, his head rises closer to the beam and the carpenter quietly moves the measuring ladder. The event remains real even when the explanation is inflated.

The narrative must preserve a distinction between **world fact, local observation, hearsay, and collective inference**. The men may be wrong about the scale of what is happening without the story treating their experience as meaningless. Their confusion can generate new scenes, journeys, rumours, institutional preparations, and genuine discoveries. Eventually they may find evidence that growth is spreading beyond the Institute—but that discovery must arrive as an event, not as an automatic reward for their certainty. Until then, “everywhere” is their characteristic mistake: a local wonder so overwhelming that they mistake it for the condition of existence itself.


## The relationships are the subject

The collective event is the setting in which the men's relationships become visible; it is not the subject that should consume all the narrative attention. The important question is not only why the men become fascinated by a growing giant, but what they become to one another while that fascination is unfolding. A scene should spend its depth on the particular bonds: the friend who knows when another man's joking is covering embarrassment; the rival who notices a change in his competitor before anyone else does; the quiet observer who keeps a careful record because he wants the group to remember accurately; the carpenter who stays late to finish a stronger bench with the man who will use it; the giant who remembers which of them dislikes being made the centre of attention.

The men must not function as a single audience. Give each pair and small alliance a history, a private language, a source of affection, and something that can trouble the bond. Two men may both admire the giant yet respond to one another in completely different ways. One is openly enthusiastic; the other becomes reserved and unexpectedly protective. Their first disagreement may concern whether the giant wants to be watched, but the lasting scene is the one in which they discover how well they can read each other's discomfort. The rivalry between two growing men may begin with measurements and teasing, then develop into a training partnership in which each knows when the other is pushing too hard. A joke that initially humiliates one man can become an affectionate shared reference only after an apology and a change in conduct. Do not skip the intermediate moments that earn intimacy.

The central giant is a person inside this network, not a prize around which the others orbit. Show his separate relationship with each man: the friend he trusts with practical decisions, the one whose teasing makes him laugh, the competitor whose strength he respects, the observer whose notes he sometimes disputes, and the newer acquaintance who is still learning how to speak to him without turning every meeting into an event. He may also have friendships that have little to do with his size or the group's fascination. Those friendships should continue when he leaves the room. The men must have lives and bonds that remain active in his absence.

The Everywhere Error should deepen those relationships rather than displace them. The men may mistake one local growth event for a universal change, but the scene should dwell on how they handle that mistake together. Perhaps one man repeats the claim outside the group and another feels exposed by it; perhaps the skeptic becomes the person everyone trusts to separate what they saw from what they have heard; perhaps two friends keep extending the joke long after the others have stopped finding it funny. The question is not merely whether the whole city is growing. It is whether these men can disagree without humiliating one another, share excitement without demanding identical feelings, and remain loyal when their interpretations diverge.

Let relationships develop in actions that require time: waiting after training, carrying the heavy end together, lending clothing that no longer fits, remembering a measurement without using it to embarrass someone, making room at a table, checking in after an overwhelming public gathering, repairing a mistake, sharing a meal, or keeping a confidence. Growth remains present in all of this. One man's new height changes how he leans down to hear his friend; another's widening shoulders make their old side-by-side walk awkward until they find a new rhythm; a reinforced bench becomes something two men built together and therefore a shared memory. Giantness gives the relationships material consequences, but the consequences matter because of who is doing them for whom.

### After the room empties

When the gathering breaks up, do not end the relational story with the spectacle. Let two men remain behind. One gathers the cups while the other checks the bolts in the new bench. They have been disagreeing all evening about whether the growth is spreading beyond the Institute. The taller man has made the universal claim; the other has refused to repeat it without evidence. At first the disagreement sounded like a contest over who understood the event. Now, as they work, the first admits that he wanted the others to feel what he felt and was afraid that careful language would make the moment seem smaller. The second tells him that he believed him about the growth all along; he only wanted the group to be able to trust what they said about it tomorrow. They finish tightening the same bolt. Neither wins the argument, but each understands the other's reason more clearly. Outside, the giant crosses the courtyard on his way home, still taller than he was at dinner, and raises one hand when he sees them. Both men wave back. The event belongs to all of them, but the friendship belongs particularly to the two who stayed.

This is the required movement of depth: a transformation changes the conditions of an encounter; the encounter reveals a relationship; the relationship changes what a character does next. Do not spend several paragraphs explaining the scale echo when those paragraphs could show two men becoming brothers, friends, lovers, rivals, confidants, or something not yet named.


## “She sickens me” — the woman above the courtyard

The sentence came from a man who had spent the better part of the afternoon pretending that he was not watching the southern horizon. By the time he said it, the woman had already passed the old bell tower. She was so tall that the tower's roof reached only a little above her waist, and the pale stone buildings along the avenue seemed arranged at her feet like objects on a workbench. She was still growing. The change was gradual but visible against the fixed lines of the city: her shoulders rose beyond the roofline, her stride lengthened, and the enormous shadow cast by her upper body moved across the courtyard several seconds after she had passed the sun.

“She sickens me,” he said.

His oldest friend did not answer immediately. He knew the voice. He had heard it when the speaker was embarrassed, when he was frightened, when he wanted to be contradicted but could not bear to ask. He had heard it most clearly the night his friend first crossed the ten-foot mark and insisted that the measuring team had made a mistake. Now the friend stood broad and tall beside him, his own new height forcing him to lower his head beneath the gallery beam. His shirt was tight across his back; the growth that had begun that morning had not finished. He could have agreed with the sentence and made the moment easy. Instead he asked, quietly, “What part?”

The question annoyed the speaker. He looked back at the woman. At this distance her face was difficult to read, but she was not moving carelessly. She paused at the river crossing while the engineers below her checked the temporary supports. She waited until the signal flags were raised, then stepped across the reinforced span exactly where the surveyors had marked the safe passage. Her size was extraordinary; her attention was precise.

“All of it,” he said. “The way everyone stops. The way they look at her as if she's the only thing that has ever happened.”

His friend recognised the complaint. It was not really about her. For years the two men had occupied the same places together: the gymnasium, the dining hall, the long benches outside the workshop. They had measured each other's progress without keeping score, or so they had said. When the speaker began growing, his friend had been the first to notice the changes and the first to make him laugh about them. When his friend grew beyond him, the speaker had congratulated him, then gone quiet for three days. Neither had ever quite discussed those three days. Now the woman had arrived at a scale that made their old rivalry look like an argument over inches, and the speaker felt himself becoming a familiar version of a man he did not particularly like.

Across the courtyard, another man called out that the whole world must be changing. Someone answered that it already had. A third began telling the story of a woman in the northern provinces who supposedly stood as high as a mountain. The surveyor at the gate told them to stop turning one sighting into a geography. They laughed, but not all of them were laughing at the same thing. One man was thrilled by the possibility; another was terrified; a third wanted to be the one who had known first. The woman continued along the river, and the crowd's account of her grew larger each time it passed between mouths.

The speaker watched his oldest friend watching him. “You think I'm jealous.”

“I think you hate how much you care that they're looking at her.”

“That isn't the same thing.”

“No,” his friend said. “It isn't.”

That answer unsettled him more than an accusation would have. His friend had not taken the woman's side against him. He had not offered the easy comfort of pretending the sentence was true. He had left room for the thing beneath it, and that was harder to escape. The speaker looked down at his own hands. They had grown again: the fingers were thicker, the knuckles more prominent, and the old ring he wore on a cord now rested against his palm instead of fitting as it once had. He had spent the morning pleased by those changes. He had wanted his friend to notice. He had wanted the others to notice. He had wanted, for one unguarded instant, to be the largest presence in the courtyard.

His friend took the measuring cord from the rail and offered him one end. “Come on.”

“What for?”

“To measure you. You keep looking at her as if the only interesting thing in the world is how tall she is.”

The speaker almost refused. Then he took the cord. They stood shoulder to shoulder against the old stone marker, their height marks already crowded together from the previous week. His friend held the upper end while he made the mark. Neither joked about who had gained more. They compared the result, then moved the cord to the next mark and checked the breadth of the speaker's shoulders. The figures were ordinary to them now, but the act was not. It returned them to a private ritual that had existed before the woman appeared, before the courtyard filled, before the whole city began speaking as if it knew what was happening everywhere.

The speaker let out a breath. “I don't want to be that man.”

“Which man?”

“The one who can only be happy when nobody else is bigger.”

His friend coiled the cord carefully. “Then don't be.”

The answer was blunt, and because it was blunt the speaker trusted it. He reached for the other man's shoulder. The contact had changed with their bodies: once it had been an easy clasp, now his hand covered much of the slope of his friend's upper arm. His friend leaned into it for a moment. There was no contest in that gesture. Their bodies had become enormous by ordinary standards, and still the gesture was recognisable from the days when they had stood at the same height.

The woman reached the far bank. The engineers waved her onward, and she continued toward the western ridge, where the road broadened into the old quarry route. Her steps sent a faint vibration through the courtyard stones. The men felt it under their feet. One of them said that the whole ridge would have to be rebuilt. Another said that every ridge in the country would. The surveyor began correcting them again, but this time the speaker joined him. He told the others what they actually knew: one woman had crossed one marked route; the bridge had held; the western road would need inspection. He did not make her smaller in order to describe her accurately.

His friend heard the change in his voice. “Better,” he said.

“Don't sound so pleased.”

“I am pleased.”

They laughed, and the sound brought two other men over from the workshop. One had been arguing with the surveyor; the other had followed the woman's passage with an expression of undisguised wonder. The four of them stood together, all at different heights, all changing at different rates. The youngest had grown enough that his sleeves no longer covered his wrists. The quietest had not gained an inch, but he had become the person everyone trusted to record the measurements. Their friendship was not produced by the woman or by the giant scale of the day. It had been forming through meals, arguments, repairs, and shared training for months. The event gave them something new to carry into it.

“She sickens me,” the speaker repeated, more softly.

This time his friend heard a different sentence inside it. “Then tell me what you mean before you tell everybody else.”

The speaker looked toward the ridge. The woman was almost beyond the city now, still immense, still moving with care. “I think I wanted to be the one they couldn't look away from.”

His friend nodded. He did not make the admission into a joke. “I know.”

That was the moment the relationship moved. Not because the speaker had become kinder in an instant, nor because his jealousy had disappeared, but because he had trusted someone with the truth underneath his cruelty and that person had remained beside him. The other two men heard enough to understand without intruding. One offered to help with the road inspection. The other asked whether they were still coming to dinner. The conversation returned to ordinary obligations, but the men walked out together, their long strides falling into a common rhythm as the speaker's shoulders continued to broaden beneath his shirt.

The woman had not been made responsible for their feelings. She had her own route, her own work, and her own scale of life. The men had their own history. Their task was not to decide whether she deserved to be seen; it was to discover what happened to their friendships when the world offered them someone they could not easily compare themselves against. By evening, the speaker would have to decide whether to apologise for the way he had spoken about her. His friend would not do it for him. He would, however, walk with him to the western road if he chose to go.

The line remained in the courtyard, but it no longer described the woman. It described the distance the speaker had almost allowed to grow between himself and the man who knew him best.
