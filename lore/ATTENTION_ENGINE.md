# GEEHUB // THE ATTENTION ENGINE

## Core relational engine

The men's arcs do not proceed independently of one another. A central relational force connects them: **competition for the Teller's attention**.

This is not reducible to sexual conquest. The competition begins with attraction, but it becomes a broader struggle over recognition, intimacy, memory, presence, loyalty, spectacle, and the desire to become unforgettable.

Joseph is the husband and therefore occupies a distinct continuity position. He does not enter the same contest as a stranger attempting to establish a place. His bond with the Teller is already historical, embodied, and recognized. That fact becomes structurally important whenever other men enter the field of attention.

The other men may seek attention through different means:

- transformation of the body;
- humor, defiance, or charisma;
- quiet presence;
- acts of loyalty;
- rivalry;
- vulnerability;
- shared danger;
- discovery;
- artistic or spiritual creation;
- becoming indispensable to a particular place or experience.

The important distinction is:

**attention → desire → transformation → identity → conflict → history**

Attention is therefore not merely a romantic subplot. It is an arc generator.

## The contest of recognition

A man can obtain attention without becoming important.

A man can become important without demanding attention.

A man can win a moment and lose the memory.

Another can barely speak and nevertheless remain present in the Teller's mind.

The narrative should preserve these differences rather than reducing every relationship to a single hierarchy.

The question underneath the competition is:

> **What does a man become when he wants to be remembered by someone who is already loved by another?**

That question generates divergent arcs.

One man pursues spectacle and discovers that being seen is not the same as being known.

Another pursues loyalty and discovers that wanting someone does not entitle him to possess them.

Two rivals may discover that their competition makes them understand one another more intimately.

A man may initially compete against another man and eventually become his ally.

Joseph may confront the meaning of being the husband while the surrounding world continually produces new forms of attraction, rivalry, and attachment.

The competition therefore produces relationships between the men as well as relationships with the Teller.

## Narrative consequence

The Hub should treat attention as a relational resource, not a numerical score.

There is no canonical leaderboard.

There is instead a changing field of:

`recognition / desire / memory / loyalty / rivalry / intimacy / transformation`

Each new relationship can alter the field.

Each new transformation can change how a man is perceived.

Each remembered encounter can retroactively change the meaning of an earlier one.

This makes the men's arcs path-dependent. They do not simply accumulate events; they accumulate significance.

## Novel rule

`GEEHUB UPDATE → RELATIONSHIP FIELD UPDATE → NOVEL UPDATE`

When a new person, attraction, rivalry, bond, transformation, or recognition event becomes durable enough to affect the world, the novel is eligible to metabolize it.

The Hub preserves the mechanism.

The novel dramatizes the consequences.

The visual layer shows what the consequences look like.

The experience layer determines how they are encountered.

The resulting world is not a collection of men competing for a prize. It is a living social field in which wanting to be seen changes the people doing the looking.


## Collective male hysteria — when the room catches sight of one man

The phrase **collective male hysteria** names a fictional social phenomenon, not a diagnosis. It describes the moment a group of adult men becomes so intensely preoccupied with another man that their attention begins to circulate between them. At first, each believes he is having a private reaction. Then he notices that the others have noticed too. A glance becomes a signal; a joke becomes permission to admit what everyone has been trying not to say; a comparison brings the whole room's attention back to the same person. The men begin reacting not only to the man who has caught their interest, but to one another's reactions to him. The attention becomes self-amplifying.

It does not require the central man to perform, provoke, or even understand what is happening. He might enter a gymnasium after training, step through a crowded common room with a jacket over one shoulder, arrive at a workshop to collect something he has made, or simply stand at the end of a table listening while somebody else speaks. Something about him gathers the room: his size, his ease, the particular way he occupies space, a voice that remains level while the room grows louder, an unexpectedly gentle manner, or the striking contrast between physical presence and ordinary politeness. There is no universal formula. The cause may be visible, but the strength of the reaction comes from the crowd's shared discovery that they are all looking.

The first sign is a small failure of conversation. Two men lose the thread of what they were saying. A third repeats a question because he has not heard the answer. Somebody makes a remark about the visitor's shoulders, his height, the way he has to turn slightly to pass between the tables. Another man laughs too loudly, embarrassed by how quickly he agreed. They try to resume the original conversation, but each new subject seems to bend back toward the visitor. Even silence becomes communicative: the men notice who is watching, who has looked away, who is pretending not to be interested, and who has plainly given up pretending.

Then the room begins to organize itself around the fascination. Men who were strangers a moment ago exchange knowing looks. The quietest one offers the most precise observation. The joker becomes an announcer, giving a running account of ordinary movements as though an entrance, a stretch, or the simple act of sitting down deserves a place in the evening's history. Someone imitates the visitor's unhurried walk; someone else protests that the imitation is inaccurate, then demonstrates a more accurate version. The correction is funnier than the original joke. A third man, who had been trying to concentrate on his food, finally puts down his fork and admits that he has also been watching. The confession releases another round of laughter, not because the interest has disappeared, but because the group can now acknowledge it together.

This is the contagious interval. Each man receives evidence that his own response is not isolated, and that evidence intensifies the response. One man's admiration gives another permission to speak; the second man's candour makes the first more confident; their exchange draws in the third. A few men become theatrical, a few become uncharacteristically shy, and one or two grow competitive, suddenly conscious of their own posture, clothing, voice, strength, or place in the room. They may stand a little straighter, adjust a sleeve, volunteer to carry something, or become unusually helpful. They are not all feeling the same thing. Attraction, admiration, curiosity, envy, amusement, identification, and the wish to be noticed can coexist in the same crowd. The spectacle is produced by their different motives converging on one person.

The central man experiences the other side of this change. He notices the broken conversation, the strange courtesy, the sudden number of people offering help, and the way a room seems to wait for his reaction. He may find it flattering, funny, bewildering, or tiring. He can enjoy being admired without agreeing to become the evening's entertainment. He can answer a compliment, return a joke, ask what everybody is looking at, or tell them that he would rather finish his meal in peace. His response is consequential. The group may settle when he makes a boundary clear; it may recover its ordinary rhythm when he joins the conversation as a person rather than an image. The attention does not grant the crowd ownership of his time, body, privacy, or affection.

A useful scene begins in a room with a purpose other than watching him. The men have gathered after a long day in the Institute: some still in training clothes, others carrying notebooks or food from the dining hall. A table is crowded with cups, a half-repaired speaker, a stack of programme sheets, and a large bowl of oranges somebody has brought from the kitchen. The visitor comes in to return a borrowed jacket. He is not announced. He does not stop at the door to make an entrance. He crosses behind two chairs, thanks the man who moves them, and folds the jacket over the back of an empty seat.

The conversation stutters. One man had been explaining a repair to the speaker and now cannot remember the name of the part. Another leans toward his friend and whispers a comment that is meant to be private but is heard by half the table. The friend gives him a look that says he noticed the same thing. At the far end, a serious man who rarely joins in is studying the visitor's proportions with the concentration he usually reserves for a difficult drawing. He catches somebody watching him and closes his notebook. The joker asks whether the room has suddenly become a portrait studio. Three men laugh; a fourth says that the lighting is actually terrible. The visitor looks from one to the next, understands that the conversation has somehow become about him, and raises an eyebrow.

That small acknowledgement makes the room worse before it makes it better. The men laugh at themselves. One tries to explain that they were discussing the training programme, which is true in the broadest possible sense and false in every immediate detail. Another says that the visitor has a way of making everyone look as though they have forgotten what they came in for. The central man replies that he has only come to return a jacket. The simplicity of the answer breaks the spell. Somebody takes the jacket, checks that the pocket still contains the keys, and thanks him. A chair is offered. He accepts or declines. The speaker is repaired, the oranges are passed around, and the conversation gradually resumes—but the room has acquired a new shared memory, and the men will remember the absurd moment when all of them lost the thread at once.

Later, when the visitor has left, the collective reaction becomes its own subject. The men compare accounts. Each remembers a different detail as the moment it began. One says it was the entrance; another insists it was the voice; the quiet man says it was the way the visitor thanked somebody without interrupting the conversation. The joker claims that the whole thing was a coordinated event, although nobody can establish who coordinated it. Someone asks whether they are going to behave like that next time. They say no, then immediately begin speculating about when he will return. Their laughter now contains recognition: the group has discovered a weakness in its own composure and has begun to make a ritual out of remembering it.

The phenomenon can develop in several directions. It may remain a brief comic disturbance, a roomful of men becoming ridiculous together and then returning to their work. It may become an affectionate social myth, with the visitor's ordinary gestures retold and enlarged until the group has to distinguish memory from embellishment. It may sharpen into rivalry, as men begin to compete for a moment of conversation or try to become equally noticeable. It may produce unexpected friendship between men who discover that they admire the same person for different reasons. Or it may become uncomfortable when the crowd mistakes its shared excitement for permission to pressure him. The story must recognize that threshold. If the attention turns into persistent surveillance, unwanted pursuit, invasive speculation, or refusal to accept a boundary, the scene has changed from playful collective fascination into a breach of trust. The repair must be shown, not assumed.

For the Attention Engine, the mechanism is:

**individual notice → recognition of shared notice → public admission → mutual amplification → temporary social choreography → boundary or reciprocation → remembered event**

The key is that the central man is not merely the object around which the others arrange themselves. He remains a participant with his own interpretation and next action. The group is changed by what it sees, but he is not required to become what the group imagines. Their fascination may make him briefly enormous in the social landscape; it cannot make their version of him more authoritative than his own choices.

The strongest version is neither a hostile mob nor a chorus of identical admirers. It is a room full of distinct men whose composure fails in different, revealing ways. One is funny, one analytical, one competitive, one openly admiring, one quietly affected, one embarrassed by the intensity of his own attention. They discover one another through the person who has caught their interest. The man at the centre may smile, tease them, join them, or leave; whatever he chooses becomes part of the event. The crowd's hysteria is memorable because, for a few minutes, an ordinary room becomes a place where nobody can quite pretend not to care—and where everyone has to decide what to do with that discovery.
