# BEOWULF AND THE GEATISH COMPANY

## The Sea-Road into the Tractus

The oldest account of Beowulf in the Tractus begins not with a monster, but with a shore. The sea is iron-grey beneath a low northern sky, and the men who come ashore have spent so many days listening to the hull complain against the waves that the silence of land feels at first like another kind of weather. They arrive as a company, not as scattered heroes: Beowulf at their head, broad-shouldered and watchful; Wulfgar, whose speech is measured; Wiglaf, younger than the others but already attentive to the weight of a promise; and the seasoned Geats whose names have been carried through the old poem in different degrees of clarity. They are adults, veterans of voyages and obligations, and each has a life that does not begin and end with the leader's reputation.

Their ship is drawn above the tide line and secured with ropes to a stone ring set into the harbour wall. The harbour-master expects a raid, a tribute demand, or a request for passage. Instead Beowulf asks where a tired crew can eat, where the horses can be watered, and whether there is a hall in which a foreign company may sit without being mistaken for an invading force. The answer takes them inland to Northgate, where the university's covered arcade meets the old road and the Long Table is already laying out supper. Thus the Geats enter the Tractus through hospitality before they enter it through legend.

The company is received with curiosity, not submission. Their armour is unfamiliar in its workmanship, their speech carries the salt and cadence of another age, and the old names in their account of kinship are known to the historians but not to the cooks serving them. The first meal is deliberately ordinary: thick barley bread, smoked fish, onions softened in butter, roasted roots, a pot of beans with herbs, and dark ale for those who choose it, with water and hot broth for the rest. Beowulf eats slowly. He notices who serves, who waits, and who sits down only after the visitors have taken their places. He has been welcomed into halls where generosity was a public performance and into halls where a guest's safety depended on the mood of the lord. Northgate's welcome is quieter. Nobody asks him to boast before he is fed.

## The Company at Northgate

The Geats are given rooms in the old Founders Dormitory while the harbour workshops examine their ship and the map library tries to establish the route by which they arrived. Their company keeps its own habits: weapons are cleaned and stored with care, boots are placed in a line near the door, and the men take turns checking the moorings and the condition of their equipment. Yet the dormitory changes the rhythm of their days. They discover hot water on demand, a kitchen in which anyone may prepare a meal, a repair bench that is open to students, and a noticeboard where a person can ask for help without declaring himself defeated.

Beowulf does not demand a private chamber or a ceremonial chair. He chooses a room near the stairs, where he can hear the company returning. Wiglaf takes the room across the passage. Wulfgar learns the kitchen's routine and becomes unexpectedly particular about the bread. One of the older Geats, whose hands are scarred from years of rope and iron, spends an afternoon with the workshop staff learning how their lifting frame works. Another discovers the print room and becomes absorbed by the strange permanence of ink on paper. Their names are entered into the university register as guests and companions, not as specimens. The archive records what they consent to share; it does not claim ownership of their memories.

The athletic commons draws the company in a different way. Its timber roof and high windows remind them of a hall, but the room is arranged for practice rather than display. Some of the Geats test their balance and strength against unfamiliar equipment. Others prefer wrestling drills, measured carries, or the patient work of repairing a damaged training frame. The university's military-like ensemble invites them to observe a formation exercise. Beowulf understands the discipline at once, but he is surprised by its purpose: the group is learning to move heavy materials through a crowded street without injuring bystanders, to raise a temporary bridge, and to coordinate a rescue when a river path floods. Strength is treated as a public capacity. The men are not being trained to find an enemy.

This becomes one of the first questions Beowulf asks the ensemble's captain. What is a strong man for, if not to stand between danger and those who cannot withstand it? The captain answers that protection is necessary, but that a man who only knows how to stand between two sides may begin to mistake every difference for a threat. Here, strength must also build, carry, repair, teach, and make room. Beowulf does not agree immediately. He keeps the answer with him.

## Heorot Remembered

The Tractus does not erase Heorot or reduce it to a story that can be corrected by a modern institution. The great hall remains in the Geats' memory with its gold fittings, benches, songs, boasts, quarrels, and long winter nights. Hrothgar's grief remains real. So does the terror of Grendel, whose visits turned the hall from a place of fellowship into a place men feared to enter after dark. Beowulf's victory is part of the history that brought him here, but the Tractus is interested in what the victory cost, what it repaired, and what it could not repair.

At Northgate's map library, a historian lays out a series of coastal charts and asks Beowulf to describe the approach to Heorot. The conversation becomes a reconstruction of sound as much as geography. Beowulf remembers the hall before the attacks: the scrape of benches, the smell of roasting meat, the harp, the clatter of cups, the small arguments that were forgotten by morning. He remembers the silence afterward. The historian records both, refusing to make the monster more vivid than the human life it interrupted.

A room in the university's winter garden is set aside for the account. It is not called the Grendel Room. The Geats choose the name Hall of Returning Voices, because the purpose is to preserve the people who gathered before fear narrowed their world. Their testimony includes the names of those who did not fight, those who kept the hearth, those who carried the wounded, and those who left. The record makes room for uncertainty. Where the old poem gives a name, the archive preserves it; where it does not, the record says so rather than inventing a life to fill the gap.

Beowulf is asked whether he would tell the story differently now. He says that a story told after victory is always in danger of making the danger seem necessary. He will not deny the fight, but neither will he allow the hall to be remembered only as the place where a monster came. This becomes a principle of the Tractus archive: preserve the deed, but also preserve the ordinary world that gave the deed its meaning.

## The Mere and the Measure of Courage

The story of the mere reaches the university through a different route. The river engineers hear of a submerged passage in the Geats' account and invite Beowulf, Wiglaf, and two of the older sailors to inspect a flooded chamber beneath the old quay. It is not the haunted mere of the poem, and nobody pretends that the two places are identical. Yet the descent unsettles Beowulf. The light thins, the water carries every sound strangely, and the stone walls seem to hold their breath. He recognises the old sensation: the knowledge that courage cannot promise a safe return.

The engineers prepare the descent carefully. They explain the ropes, the air lines, the signals, and the limits beyond which the team will turn back. Beowulf dislikes the idea of a boundary being set by people who will not descend with him. Wiglaf points out that the boundary protects the whole party, including those waiting above. The exchange is not a quarrel about bravery. It is a disagreement about responsibility. Beowulf has spent much of his life proving that he can enter danger; Wiglaf asks whether the proof is worth anything if he makes others carry the consequences of his refusal to stop.

They descend only as far as the survey allows. Beowulf returns with a fragment of carved timber and a new respect for the quiet competence of the engineers. Later, in the Founders kitchen, he and Wiglaf share a late meal of stew, bread, and sharp cheese. Neither apologises for the conversation. They have learned something more useful than agreement: each can challenge the other without withdrawing from the company.

## The Dragon's Hoard and the Question of Inheritance

The dragon is not imported into the Tractus as a convenient beast for another contest. Its hoard is treated as the central problem of Beowulf's final reign: wealth gathered beyond the reach of ordinary exchange, guarded so fiercely that it becomes useless to the living, and disturbed by a small act whose consequences spread far beyond the person who first touched it. The Tractus scholars compare the hoard with buried royal stores, sealed archives, abandoned fortunes, and institutions that preserve resources while forgetting the people those resources were meant to sustain.

Beowulf understands the danger of a treasure that has no living purpose. He has seen gold pass from hand to hand as a sign of loyalty, a reward for courage, and a burden laid upon the dead. At the House of Small Accounts, he watches the keepers record a gift of timber for a family whose roof has failed. No name is carved into the beam. No debt is entered unless the giver and receiver both wish it. The wood is used, the family stays warm, and the account closes without anyone becoming the other's property.

He asks whether this is wealth. The keeper answers that it is wealth in motion. Beowulf thinks of the dragon's chamber, where treasure lay untouched beneath dust, and of the people who had once depended on a king's open hand. He does not become a convert to every custom of the Tractus. He remains a king from a world in which treasure, oath, and gift carry dangerous obligations. But he begins to distinguish inheritance from possession. A thing may be entrusted to the future without being locked away from it.

The old poem's final battle remains part of his story. The company does not pretend that Beowulf's death can be undone by a new setting. Instead the Tractus gives the surviving Geats a place to carry the memory without being trapped inside it. Wiglaf is not asked to become Beowulf. He is asked what the company needs now, what must be repaired, what must be returned, and what should be allowed to change. His answer begins with the ship.

## The Geatish House

A long, low building is eventually granted to the company near the river road, between the university workshops and the public gardens. It is called the Geatish House, though the name refers to a living fellowship rather than a museum. Its central room has a hearth, a long table, benches, shelves for maps and tools, and a wide door through which a damaged boat can be brought for repair. Smaller rooms hold sleeping quarters, a quiet library, a gear store, and a kitchen that the Geats gradually learn to share with visiting students.

The company keeps its customs but allows them to breathe. They still gather before a voyage, still speak the names of absent companions, still maintain their equipment, and still judge a promise by whether a man keeps it when nobody is watching. They also learn the Tractus customs of consent, privacy, and chosen accounting. A guest may sit at the table without swearing an oath. A disagreement need not become a feud. A man may leave the company for a season and return without being treated as a deserter. Their fellowship becomes stronger because its members can remain by choice.

Beowulf's place is marked by an empty chair near the hearth, but the chair is not kept as a shrine. It is used when a visitor needs a place, and every so often someone moves it closer to the fire. The company says that this is what he would have preferred: a seat that remains useful. On the wall hangs a copy of the first harbour chart, with the sea-road drawn in dark ink and the route inland marked in red. Beneath it is a short inscription in the Geatish hand, translated for the university visitors:

*Let the hall be remembered by the lives that return to it.*

The Geatish House becomes a place where the old heroic world can meet the new without either pretending to be the whole of history. Its men are not turned into mascots, soldiers, or relics. They are sailors, builders, teachers, friends, and keepers of a difficult inheritance. Their strength remains visible, but it is no longer the only thing the Tractus asks them to offer. The company has crossed the sea-road and found another kind of hall: one whose doors stay open because nobody inside is required to become a legend.
