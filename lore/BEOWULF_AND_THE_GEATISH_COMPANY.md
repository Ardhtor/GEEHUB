# BEOWULF AND THE GEATISH COMPANY

## The Sea-Road into the Tractus

The oldest account of Beowulf in the Tractus begins not with a monster, but with a shore. The sea is iron-grey beneath a low northern sky, and the men who come ashore have spent so many days listening to the hull complain against the waves that the silence of land feels at first like another kind of weather. They arrive as a company, not as scattered heroes: Beowulf at their head, broad-shouldered and watchful; Wulfgar, whose speech is measured; Wiglaf, younger than the others but already attentive to the weight of a promise; and the seasoned Geats whose names have been carried through the old poem in different degrees of clarity. They are adults, veterans of voyages and obligations, and each has a life that does not begin and end with the leader's reputation.

Their ship is drawn above the tide line and secured with ropes to a stone ring set into the harbour wall. The harbour-master expects a raid, a tribute demand, or a request for passage. Instead Beowulf asks where a tired crew can eat, where the horses can be watered, and whether there is a hall in which a foreign company may sit without being mistaken for an invading force. The answer takes them inland to Northgate, where the university's covered arcade meets the old road and the Long Table is already laying out supper. Thus the Geats enter the Tractus through hospitality before they enter it through legend.

The company is received with curiosity, not submission. Their armour is unfamiliar in its workmanship, their speech carries the salt and cadence of another age, and the old names in their account of kinship are known to the historians but not to the cooks serving them. The first meal is deliberately ordinary: thick barley bread, smoked fish, onions softened in butter, roasted roots, a pot of beans with herbs, and dark ale for those who choose it, with water and hot broth for the rest. Beowulf eats slowly. He notices who serves, who waits, and who sits down only after the visitors have taken their places. He has been welcomed into halls where generosity was a public performance and into halls where a guest's safety depended on the mood of the lord. Northgate's welcome is quieter. Nobody asks him to boast before he is fed.

## The Company at Northgate

The Geats are given rooms in the old Founders Dormitory while the harbour workshops examine their ship and the map library tries to establish the route by which they arrived. Their company keeps its own habits: weapons are cleaned and stored with care, boots are placed in a line near the door, and the men take turns checking the moorings and the condition of their equipment. Yet the dormitory changes the rhythm of their days. They discover hot water on demand, a kitchen in which anyone may prepare a meal, a repair bench that is open to students, and a noticeboard where a person can ask for help without declaring himself defeated.

Beowulf does not demand a private chamber or a ceremonial chair. He chooses a room near the stairs, where he can hear the company returning. Wiglaf takes the room across the passage. Wulfgar learns the kitchen's routine and becomes unexpectedly particular about the bread. One of the older Geats, whose hands are scarred from years of rope and iron, spends an afternoon with the workshop staff learning how their lifting frame works. Another discovers the print room and becomes absorbed by the strange permanence of ink on paper. Their names are entered into the university register as guests and companions, not as specimens. The archive records what they consent to share; it does not claim ownership of their memories.

The athletic commons draws the company in a different way. Its timber roof and high windows remind them of a hall, but the room is arranged for practice rather than display. Some of the Geats test their balance and strength against unfamiliar equipment. Others prefer wrestling drills, measured carries, or the patient work of repairing a damaged training frame. The university's military-like ensemble invites them to observe a formation exercise. Beowulf understands the discipline at once, but he is surprised by its purpose: the group is learning to move heavy materials through a crowded street without injuring bystanders, to raise a temporary bridge, and to coordinate a rescue when a river path floods. Strength is treated as a public capacity. The men are not being trained to find an enemy.

This becomes one of the first questions Beowulf asks the ensemble's captain. What is a strong man for, if not to stand between danger and those who cannot withstand it? The captain answers that protection is necessary, but that a man who only knows how to stand between two sides may begin to mistake every difference for a threat. Here, strength must also build, carry, repair, teach, and make room. Beowulf does not agree immediately. He keeps the answer with him.

## Heorot Remembered

The Tractus does not erase Heorot or reduce it to a story that can be corrected by a modern institution. The great hall remains in the Geats' memory with its gold fittings, benches, songs, boasts, quarrels, and long winter nights. Hrothgar's grief remains real. So does the terror of Grendel, whose visits turned the hall from a place of fellowship into a place men feared to enter after dark. Beowulf's victory is part of the history that brought him here, but the Tractus is interested in what the victory cost, what it repaired, and what it could not repair.

At Northgate's map library, a historian lays out a series of coastal charts and asks Beowulf to describe the approach to Heorot. The conversation becomes a reconstruction of sound as much as geography. Beowulf remembers the hall before the attacks: the scrape of benches, the smell of roasting meat, the harp, the clatter of cups, the small arguments that were forgotten by morning. He remembers the silence afterward. The historian records both, refusing to make the monster more vivid than the human life it interrupted.

A room in the university's winter garden is set aside for the account. It is not called the Grendel Room. The Geats choose the name Hall of Returning Voices, because the purpose is to preserve the people who gathered before fear narrowed their world. Their testimony includes the names of those who did not fight, those who kept the hearth, those who carried the wounded, and those who left. The record makes room for uncertainty. Where the old poem gives a name, the archive preserves it; where it does not, the record says so rather than inventing a life to fill the gap.

Beowulf is asked whether he would tell the story differently now. He says that a story told after victory is always in danger of making the danger seem necessary. He will not deny the fight, but neither will he allow the hall to be remembered only as the place where a monster came. This becomes a principle of the Tractus archive: preserve the deed, but also preserve the ordinary world that gave the deed its meaning.

## The Mere and the Measure of Courage

The story of the mere reaches the university through a different route. The river engineers hear of a submerged passage in the Geats' account and invite Beowulf, Wiglaf, and two of the older sailors to inspect a flooded chamber beneath the old quay. It is not the haunted mere of the poem, and nobody pretends that the two places are identical. Yet the descent unsettles Beowulf. The light thins, the water carries every sound strangely, and the stone walls seem to hold their breath. He recognises the old sensation: the knowledge that courage cannot promise a safe return.

The engineers prepare the descent carefully. They explain the ropes, the air lines, the signals, and the limits beyond which the team will turn back. Beowulf dislikes the idea of a boundary being set by people who will not descend with him. Wiglaf points out that the boundary protects the whole party, including those waiting above. The exchange is not a quarrel about bravery. It is a disagreement about responsibility. Beowulf has spent much of his life proving that he can enter danger; Wiglaf asks whether the proof is worth anything if he makes others carry the consequences of his refusal to stop.

They descend only as far as the survey allows. Beowulf returns with a fragment of carved timber and a new respect for the quiet competence of the engineers. Later, in the Founders kitchen, he and Wiglaf share a late meal of stew, bread, and sharp cheese. Neither apologises for the conversation. They have learned something more useful than agreement: each can challenge the other without withdrawing from the company.

## The Dragon's Hoard and the Question of Inheritance

The dragon is not imported into the Tractus as a convenient beast for another contest. Its hoard is treated as the central problem of Beowulf's final reign: wealth gathered beyond the reach of ordinary exchange, guarded so fiercely that it becomes useless to the living, and disturbed by a small act whose consequences spread far beyond the person who first touched it. The Tractus scholars compare the hoard with buried royal stores, sealed archives, abandoned fortunes, and institutions that preserve resources while forgetting the people those resources were meant to sustain.

Beowulf understands the danger of a treasure that has no living purpose. He has seen gold pass from hand to hand as a sign of loyalty, a reward for courage, and a burden laid upon the dead. At the House of Small Accounts, he watches the keepers record a gift of timber for a family whose roof has failed. No name is carved into the beam. No debt is entered unless the giver and receiver both wish it. The wood is used, the family stays warm, and the account closes without anyone becoming the other's property.

He asks whether this is wealth. The keeper answers that it is wealth in motion. Beowulf thinks of the dragon's chamber, where treasure lay untouched beneath dust, and of the people who had once depended on a king's open hand. He does not become a convert to every custom of the Tractus. He remains a king from a world in which treasure, oath, and gift carry dangerous obligations. But he begins to distinguish inheritance from possession. A thing may be entrusted to the future without being locked away from it.

The old poem's final battle remains part of his story. The company does not pretend that Beowulf's death can be undone by a new setting. Instead the Tractus gives the surviving Geats a place to carry the memory without being trapped inside it. Wiglaf is not asked to become Beowulf. He is asked what the company needs now, what must be repaired, what must be returned, and what should be allowed to change. His answer begins with the ship.

## The Geatish House

A long, low building is eventually granted to the company near the river road, between the university workshops and the public gardens. It is called the Geatish House, though the name refers to a living fellowship rather than a museum. Its central room has a hearth, a long table, benches, shelves for maps and tools, and a wide door through which a damaged boat can be brought for repair. Smaller rooms hold sleeping quarters, a quiet library, a gear store, and a kitchen that the Geats gradually learn to share with visiting students.

The company keeps its customs but allows them to breathe. They still gather before a voyage, still speak the names of absent companions, still maintain their equipment, and still judge a promise by whether a man keeps it when nobody is watching. They also learn the Tractus customs of consent, privacy, and chosen accounting. A guest may sit at the table without swearing an oath. A disagreement need not become a feud. A man may leave the company for a season and return without being treated as a deserter. Their fellowship becomes stronger because its members can remain by choice.

Beowulf's place is marked by an empty chair near the hearth, but the chair is not kept as a shrine. It is used when a visitor needs a place, and every so often someone moves it closer to the fire. The company says that this is what he would have preferred: a seat that remains useful. On the wall hangs a copy of the first harbour chart, with the sea-road drawn in dark ink and the route inland marked in red. Beneath it is a short inscription in the Geatish hand, translated for the university visitors:

*Let the hall be remembered by the lives that return to it.*

The Geatish House becomes a place where the old heroic world can meet the new without either pretending to be the whole of history. Its men are not turned into mascots, soldiers, or relics. They are sailors, builders, teachers, friends, and keepers of a difficult inheritance. Their strength remains visible, but it is no longer the only thing the Tractus asks them to offer. The company has crossed the sea-road and found another kind of hall: one whose doors stay open because nobody inside is required to become a legend.


## The First Winter at the Geatish House

The first winter does not begin with a ceremony. It begins with rain coming in beneath the western door and a disagreement about where the ship's spare timber should be stored. The Geats have been given a building, but a building is not yet a home. Its roof must be inspected, its hearth drawn properly, its drainage cleared, and its rooms made useful to people whose habits were formed aboard ships and in crowded halls. Wiglaf discovers that leadership consists, on most mornings, of hearing three reasonable objections before breakfast and finding a fourth solution that none of the three had considered. He asks the shipwrights to help raise a covered rack along the eastern wall, where oars, planks, and lengths of rope can dry without obstructing the passage. Wulfgar takes charge of the kitchen stores, not because he has been appointed steward, but because he is the first to notice that the men are eating well on the first day and poorly on the fourth. He makes a list of what is used, what is wasted, and what ought to be bought fresh at the market.

The company establishes a rhythm around the weather. At dawn, two men walk to the harbour to check the ship's lines; another pair sweep the yard and clear the drains. Those who have trained in the athletic commons return with new exercises for balance and controlled lifting, while the older sailors teach the university ensemble how to judge a load by the sound of its ropes. In the afternoon, the workshops are warm with the smell of cut oak, hot iron, and linseed oil. The Geats learn to use the university's lifting frame, and the makers learn why a ship's joints must flex without loosening. Their first collaboration is a simple repair cradle for the longboat, but the design is copied twice: once for the harbour, and once for the river rescue stores. The Geats are pleased that their knowledge has travelled beyond their own ship.

Wiglaf's bond with Diabound Kernel becomes part of this routine without becoming its centre. The creature roosts on the high platform beside the house, where the roofline breaks the wind and the river remains visible. It likes the shallow pool after rain and the polished brass fittings that the makers bring for inspection, though it is more interested in the movement of their hands than in the metal itself. Its serpent-tail often reaches down to examine a tool left on the ground, lifting it carefully and setting it nearer the person who dropped it. The first time this happens, a young workshop apprentice steps back in alarm. Wiglaf does not laugh at him or order him forward. He explains that the tail is curious, that the creature has not offered an invitation to touch it, and that the apprentice may simply leave the tool where it is. Diabound watches the exchange with its chest-eye dimmed to a soft blue-green glow, then settles its head against the edge of the platform.

The company learns the creature's preferences by repetition. It accepts grooming along the outer plates of its shoulder when it has lowered one wing and remained still; it refuses the same attention near the tail-head, which is permitted to inspect the brush but not to be handled. It enjoys having the scales around its forelimbs rinsed after a muddy walk, and it dislikes the smell of the strong resin used in the shipyard. The animal-care scholars record these observations only after Wiglaf has checked that they are accurate, and the record is kept beside the harness inspection notes rather than in a catalogue of tricks. Diabound's freedom is made practical: the roost has more than one exit, the flight path is never blocked, and no one closes the yard gate when the creature is away.

One evening, after the rain has passed and the clouds open above the river, the Geats hold their first full winter supper. Wulfgar has prepared smoked mackerel with mustard, a pot of barley and root vegetables, roasted onions, thick slices of bread, and a baked apple dish with honey for those who want it. The university ensemble brings a great pot of bean stew, and the makers arrive with a wheel of sharp cheese wrapped in cloth. The long table is crowded, but not arranged by rank. The older sailors sit beside students who have never been to sea; a map librarian finds himself explaining the old coast to a carpenter; two young members of the ensemble argue amiably with a Geat about whether a ship is best understood as a building that moves or a road that floats. The argument continues through the second helping and is settled only when the carpenter draws both ideas on the back of a menu.

Wiglaf speaks briefly after the meal. He does not imitate Beowulf's formal boasts, and he does not ask the company to promise that the house will remain unchanged. He says that the ship will be ready to sail again when the repairs are complete, and that no man will be required to remain merely because the others have chosen to stay. Some may travel, some may study, some may take work in the harbour, and some may return to the Geatish coast. The house will keep a place for them, but it will not turn a place into a chain. There is a silence after he finishes, not because the company doubts him, but because each man is considering what freedom sounds like when spoken by someone who has the authority to deny it. Wulfgar raises his cup first. The others follow, and the toast is to the house, the ship, and the road between them.

Later, Wiglaf walks alone to the roost. Diabound has folded its upper wings close and left the lower pair slightly open, making a sheltered space against the cold. The serpent-head is awake, its chin resting on the stone, while the main head follows Wiglaf's approach. He sits on the low wall and tells the creature that the ship will soon be repaired. He expects to take it downriver for a short journey, perhaps to the old ferry settlement, where a family has asked for help replacing a storm-damaged landing. He does not ask Diabound to come. After a while the creature rises, lowers one wing, and waits. Wiglaf smiles, but he does not climb aboard until the next morning, when the invitation is offered again.

The first winter changes the Geatish company in small, durable ways. Their tools acquire places on the wall; their names appear in the workshop rota; the kitchen begins to smell of food from two traditions; and the yard becomes a meeting point for people who have no reason to call themselves Geats. The old company remains recognisable. They still keep watch, maintain their ship, remember their dead, and speak plainly about courage. But the house has taught them that fellowship can survive a difference of purpose. Beowulf's empty chair is moved closer to the hearth when the weather turns cold. It is used by a visiting sailor who has nowhere else to sit, and nobody asks him to give an account of himself before the meal is served.

## The River Road Expedition

When the ship is finally seaworthy, Wiglaf does not take the company away at once. The first journey is made by river, with a small crew and a practical purpose. The old ferry settlement has lost part of its landing to the winter floods, and the university ensemble has offered timber, rope, and a lifting frame. The Geats bring their knowledge of water and current; the makers bring measurements; the local residents bring the memory of where the river rises when the snow melts. Diabound accompanies them only after choosing the route from the open meadow, following the river at a height that does not frighten the horses below. It lands beyond the settlement and walks the final stretch with its wings folded, allowing the children to watch from a distance without being asked to approach.

The work takes two days. The first is spent clearing broken boards and finding the old stone anchors beneath the mud. The second is spent raising the new landing, setting the posts, and testing the ropes under load. Wiglaf works beside the ferry keeper rather than directing from the bank. When a beam shifts unexpectedly, one of the Geats catches its lower end while the ensemble captain signals the others to release the line. Nobody is injured. The repair is not heroic in the old sense, but the ferry runs again before dusk, and a woman carrying baskets of winter greens crosses the river without having to wait for a boat to be hauled against the current.

On the return journey, the company stops at a low riverside inn. They eat freshwater fish, potatoes roasted in their skins, cabbage with caraway, and a dark loaf served with butter. The innkeeper asks Wiglaf whether he intends to become another Beowulf. Wiglaf answers that the world has already had Beowulf, and that the company needs a leader who can hear what the living are asking for now. The innkeeper considers this, then brings another loaf to the table. It is the first time Wiglaf notices that people have begun to ask him questions meant for a king. He does not know whether he wants the title. He knows that the landing is repaired, the ship is sound, and the men have returned together. For the moment, those are sufficient measures.

The expedition becomes the first entry in the Geatish House's open ledger. It records the materials used, the people who contributed, the repairs completed, and the knowledge that should be carried into the next project. It does not list the settlement as owing the Geats a favour. The ferry keeper contributes a winter store of smoked fish to the house, freely and without a tally. Wulfgar accepts it, writes down the date so the stores can be managed, and leaves the question of repayment untouched. The company has begun to understand the difference between remembering a gift and turning it into a debt.


## The Sea-Road Opens Again

By the time the first thaw reaches the river, the ship is ready to leave the harbour. The repair cradle has been dismantled and stored beneath the Geatish House, the seams have been checked, and the new landing ropes have been coiled in the hold. Wiglaf asks the company to meet at the long table before they decide whether to sail. He has learned not to mistake the readiness of a vessel for the readiness of the people aboard it. Some of the men want to return to the Geatish coast; others have found work and friendship in Northgate and would rather remain for another season. One has begun studying the river charts and wants to accompany the map librarians on a survey. Another has been invited to help the makers develop a stronger joint for the harbour cranes. None of these wishes is treated as disloyalty.

Wulfgar, the Danish herald who had travelled with the company after the events at Heorot, speaks first. He has never considered himself a Geat, and the distinction matters to him. His place at the table is not a courtesy that can be withdrawn when the company makes a decision. He has become a trusted interpreter between the customs of Heorot and Northgate, and he intends to visit the Danish coast before returning to Hrothgar's people. He proposes that the voyage should be a circuit rather than a departure: take the ship out, visit the old coastal settlements, carry letters and news, and return when the weather allows. The company accepts the proposal because it leaves room for different futures. The Geatish House will remain open, and the ship will become a route between it and the coast.

The departure morning is clear and cold. The harbour smells of wet rope, tar, salt, and bread from the ovens behind the quay. Wulfgar brings a packet of letters wrapped in waxed cloth. The shipwrights inspect the mast once more, while the ensemble captain checks the lifting gear and the emergency lines. Diabound Kernel waits in the meadow beyond the harbour, its wings folded and its chest-eye following the movement of the crew. It has not been asked to accompany the ship. When Wiglaf walks over to say goodbye, Diabound lowers its head and touches its brow briefly against his shoulder. It then turns toward the open sky. The gesture is an invitation, but Wiglaf does not assume that it is a promise. He tells the creature that the river road will remain clear, and boards the ship without asking it to follow.

The first days at sea are occupied by familiar work. The men trim the sail, inspect the lashings, share watches, and learn again how little a person can control once the coast has fallen below the horizon. The ship is not crowded with speeches. There is a rhythm to the deck: the creak of the mast, the slap of water against the hull, the rasp of a whetstone, the low voices of men handing over the watch. At night, the stars appear with a clarity that Northgate's lamps had hidden. Wiglaf finds himself looking for the river even though he knows it cannot be seen. He understands that a home can become a direction as much as a place.

On the fourth day they reach a small coastal settlement where the old sea wall has begun to collapse. The inhabitants have been stacking stones against the weakest section, but the winter storms have undermined its foundation. The Geats go ashore with the settlement's consent and inspect the wall. Their first instinct is to rebuild it as a defensive barrier; the local mason explains that the wall must also allow water to drain, or the next storm will destroy it from behind. The disagreement produces a useful design. The Geats contribute a method of setting heavy stones with timber levers; the mason contributes the drainage channels and the knowledge of the local tides. The wall is not made taller. It is made more intelligent.

Among the settlement's residents is a boatbuilder named Asta, whose workshop has been run by his family for three generations. He is an adult man, broad in the back and spare in his movements, with a habit of testing every piece of timber by tapping it twice with his knuckles. He recognises the ship's construction as an older Geatish pattern and asks permission to inspect the repaired keel. The conversation lasts most of the afternoon. Asta has been trying to build a lighter river craft that can be hauled across a gravel bank by two people, and the Geats show him how to distribute the weight without weakening the hull. In return he teaches them a local method of sealing seams with a mixture that remains flexible in cold weather. Neither side calls the exchange a lesson. They simply work until the light begins to fail.

That evening the settlement serves a meal in the boatbuilder's hall: mussels cooked with leeks, smoked haddock, oat cakes, pickled cabbage, and a thick broth made from the day's fish. The Geats bring dried apples and a small keg of Northgate's dark malt drink. Asta's sister plays a three-stringed instrument while the older men sing a work song whose refrain changes slightly from one verse to the next. Wulfgar recognises a phrase from a Danish harbour song and joins the final verse. The room discovers that the songs are related without being identical. The difference becomes the entertainment of the evening. Two men argue over the proper rhythm, then agree to teach each other both versions.

Asta asks Wiglaf whether the company will settle permanently in Northgate. Wiglaf says that the house is theirs to use, but not a place that requires them to stop travelling. Asta understands this better than Wiglaf expects. A boatbuilder's home, he says, is partly the workshop and partly every harbour where someone knows how to mend what has broken. He offers to visit Northgate when the lighter river craft is ready for its first trial. Wiglaf accepts, and the invitation is entered in the ship's log as a future visit, not a debt.

## The Men Who Stay

When the ship returns to the Tractus, the company does not return as a single block. Two Geats remain on the coast for a season to work with Asta. One comes back to Northgate with a new understanding of seam repair; the other stays because he has begun a life with a local man who shares his interest in boat design. Their relationship is not announced as a public event. They tell Wiglaf over breakfast, and he asks whether they want their names included in the house ledger. They choose to record the partnership as a household arrangement, with the details kept private. The company accepts this without teasing or ceremony. Later, Wulfgar leaves for the Danish coast with the letters he carried out, promising to return when his duties allow. He is not counted among the Geats, but his place at the table remains available.

The changes unsettle one of the older sailors, who had believed that the company should remain together in the form Beowulf left it. He says so one evening while the others are repairing nets in the yard. Wiglaf listens, then asks whether the man wants the company to stay together because he loves its fellowship or because he fears that it will disappear if its members choose different lives. The sailor does not answer immediately. He has spent years measuring loyalty by the willingness to remain at another man's side, and he has not yet learned to recognise loyalty in a letter sent from a distant harbour or a promise kept by someone who is not present. The conversation is left unfinished. The next morning, Wiglaf finds the sailor teaching a young Northgate student how to splice a rope. They do not discuss the argument again, but the student is invited to supper.

The Geatish House changes to accommodate the new arrangement. A room that had been used for spare equipment becomes a small chart room, with coastal maps, tide tables, and copies of the harbour surveys. The workshop bench is extended so visiting craftspeople can work beside the Geats without waiting for a place. A shelf is set aside for objects brought back from the coast: a piece of sea glass, a carved wooden gull, a coil of unfamiliar cord, and a small stone marked by the tide. Each object is labelled with its place of origin and the name of the person who brought it. Nothing is described as a relic unless its history is known.

Diabound returns to the house on the afternoon of the ship's arrival. It has been away for several days, and nobody knows where it went. Its scales carry a trace of pale salt, and the serpent-tail has a thin strand of river reed caught around one horn. Wiglaf does not ask where it has travelled. He offers a basin of fresh water and waits. Diabound drinks, lowers one wing, and allows him to inspect a small scrape along the outer edge of a forelimb. The animal-care scholar cleans it while Wiglaf keeps his hands visible and lets the creature decide how close he may stand. When the work is finished, Diabound takes the brass fitting from the repair bench and places it beside the harness. The makers laugh softly. The creature has remembered the work and returned to it in its own way.

The house's next gathering is smaller than the winter supper but more intimate. Asta has sent a sample of the flexible seam mixture, along with a note about the river craft. Wulfgar's first letter from the Danish coast has arrived. The two men working with Asta have sent word that they will stay until the lighter boat is ready. The older sailor who argued with Wiglaf has placed a new rope splice on the wall, with a note explaining the method to anyone who wants to learn it. The company eats roast chicken with barley, leeks, and parsley, followed by stewed pears. The meal is long, and the conversation moves between weather, tools, old stories, and the possibility of another voyage.

Wiglaf looks at the empty chair near the hearth. It is occupied tonight by a visiting carpenter who has come to ask for help with a roof beam. The chair is no longer a memorial that the company must protect from use. It has become a place that can receive another person without displacing the dead. Wiglaf thinks that this may be the truest form of inheritance they have found: not to preserve the exact shape of the company forever, but to keep its best habits available to those who come after it. In the morning he will answer Asta's letter, arrange the next ship inspection, and ask the map library whether the old sea-road can be charted alongside the new river passage. The work will continue, and the company will remain a company, though its members may now be found in more than one place.


## Asta's River Craft

Asta arrives at Northgate three weeks later, bringing his new river craft in sections aboard a coastal trader. The Geatish House yard becomes a workshop: planks are laid across trestles, the flexible seam mixture is warmed beside the hearth, and the makers check each joint against Asta's drawings. The boat is narrow, with a shallow draft and a raised bow that can pass over gravel bars. He names it *Morrow*, after his father's word for a vessel that has survived a difficult night. A boat, Asta says, should be named for what it has endured rather than what its owner expects it to become.

The trial is planned as useful work, not a public spectacle. The *Morrow* carries timber samples, tools, water, and provisions to the old ferry settlement, whose main road remains soft after the thaw. Wiglaf takes the steering oar, Asta watches the channel from the bow, and Eadric—the older sailor who once feared the company would dissolve—joins them as the third crewman. At the first bend, the craft passes over a gravel shelf that would have grounded the Geatish longboat. Asta calls the depth, Wiglaf adjusts the oar, and Eadric tightens the cargo lashings. Their movements are economical, the result of several weeks spent learning one another's habits.

At the settlement, the shallow craft unloads grain and a crate of winter tools directly onto the landing. Local boatmen inspect the seams and ask how the planks flex under load. Asta insists that they record the difficulties as carefully as the successful passages. A design that remembers its failures, he says, is more useful than one that remembers only its first day. The map librarians therefore mark the route as provisional and schedule tests in low water and after the next heavy rain. Wiglaf approves. A successful journey is evidence, not a guarantee.

That evening the Geatish House holds a work supper. Asta brings smoked eel and pickled samphire from the coast; Wulfgar's latest letter arrives from the Danish settlement, where he has delivered the messages and been asked to remain through summer to help settle a harbour-access dispute. He writes that Hrothgar's people remember the old company with affection. Wiglaf places the letter in the chart room beside the route records. It will be read by those who need to know, not displayed as a public announcement.

Eadric speaks after the meal. He has spent much of his life believing that a company survives only when every member remains within sight. He has begun to understand that a man can be absent and still keep faith, and that teaching a craft beyond the company can strengthen it. He does not pretend that he no longer misses the old arrangement. Wiglaf asks whether he will teach the new students his rope splice before the next voyage. Eadric agrees, on the condition that they learn to coil the rope properly before they touch the knife. The students arrive the following morning, and he makes them begin again whenever a coil twists.

## A Map with Two Kinds of Road

The map library lays out a new chart joining the coastal circuit to the river passages. Deep-water routes and shallow-craft routes are marked separately; uncertain sections remain shaded rather than completed by guesswork. Asta adds his workshop and the sea wall, the ferry keeper supplies measurements from the repaired landing, and the Geats who stayed on the coast contribute the names of coves where a vessel can shelter during a sudden change in weather. The chart records what has been travelled, what has been measured, and what remains unknown.

The question of access takes longer than the drawing. Some settlements want every copy shared; others prefer that sheltered anchorages remain known only to local crews. Wiglaf proposes that the public chart show established passages and places where assistance can be found, while detailed local knowledge remains with the communities that supplied it. Asta supports the distinction. A map can help a stranger reach a harbour, he says, but it should not make a small settlement responsible for every stranger who arrives without warning. The published chart carries the contributors' names and a clear account of its limits.

The *Morrow* receives a permanent berth beside the Geatish longboat. Neither replaces the other: the longboat is suited to open water and larger crews, while the smaller craft reaches tributaries and shallow landings. Together they make possible a seasonal service carrying tools, letters, food, and repair materials between the coast and the river settlements. It is not a fleet or a military force. It is a network maintained by people who know the water and are willing to share what they have learned.

Diabound Kernel watches the first departure from the meadow. It approaches the water, examines the new craft, and places one claw carefully on the bank beside its bow. Asta waits without approaching. After a moment the creature withdraws, turns toward Wiglaf, and lowers its wing. The invitation is for a flight, not a voyage. Wiglaf accepts after the *Morrow* has passed the first bend. From above, the new chart is visible as a chain of human decisions: a repaired landing, a workshop, a sheltered cove, a house with a lit window. The routes are not lines imposed upon the land. They are relationships made durable by repeated journeys.

At the Geatish House, the chart is hung beside the harbour drawing that marked the company's first arrival. One records the passage by which they came; the other records the passages they have made possible since. Beneath both, the empty chair remains in use. Asta sits there while writing to his family, and Eadric sets a cup of tea beside him without interrupting. The company is no longer held together by the expectation that every man must stand in the same room. It is held together by the knowledge that the road between them can be travelled again.


## The company after the voyage

The Geatish company has begun to discover that returning is a different task from arriving. The first voyage gave the men a shared direction; the settlements that followed have given them separate responsibilities. Asta now spends several mornings each week at the boatyard, where he checks timber deliveries and listens to the boatwright explain why a hull has begun to flex in a particular way. Wiglaf has become a reliable link between the harbour and the inland workshops. Eadric keeps the household accounts and has learned that a generous promise is only useful when the stores can support it. Their work is less dramatic than the voyage, but it is what allows the voyage to matter.

The Geatish House has changed with them. The long room still contains the old benches, but the wall beside the hearth now carries a working chart of the settlements and the routes between them. Notes are pinned beside the map: a landing that needs new steps, a mill waiting for a replacement gear, a family asking whether the riverboat can carry a crate of winter apples. The chart is revised after each journey. A route is marked reliable only after someone has travelled it in more than one kind of weather, and a new settlement is not treated as connected until there is a practical way to reach it and a person who can receive a visitor.

Diabound Kernel has become part of this pattern without being reduced to a transport service or a symbol. It chooses when to approach, when to fly and when to remain in the meadow. The company has learned to read its silences as carefully as its invitations. When the creature rests near the river, the men leave a clear path to the water. When it takes to the air, someone usually watches long enough to see which ridge it follows. Their relationship is made of repeated attention rather than command.

At supper, the conversation is no longer dominated by the events of the first arrival. The men discuss repairs, weather, the quality of the new barley, a dispute over a boundary hedge, and whether the southern landing should have a covered storehouse. Stories of the voyage still return, but each telling changes slightly as the company understands what came after it. Asta sometimes looks at the empty chair beneath the harbour drawing and remembers the people who have not yet made the journey. He writes to them with practical detail: how long the crossing takes, what the road is like beyond the landing, where a traveller can sleep, and who will be waiting.

The company is becoming a network without ceasing to be a company. Its strength lies not in keeping every member together, but in making separation survivable and return possible. The next chapter should follow one of these ordinary routes closely enough for the reader to feel its length: the preparation before departure, the changing river, the stop at a settlement, the meal offered by a household, the small repair that delays the boat, and the lamps at the Geatish House when the traveller finally comes home.
