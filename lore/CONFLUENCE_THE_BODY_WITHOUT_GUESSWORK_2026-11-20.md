# The Body, Without Guesswork
## A Common Table chapter

The conversation began as a joke and became useful only after the men stopped trying to win it.

The Common Table occupied the warm side of Exchange Hall, where the tall windows looked through the cedar arcade toward the sequoias. Late light crossed the long oak surface in broad bars. A few men had come straight from training; others were finishing shifts in the repair rooms or leaving the Institute library. Their ordinary presence mattered. Nobody had arrived for a formal lecture, and nobody had been appointed the authority on anybody else's body.

Tyler Boeskin had been turning the original noticeboard sentence over in his head. “I can never figure butthole stuff out,” he said, repeating it with the same bluntness that had made the first notice funny. “It sounds ridiculous until you realise half the room has probably wondered something similar and just never asked.”

Marcus leaned back. “Then ask the actual question. Not the euphemism. What are you trying to understand?”

That changed the temperature of the table. Daniel, a maintenance technician with a habit of checking every claim twice, began with anatomy rather than bravado. The anus was the external opening; the anal canal led inward; the rectum was the final section of the large intestine. The internal and external anal sphincters were distinct muscles, one operating largely without conscious control and the other under voluntary control. The pelvic floor formed a broader supporting group of muscles beneath the pelvis. They were related structures, he said, but not interchangeable names for one mysterious region.

A man across from him asked why the area could feel tight even when nothing was wrong. Daniel answered carefully: muscle tension, stress, posture, constipation, and straining could all affect how the pelvic floor and surrounding muscles felt. The sensation alone could not establish a diagnosis. Someone else mentioned that discomfort might come from irritated skin, hemorrhoids, or a small fissure, and that those conditions were not the same thing. The table began to distinguish pressure from burning, itching from pain, and a brief unfamiliar sensation from a symptom that persisted or worsened.

Tyler listened, then asked the question that had been hiding beneath the jokes: “How do you know when you're supposed to leave it alone and when you're supposed to get it checked?”

“Don't make yourself an amateur doctor,” Marcus said. “Pay attention, be sensible, and get help when there's a reason.” They named the warning signs without dramatizing them: persistent or recurrent bleeding, significant or worsening pain, a new lump, discharge, fever, or a change in bowel habits that did not settle. Severe pain or heavy bleeding called for prompt medical attention. The men agreed that embarrassment was a poor reason to delay care, and that a clinician had heard far more ordinary bodily questions than any person at the table could imagine.

The subject moved to hygiene. One of the trainers described the difference between cleaning and over-cleaning: gentle washing with water, avoiding harsh fragranced products, and not scrubbing delicate skin until it became irritated. Another man admitted that he had once treated every bit of discomfort as a cleanliness problem and had made it worse by repeatedly washing. That admission earned no ridicule. It made the conversation more practical. They discussed hydration, fibre, regular movement, responding to the body's signals, and avoiding prolonged straining on the toilet. None of it was presented as a universal cure; it was ordinary care, not a test of discipline.

Then the discussion reached intimacy, where the men became more deliberate. They talked about how different bodies have different comfort levels, how relaxation and communication matter, and why pain should not be treated as proof that someone is doing something correctly. Consent was not a vague atmosphere but an ongoing exchange: asking, listening, checking in, and stopping when someone wanted to stop. No one owed another person access to their body, an explanation for a boundary, or a performance of confidence. The men were specific about the principle without turning anyone at the table into an example.

Tyler looked down at his hands. “I think I thought everyone else just knew all this.”

“Most people know a few things,” Daniel said. “Then they fill the gaps with guesses.”

The line settled. It explained the noticeboard, the jokes, and the strange confidence with which men could discuss shoulders, chest measurements, training volume, and injuries while treating the pelvic floor as an unspeakable mystery. The problem was not that the body was inherently embarrassing. The problem was that silence made ordinary questions feel private in the wrong way: not personal, but forbidden.

The Institute of Becoming took the discussion forward without turning it into compulsory instruction. A small adult health series was added to the calendar under the title **The Body, Without Guesswork**. Its first session would cover pelvic anatomy, bowel function, common sources of irritation and pain, basic hygiene, signs that warrant medical attention, and the language of boundaries and communication. A clinician would lead the health portion; the Institute's facilitators would handle questions about consent and respectful discussion. Attendance would be voluntary, questions could be submitted anonymously, and no participant would be asked to disclose personal experience.

The noticeboard acquired a second line beneath the original: *Knowing your body is not the embarrassing part.*

By the following week, the Common Table had changed in a small but durable way. Men still joked there. They still argued about training, food, work, and who had left a wet towel in the changing room. But when a bodily question came up, someone was more likely to ask what was actually meant, and someone else was more likely to answer without pretending to know everything. The conversation had not made them experts. It had made them less willing to confuse confidence with knowledge.

The new workshop notice went up beside the timber-framed entrance to the Institute. Daniel checked the wording for accuracy, Marcus checked that the session was clearly marked for adults, and Tyler insisted that the anonymous question box be large enough to be useful. The box was made from cedar offcuts in the repair room. Its lid opened quietly, and its first card contained only six words:

*Is this normal, or should I ask?*

Nobody signed it. Nobody needed to.


## The second session: the body, without guesswork

The notice had been on the Common Table board for three days before anyone admitted that they had read it carefully. It was a plain sheet, pinned between a lost set of keys and an announcement for the next Open Works Weekend. At the top, in heavy lettering, it said **THE BODY, WITHOUT GUESSWORK**. Underneath were four lines: pelvic anatomy; ordinary bodily changes; hygiene and irritation; when to ask a clinician. At the bottom, someone had added, in smaller handwriting, *Questions can be written anonymously. Nobody has to tell a personal story.*

Marcus arrived early and found Daniel Roman arranging the chairs. The room had been set up like a workshop rather than a lecture: a broad table, anatomical diagrams, a clean model of the pelvic floor, paper, pens, and a box for anonymous questions. There was no examination equipment and no expectation that anyone would demonstrate anything. Daniel had insisted on that distinction. “We're here to understand the map,” he said, “not to inspect one another.”

Tyler came in carrying two coffees and looked at the diagram already propped against the wall. “I thought this was going to be a conversation,” he said. “It is,” Marcus replied. “The picture just means we can stop waving our hands around and pretending we know where everything is.” Tyler laughed, then moved closer. The diagram showed the pelvis from the side, with the bladder, rectum, pelvic floor, and surrounding muscles marked in separate colors. A second drawing showed external anatomy without exaggeration or comic labels. Its plainness made it easier to look at.

Daniel began with the part that had caused the most confusion at the previous gathering: the difference between the anus, the rectum, and the pelvic floor. The anus is the external opening; the rectum is the final section of the large intestine; the pelvic floor is a group of muscles and connective tissues that supports the pelvic organs and contributes to continence and other functions. The internal and external anal sphincters help regulate opening and closure. They are not one simple switch, and the surrounding muscles do not operate in isolation. “That,” Daniel said, tapping the diagram, “is already more useful than most of the advice you find in a locker room.”

One of the men asked why the area could feel tense even when nothing was happening. Daniel explained that pelvic-floor muscles, like other muscles, can remain contracted or become poorly coordinated. Stress, habit, pain, and other factors can contribute. Another participant added that straining during bowel movements could aggravate discomfort, and that hydration, dietary fiber, and avoiding prolonged straining were ordinary parts of bowel care. Nobody tried to turn the explanation into a diagnosis. The point was to understand what the structures did and to recognize the limits of a conversation around a table.

The anonymous questions made the discussion more concrete. One asked whether itching always meant poor hygiene. The answer was no: irritation can have many causes, including moisture, friction, skin conditions, infection, or products that are too harsh. Gentle external washing with water, careful drying, and avoiding perfumed products or aggressive scrubbing are usually better than trying to make the area feel sterile. Another question asked whether a small amount of discomfort after exercise was automatically serious. The group discussed how sweat, tight clothing, friction, and prolonged sitting can contribute to irritation, while emphasizing that persistent or worsening symptoms deserve proper assessment.

A third card asked about hemorrhoids. Marcus read it aloud without changing his voice. Daniel described them as swollen vascular structures in and around the lower rectum and anus, noting that they can cause itching, discomfort, swelling, or bright-red bleeding. He also explained that anal fissures are small tears in the lining and can cause sharp pain, particularly during or after a bowel movement. The two conditions are not interchangeable, and neither should be diagnosed by guessing from a single symptom. Persistent bleeding, significant pain, a new lump, discharge, fever, or a marked change in bowel habits should be discussed with a healthcare professional. Severe bleeding or severe pain calls for urgent medical attention.

Tyler had been quiet through most of this. At last he said, “I think the problem is that everyone wants a rule that covers every body. Then if their body doesn't follow the rule, they think they've done something wrong.” The room stayed quiet for a moment. Daniel nodded. Bodies vary. Symptoms have context. A diagram can explain anatomy, but it cannot tell a person what is happening inside them. The responsible next step is sometimes observation, sometimes a change in routine, and sometimes a clinician who can ask the right questions and perform an appropriate examination.

The conversation moved to language. The men practiced asking direct questions without turning them into jokes or confessions. “I've had pain in this area for a week; what should I do?” was clearer than a long story designed to hide the concern. “Can you explain what this examination involves before we start?” was a reasonable request. “I don't want to answer that in front of the group” was also a complete sentence. Consent applied to the workshop itself: no one had to disclose personal history, accept advice, be touched, or explain why they preferred to listen.

Marcus brought the discussion back to the title. “Without guesswork doesn't mean we become experts,” he said. “It means we stop pretending.” The distinction settled over the table. A man could know the names of the structures, understand common sources of irritation, and still need medical help. Another could be entirely comfortable discussing anatomy and still have no useful answer for a particular symptom. Neither confidence nor embarrassment changed the underlying facts.

After the session, the group carried the diagrams to the Institute's resource room. The staff member there agreed to keep a small reference shelf with medically reviewed material and a list of local clinical services. The next workshop would cover bowel health and common warning signs, with a clinician invited to answer general questions. A separate session on intimacy and communication would be planned with clear boundaries and adult-only attendance. The Institute would review the material before posting it, and the notice would state plainly that the series was educational, not a substitute for individual care.

Tyler stayed behind to stack the chairs. He looked again at the original notice and added a final line beneath the others: *You can be specific without making someone else uncomfortable.* Marcus read it, considered it, and added a second: *You can ask for help without having to earn it.*

By the following morning, the board had acquired a small crowd of handwritten questions. Some were about anatomy; some were about exercise, clothing, bowel habits, or symptoms people had been putting off discussing. The questions were folded and placed in the box. No names were requested. The Common Table continued around them: breakfast plates, a delivery trolley, a disagreement about the Open Works schedule, someone asking whether the repair library had a replacement hinge. The subject had not become the whole life of the place. It had become one more thing the men could handle openly, carefully, and without turning one another into a spectacle.

The Institute recorded the first session as complete, but the series remained open. Its next task was not to produce a room full of men who knew every answer. It was to make the next question easier to ask, and the answer easier to check.
