# Gods of Domination and Submission
## The First Testament — The Throne That Waited

In the first account, the god of domination arrived before the throne had been built. He stood in the empty court with a crown in one hand and a length of dark cloth in the other. His body was magnificent, his voice carried across the stone, and the men who watched him understood that he could command the court to fill itself with kneeling figures. He could have ordered every door shut. He could have made the lamps burn without fuel and the great doors move without hands. Nothing in the court possessed the strength to resist him.

He did not use that power.

The men waited for an explanation. The god set the crown on the unfinished seat and laid the cloth across its arm. He said that a throne was not a measure of how many bodies could be made to lower themselves. It was a measure of what a person was willing to entrust to the one who occupied it.

The keepers named him the Domitor, because they had no better word for the god who could command but did not command. He permitted the title, then corrected the record: "I am not the one who decides whether another man will submit. I am the one who must prove that a man's choice will remain his after he has made it."

A second god came to the court at dusk. He was broad and powerful, with a calm face and a heavy mantle fastened at the shoulder. He looked at the empty throne, then at the Domitor.

"Will you take the seat?" he asked.

"Not unless someone asks me to."

"Will you command?"

"Only where the command has been invited, and only within what has been agreed."

The second god studied him for a long time. "Then you have not yet learned domination."

The Domitor lowered his head. "Perhaps. Or perhaps you have mistaken restraint for weakness."

The second god did not answer. He placed his own mantle on the low bench and sat opposite the throne. He did not bow. He did not challenge. He simply remained.

The court came to call him the Voluntary One, though he rejected the title as soon as he heard it. He said that the name made his choice sound like a permanent condition. He had come to the court to test a particular form of trust, not to surrender his right to choose again.

The first trial began with a request. The Voluntary One asked the Domitor to direct the order of the evening: where the men would stand, which door would be used, who would speak first, and when the meeting would end. The request was clear, narrow, and temporary. The Domitor listened, repeated the terms, and asked whether the Voluntary One wished to add a boundary. The answer was that the Domitor could direct the ceremony but could not decide what the Voluntary One felt or what he would agree to tomorrow.

The Domitor accepted.

The court was arranged. The lamps were lit. The men took their places. The Domitor gave the instructions, and the Voluntary One followed them. Nothing in the trial required humiliation or force. Its difficulty lay elsewhere: each man had to remain attentive to the difference between an agreed role and an unlimited claim.

When the ceremony ended, the Domitor stepped away from the throne. The Voluntary One stood, took his mantle from the bench, and left the court through the open western door. The Domitor did not call him back.

The keepers expected the god to look disappointed. Instead he began dismantling the ceremony. He put the lamps away, folded the dark cloth, and carried the crown to the registry.

"Why are you returning it?" one keeper asked.

"Because a crown used for one evening is not a permanent office."

"Will he return?"

"I do not know."

"Do you want him to?"

"Yes."

The keeper waited.

The Domitor added, "Wanting is not the same as requiring."

The next day the Voluntary One returned. He did not sit where he had sat before. He stood beside the throne and asked the Domitor whether he would accept a different arrangement. This time, the Voluntary One would choose the place and the order, while the Domitor would agree to follow. The Domitor considered the request and accepted it. Their positions changed without changing their worth.

The court learned that authority could be exercised in more than one direction. A man might invite another to lead, then lead in return. A man might kneel in one ceremony and stand in the next. Neither action established a permanent rank. Trust was not the ability to predict what another person would do; it was the knowledge that an agreement could be spoken, revised, and ended without punishment.

The throne remained in the court, but it was no longer the centre of every gathering. Sometimes it was used. Sometimes it was covered. Sometimes the men met at the long table with no one seated above the others. The Domitor continued to possess his power. The Voluntary One continued to possess his choice. Neither god was diminished by the other's presence.

### The Second Testament — The Boundary of the Crown

In the second account, a younger god arrived and asked to be commanded without limit. He said that he wanted to be relieved of every decision. The Domitor listened, then refused the wording.

"Why?" the younger god asked. "You are the god of domination."

"I am not the god of taking a man's future away because he is tired of choosing today."

"I want to give it."

"Then we will begin with what you mean by that."

They sat at the long table and named the terms. They distinguished a chosen instruction from a permanent transfer of personhood, a temporary role from a fixed identity, and a permitted action from an assumption that permission would continue forever. The younger god could ask for structure, direction, and a defined period in which he would not be responsible for arranging the ceremony. He could also stop, change the terms, or return to the table.

The Domitor asked him to name the signal that would end the arrangement. The younger god did so. They wrote it down, checked that both understood it, and kept a copy with the ceremony record.

The trial proceeded. It was quiet. The younger god found relief in following a clear sequence, and the Domitor found that leadership demanded more attention than command alone. He had to observe the other man's condition, remember the stated limits, and refrain from treating compliance as proof that everything was well.

When the trial ended, the younger god asked to sit alone for a while. The Domitor left him the room.

Later, the younger god returned to the table. "I want to do it again," he said, "but not in the same way."

"Then it will be a new agreement."

The keepers wrote the principle beneath the second testament:

**Submission is a chosen act, not an identity assigned by another. Domination is a responsibility bounded by the terms of the one who invites it.**

The gods did not make this rule for every court or every relationship. They recorded it because the first court had discovered that power could only be trusted when it remained answerable to the people affected by it.

The crown was not destroyed. The throne was not abandoned. The court continued to explore authority, yielding, leadership, ritual, discipline, and return. But no one was required to remain in a role after the agreement had ended. The door remained open, and the registry preserved each version of the terms without replacing the earlier account.

The Domitor's final inscription was brief:

*The strongest command is the one that can be refused without fear.*

The Voluntary One added a second line:

*The deepest yielding is the one that leaves the self intact.*

Neither line was placed above the other.
