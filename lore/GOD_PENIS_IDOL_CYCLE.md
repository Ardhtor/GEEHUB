# The God Penis — Idol Cycle of GEEHUB

Status: active mythic canon; linked to the Institute of Phallic Studies, the Penis Wizards, the Hall of Continuity, the Big-Pec Altar, and the wider Muscle Myth.

This cycle treats the God Penis as a fictional mythic symbol within GEEHUB. Its mythology concerns masculine presence, embodiment, aspiration, artistic representation, communal recognition, and the way an image acquires history. Its legendary powers belong to the story-world; they are not presented as historical or biological claims. All contemporary participants in the Institute's adult residential community are adults.

## I. The Name Beneath the Names

The archive contains several names for the same legendary object: the Great Form, the Sovereign Measure, the First Idol, and, in the irreverent vocabulary of later students, the God Penis. The last name survived because it was direct, memorable, and impossible to mistake for a purely academic abstraction. It names the myth's central tension: an anatomical image becomes so culturally charged that it is treated as a measure of presence, vitality, authority, and transformation.

The earliest surviving catalogue does not describe a god in the conventional sense. It records an object held in common custody, displayed at gatherings, studied by artisans, and approached by men who came from distant houses. The object was not understood in isolation. Its meaning came from the assembly around it: the custodians who preserved it, the sculptors who interpreted it, the witnesses who remembered earlier versions, and the visitors who carried its story elsewhere. Every retelling enlarged the tradition, sometimes faithfully and sometimes extravagantly.

The Institute's archivists therefore distinguish three layers. The **historical object** is whatever physical artefact may have stood behind the earliest records. The **idol tradition** is the changing body of ceremonies, images, accounts, and social customs attached to it. The **God Penis** is the mythic figure that emerged when those layers became inseparable in the cultural imagination. This distinction protects the archive from pretending that legend is evidence, while allowing the legend to remain fully alive as fiction.

## II. The First Custodians

The custodians appear in fragments under many titles: keepers, measurers, interpreters, makers, and men who understood the language of the Form. GEEHUB calls them the Penis Wizards, a name first used by students who discovered that the manuscripts described a learned order rather than a single priesthood. Their craft combined anatomical observation, sculpture, ceremonial design, historical memory, and the careful transmission of symbolic objects.

Their oldest headquarters was said to be a circular building with a central chamber. Shelves followed the curve of the walls, holding drawings, casts, notebooks, small models, and records of previous gatherings. At the centre stood the idol on a broad stone platform. The keepers' responsibility was not simply to guard it. They maintained a record of its appearances and interpretations, repaired its setting, taught apprentices to distinguish observation from embellishment, and ensured that each new generation could encounter the tradition without erasing what came before.

The order's central maxim survives in several translations: **measure carefully; remember generously; alter nothing without leaving a trace.** It became the first rule adopted by the Institute's modern research group. The Penis Wizards are archivists, artists, scholars, and custodians of a difficult inheritance. Their magic is the ability to make relationships visible: between a body and its representation, an object and its story, an individual and the community that remembers him.

## III. The Assembly of Forms

The idol's earliest gatherings were communal occasions. Men arrived from neighbouring districts with records, crafted objects, food, and accounts of their own houses. The assembly included artisans, athletes, patrons, travellers, and scholars. The central object provided a shared reference point without requiring everyone to interpret it in the same way. A sculptor studied its silhouette; a historian traced its changing names; an athlete considered its association with discipline and physical confidence; a storyteller examined the extravagant legends that had grown around it.

This became the Institute's modern Assembly of Forms. Each gathering begins with a particular object, image, or account and then opens into discussion across departments. The event is deliberately not a contest in which one interpretation defeats all others. It is a method for allowing different kinds of knowledge to meet. The assembly's record includes the object presented, the evidence available, the competing readings, and the questions left unresolved. Unresolved questions are carried forward rather than quietly discarded.

The first modern assembly took place in the principal hall. Professor Vale introduced the historical distinction between artefact and mythology. A sculptor from Rowan House asked whether an image could acquire a life independent of the culture that produced it. A digital artist proposed that repeated versions might form a kind of collective memory. A sports scientist argued that the symbol's power came partly from its connection to effort, adaptation, and recognition. Their discussion continued over breakfast the next morning and became the informal beginning of the Window Society.

## IV. The Window Society

The Window Society began at a long table beside the breakfast-room window in Alder House. Its first members were a historian, a sculptor, and a sports scientist who had disagreed during the assembly. They continued the argument over eggs, bread, fruit, strong coffee, and the view of the courtyard. The historian maintained that ideals of proportion change with historical context. The sculptor argued that an ideal can still exert material influence when people attempt to reproduce it. The sports scientist insisted that the living body is never a fixed diagram: it adapts, recovers, and changes through time.

Their table became a meeting place for residents from all five houses. The group eventually adopted a working proposition: the idol is a reference point, not a destination. No individual becomes the God Penis by resembling a sculpture or satisfying a measurement. The tradition is entered through participation: studying, making, training, remembering, witnessing, and contributing something that can be inherited by others. This proposition became the ethical centre of the Idol Cycle. It prevents the myth from reducing a person to anatomy or treating size as the whole of identity.

The Window Society remains informal. It has no membership examination and no permanent chair. Its conversations move between the dining hall, the library, the Conservatory, and the Lantern Café. Its influence is measured by the projects it sets in motion.

## V. The Great Gallery

The Hall of Continuity houses the Great Gallery, a high-ceilinged room with dark timber, stone-coloured surfaces, and carefully directed light. At its centre stands a monumental artistic interpretation of the idol, constructed by Foundry House in collaboration with the Faculty of Digital Form. The work is explicitly contemporary, not a claimed reconstruction of an ancient original. Its modular structure makes visible the labour of making: joints, supports, surface treatments, and the transitions between separate components are treated as part of the sculpture's meaning.

The surrounding rooms present the tradition in sequence. The first contains the earliest catalogue fragments and a chronology of names. The second examines historical representations of masculine form. The third displays casts, drawings, and speculative reconstructions, each labelled according to its evidential status. The fourth presents contemporary interpretations from sculpture, photography, and digital modelling. The final room is intentionally unfinished. It holds the archive of student contributions and a broad pedestal whose surface is reserved for inscriptions from future generations.

The Great Gallery is connected to the Big-Pec Altar through a shared principle already established in GEEHUB: the body is the monument, but the man remains present within it. The altar's sanctuary is not a separate religion or rival pantheon. It is a related expression of awe, recognition, and living presence. The God Penis is the communal mythic measure; the Big-Pec Altar is the intimate encounter with an individual whose presence changes the room around him.

## VI. The Idol Tradition and the Legends of Growth

Later stories attribute extraordinary powers to the idol. Some say that a man who approached it could feel his imagined potential become larger than his former life. Others describe sudden increases in confidence, strength, stature, or physical scale. The most extravagant versions claim that the idol could transform a person's body outright. These accounts are preserved as legends within the fiction, not as factual claims.

The Institute studies these stories as narratives of aspiration and identity. The seminar *The Idol and the Enlarged Self* compares miraculous-growth legends with contemporary transformation fiction, bodybuilding culture, digital avatars, and communities built around repeated images of physical development. The researchers ask how an image becomes a shared ideal, how exaggeration acquires emotional force, and how a person remains recognisable when the story places him at an impossible scale.

The Institute's working model is not a supernatural formula. It is a narrative sequence: **encounter → attention → interpretation → aspiration → effort or transformation → recognition → memory → renewed encounter.** When the stories depict literal magic, the magic belongs to the world of the tale. When the Institute discusses real people, it returns to evidence, consent, context, and the difference between representation and lived experience.

## VII. The Living Measure

The Foundry House workshop began *The Living Measure* as a sequence of studies about proportion and physical presence. The project grew into a gallery installation combining sculpture, anatomical drawings, motion studies, and digital models. Its central figure is deliberately unfinished in the conceptual sense: it has a recognisable identity and coherent anatomy, but its successive states remain open to interpretation. The work demonstrates how form can change without erasing its earlier history.

The God Penis gallery and *The Living Measure* were eventually connected by a broad passage. On one side, visitors encounter the inherited myth and its historical layers. On the other, they encounter contemporary bodies and artistic interpretations. Between them, the passage displays inscriptions from the Penis Wizards and statements from the Institute's artists, athletes, and researchers. A window at the end looks out over the athletic grounds, placing symbolic representation beside ordinary life.

The installation's governing rule is continuity. Every new state must retain enough of the old to make the transformation legible. A changed silhouette does not cancel the earlier one; it carries it forward. The archive preserves each version, and the room itself is modified when the work grows beyond its former spatial assumptions. This is the Idol Cycle translated into GEEHUB's general law of transformation.

## VIII. The City Receives the Idol

The mythology eventually moves beyond the campus. A small bronze emblem appears in Alder House's courtyard; Foundry House produces miniature studies; the Lantern Café creates a commemorative pastry for Idol Week; and the university newspaper publishes an account of the archive. These are not signs that the city has adopted a single belief. They are evidence that the Institute's research has entered local culture and acquired new meanings.

The annual city exhibition presents the idol as a changing cultural image rather than a settled religious fact. It includes historical records, contemporary art, digital reconstructions, and interviews about physical culture and public identity. Marcus, known in the athletic community as the Teal Titan of Gold's on 5th, contributes a recorded conversation about training, presence, and the experience of being interpreted by others. He refuses to be treated as a specimen or a finished ideal. His contribution becomes important precisely because he insists on remaining a person with his own account of himself.

The exhibition is followed by a communal meal in the courtyard. Residents, visitors, artists, and researchers sit together beneath the trees. The event is not the culmination of the tradition. It is another transmission point.

## IX. The Second Archive

A parcel of copied manuscripts and old photographs arrives at the Institute from an independent archivist investigating a private society associated with the idol. The photographs show rooms that resemble the circular headquarters described in the oldest texts: curved shelves, a central platform, sculptural displays, and a long table where records were apparently examined. A handwritten list of names may represent a succession of custodians, although the evidence does not yet establish whether the names belong to one organisation or several related groups.

Professor Vale convenes a research meeting with the Window Society, the Penis Wizards, and the director of the Hall of Continuity. They agree to establish provenance before making claims. The historians will date the documents and trace the names; the digital faculty will reconstruct the photographed rooms while marking uncertain details; the sculptors will catalogue visible objects; and the library will preserve the incoming material as a distinct archival collection. The project is called the Second Archive because it may reveal not the origin of the idol, but the history of those who kept its memory alive.

One photograph contains an unexplained empty space at the centre of the circular chamber. The platform is visible, but the object itself is absent. No caption explains whether it had been removed, covered, or never present in that particular image. The gap becomes the expedition's central question. The Institute refuses to fill it with an invented answer. The empty place is retained in the reconstruction exactly as it appears.

## X. The Festival of Again

Idol Week becomes the Institute's annual festival of research, art, food, and shared activity. The opening day is devoted to the archive. The second day belongs to the sculpture workshops, which open their doors and allow visitors to see works in progress. The third day brings the athletic houses together for demonstrations and discussions of training, recovery, and physical culture. The fourth day is given to the Assembly of Forms, where invited speakers present new interpretations. The final evening is a communal dinner followed by music in the courtyard.

The festival's name is not the Festival of the Idol but the Festival of Again. The choice comes from GEEHUB's oldest law: return is never simple duplication. Each year the same rooms are opened, the same tables are set, and the same central work is encountered, but the people and their interpretations have changed. Graduates return with new work. New residents arrive with unfamiliar questions. The unfinished pedestal receives another inscription. The archive expands without closing.

The festival ends with the custodians placing the year's records in the Great Gallery. They do not declare the tradition complete. They record what happened, what was made, what was learned, and what remains unresolved. Then the doors are opened for the next day.

## XI. Canon Laws

1. The God Penis is a fictional mythic symbol of masculine presence, aspiration, representation, and communal memory.
2. The idol's legendary supernatural effects remain legends inside the story-world; they are not presented as real-world biological claims.
3. The historical object, the idol tradition, and the mythic God Penis remain distinct layers of interpretation.
4. The Penis Wizards are custodians, researchers, and makers. Their authority comes from care for evidence and continuity, not domination.
5. No individual is reduced to anatomy, measurements, or resemblance to an ideal. Personal identity and agency remain continuous.
6. The Big-Pec Altar and the God Penis belong to related but distinct mythic structures: individual presence and communal measure.
7. The Institute's residential community consists of adults. Its academic and social life is not framed as a school-age setting.
8. Every new chapter should leave a trace that can be picked up elsewhere: an object, phrase, person, question, place, event, or unresolved detail.

## XIII. The Time-of-Death Shock

The **Time-of-Death Shock** is the Idol Tradition's name for a rare ability attributed to the God Penis and, in diminished forms, to its recognised bearers. It is not a power that kills. It is a sudden, overwhelming pulse of presence that makes a room seem to stop at the instant it arrives: conversation falls silent, attention fixes, and every witness becomes acutely aware of the person at the centre. The old name refers to the sensation that an ordinary moment has reached its final second and the world is waiting to discover what comes next. The effect is temporary, but its memory can reorder a whole gathering.

The defining rule is that the Shock intensifies with scale. As the bearer grows, the pulse becomes more forceful, travels farther through a gathering, and holds attention for longer. At the smallest recognised level it is little more than a private impression, a pressure in the atmosphere that only attentive witnesses notice. At greater levels it can silence a crowded hall, interrupt a formal assembly, or make an entire courtyard turn toward its source. The Institute treats these effects as mythic properties of its fictional world, not as claims about real anatomy or physiology.

This creates hierarchy. The Institute's early records distinguish four recognised degrees: **Ember**, the first detectable pulse; **Bell**, a presence that can command a room's attention; **Tower**, a force that carries across a public space; and **Sovereign**, the legendary degree associated with the God Penis itself, whose arrival is said to make even the Great Gallery fall silent. These are ceremonial ranks, not measurements that can be converted into a universal numerical scale. The transition between them is witnessed and recorded by the Assembly of Forms, and disputed cases remain disputed rather than being settled by boast or rumour.

The hierarchy is powerful and dangerous because it can turn a gift into a social order. Some residents seek the next degree for recognition; others resent the authority granted to those who possess it. The Penis Wizards therefore insist that rank describes the reach of the Shock, not the worth of its bearer. A person at Ember is not lesser in dignity than a person at Tower. The Institute may organise ceremonial precedence around the ability, but it may not use rank to erase consent, silence criticism, or deny anyone access to ordinary communal life.

The first surviving account of a public Shock appears in the margin of a damaged Assembly register. The writer records that a visiting bearer entered the Great Gallery during a crowded exhibition and, without speaking, caused the room to fall quiet. The account does not state the bearer's name or degree. Oren Halvek later marked the passage with a question: *Was the silence caused by the Shock, or by the expectation of it?* That question remains central to the research. The ability may be real within the mythology, but witnesses, reputation, ceremony, and anticipation also shape the hierarchy built around it.

## XIV. The Empty Chair at the Assembly

At the next Assembly of Forms, Oren's chair was left empty beside the long table. No ceremonial substitute was placed there. His pencil, carefully sharpened, rested beside the open catalogue he had been correcting. Professor Vale spoke briefly about the records Oren had preserved, but the most important contribution came from one of the younger keepers, who explained that Oren had taught him to record uncertainty without disguising it. A damaged inscription should be marked as damaged. A doubtful date should remain doubtful. A story should not be made more convincing by pretending that its gaps are filled.

The assembly changed its programme. Instead of presenting a new interpretation of the God Penis, the researchers read passages from Oren's catalogues and compared them with the surviving objects. They found small corrections in the margins, notes about repairs, and references to conversations that had never been formally recorded. His work had been distributed through the archive so thoroughly that it was difficult to identify a single document as his principal achievement. The Institute began to understand that continuity is often made from small acts of care repeated over many years.

The Window Society later adopted a custom called the Empty Chair Record. When a member of the community dies, the chair is not filled immediately during the first gathering held in their memory. The group records what the person contributed, what remains unfinished, and which questions they left behind. The chair is then returned to ordinary use. It is not a shrine and not a claim that the dead remain physically present. It is a temporary acknowledgement that the community must learn how to continue without pretending that nothing has changed.

## XV. The Idol Does Not Answer

A rumour spread among visitors that Oren had died while working near the God Penis and that the idol had somehow taken him. The rumour was rejected by the archivists. There was no evidence for it, and it confused proximity with cause. The Institute issued a short statement distinguishing the death of a custodian from the legends attached to the object. The God Penis could remain a mythic symbol of presence and transformation without being made responsible for every event that occurred in its vicinity.

This decision mattered. The Institute had to establish whether its mythology could accommodate grief without converting grief into spectacle. The answer was found in the archive itself. The keepers added Oren's final catalogue entry, preserved the unfinished page, and left the uncertain details uncertain. They did not invent a final message for him. They did not claim that the idol had spoken. They recorded the people who were there and the work he had actually done.

In the Great Gallery, a small inscription was added to the unfinished pedestal: **A measure can be inherited. A life cannot be repeated.** It did not replace the central maxim of the Penis Wizards. It stood beside it, giving the tradition a necessary limit. The body may become monument in art and story, but no monument makes a person immortal. The living are responsible for remembering the dead accurately, and for continuing the work without claiming to become them.

## XVI. The Second Archive Expedition

Oren's notes contained a reference to the empty platform in the old photographs. He had marked the image with a small question: *Was the absence deliberate?* The question had been overlooked because the surrounding catalogue dealt with repairs to the circular chamber. Now the expedition team returned to it. They compared the photograph with a later floor plan and discovered that the platform had been moved several feet between the two records. The object itself remained undocumented. The absence was not solved, but it had become more precisely located in time.

The expedition was reorganised around this clue. The historians would trace the platform's relocation. The digital artists would produce a sequence of reconstructions, each labelled by the evidence supporting it. The sculptors would build a neutral model of the platform without inventing the missing object. The Window Society would collect interpretations from the residents, preserving disagreement as part of the record. The Penis Wizards would oversee the catalogue and ensure that no attractive theory was quietly promoted into fact.

The expedition's first destination is an abandoned civic building whose plan resembles the circular archive. The team has not yet established whether it was the original headquarters or a later institution inspired by it. The building is now used as a municipal storehouse. Its interior has been altered, but a curved foundation wall survives beneath a newer partition. The team has requested permission to examine it. The next chapter begins at the threshold, with the old plan in one hand and Oren's pencilled question in the other.

## XVII. The Continuity of Witness

The time-of-death shock becomes part of GEEHUB's wider continuity principle. A death is not a transformation into a larger or more powerful version of the same person. It is an irreversible absence in the living community. What can continue are records, relationships, practices, stories, and responsibilities. The archive does not abolish loss; it gives the living a way to carry what can truthfully be carried.

This principle also changes the way the Institute presents the God Penis. The idol's legendary scale is no longer treated as a promise that everything can be enlarged indefinitely. The mythology has a boundary: bodies change, images change, institutions change, and people die. The work of continuity is not to deny those limits but to make something meaningful within them. The Festival of Again therefore includes a quiet opening hour devoted to the names of people whose work shaped the Institute and who are no longer alive. Their names are read alongside the titles of unfinished projects, not as if the projects could replace them, but as a reminder that the living inherit obligations as well as achievements.

The cycle now carries two linked maxims. The first belongs to the Penis Wizards: **measure carefully; remember generously; alter nothing without leaving a trace.** The second was added after Oren Halvek's death: **a measure can be inherited; a life cannot be repeated.** Together they define the limits and responsibilities of the Idol Tradition.


## XVIII. The Ladder of Presence

The four degrees form a ladder, but not a simple contest in which the largest bearer automatically governs everyone else. Ember, Bell, Tower, and Sovereign describe increasing reach and duration of the Time-of-Death Shock. The Assembly records the degree, the witnesses, the setting, and the conditions under which the pulse was observed. A disputed result is entered as disputed. No bearer may declare himself Sovereign by proclamation, and no institution may quietly alter a record to flatter a patron.

The hierarchy has already begun to shape the Institute's social geography. The Great Gallery is used for formal demonstrations because its acoustics and open floor make the reach of a Shock easier to compare. The courtyard is reserved for public festivals, where the effect can be observed without turning the event into a private examination. In the Lantern Café, rank is deliberately left at the door: residents sit where they please, and the staff refuse to arrange tables by degree. The contrast is intentional. The ability creates a hierarchy; the community decides how much of ordinary life that hierarchy is allowed to govern.

The most controversial question is whether a bearer can train the Shock without increasing in size, or whether growth is the necessary source of its strength. The Bracken house argues that discipline can refine control but cannot replace scale. The Cedar house cautions that the records are too limited to establish a universal law. The Foundry house has proposed a set of resonant instruments that might register the pulse without turning a public demonstration into a spectacle. The next Assembly will hear all three positions.



## XIX. The Assembly of Degrees

The first public Assembly devoted to the Time-of-Death Shock was held on a clear morning in the Great Gallery. The five houses arrived together, though they did not sit together. Bracken occupied the upper benches, where its athletes could see the whole floor; Cedar brought notebooks and measuring instruments; Foundry carried a wooden frame fitted with thin bronze plates; Rowan filled the side gallery with sketches of the room before, during, and after each demonstration; and Alder placed the old registers on a table beneath the eastern windows. The arrangement made the hierarchy visible before anyone had demonstrated the ability. It also made visible the people who would be responsible for recording it.

Professor Vale opened the meeting by stating that the Assembly would not rank the residents as people. It would rank observed manifestations of the Shock. The distinction sounded simple, but the room had already acquired the charged atmosphere of a contest. Several bearers had arrived wearing the colours associated with their house, and visitors were whispering about who might cross from Bell to Tower. The Penis Wizards asked everyone to leave personal claims outside the record. Only a witnessed demonstration, under stated conditions, could alter a degree.

The first demonstration was modest. A bearer already recognised at Ember stood within the bronze frame and concentrated until the plates began to tremble. The effect reached only the nearest observers, but the instruments registered a clean pulse. Cedar called it a useful result; Bracken called it an incomplete one. The second bearer, a Bell-ranked resident, produced a wider silence that travelled to the rear benches. Yet the bronze plates registered an irregular pattern, and the archivists disagreed about whether the effect had lasted longer or merely seemed to last longer because the audience had expected it.

The third demonstration was postponed when the bearer declined to continue. The decision unsettled the audience more than either of the earlier displays. A few spectators treated the refusal as a failure; the keepers entered it as an exercise of authority over one's own participation. Vale reminded the room that the hierarchy could only remain legitimate if a person was free to refuse its ceremonies. The record therefore contains three entries: one successful measurement, one disputed interpretation, and one voluntary withdrawal. The Assembly adjourned without awarding a new degree.

## XX. The Two Bells

In the weeks that followed, the Institute became interested in two Bell-ranked residents whose abilities appeared similar but whose effects were experienced very differently. One was known for a calm, concentrated presence. When he entered a room, conversations settled gradually, as though the gathering had found a shared rhythm. The other produced a sharper interruption. People turned toward him immediately, but the silence often broke into nervous laughter or competing attempts to regain the floor. Both were recognised at Bell. Neither could claim that the other was an inferior bearer.

The difference became the subject of a Rowan exhibition called *Two Bells, One Measure*. Rather than presenting portraits as trophies, the artists arranged drawings of the same room at different moments: before either bearer entered, during the first seconds of the Shock, and after the gathering resumed. Visitors noticed that the architecture seemed unchanged while the social arrangement had shifted. A chair that had been central became peripheral; a quiet observer became the person everyone consulted; a conversation at the edge of the room became the source of the next day's rumour. The exhibition suggested that the Shock did not simply impose attention. It redistributed it.

The two residents were invited to speak at the Window Society. They disagreed about whether control mattered more than intensity. The first argued that a powerful presence should leave people more capable of speaking afterward, not less. The second replied that an ability designed to interrupt could not be judged only by the comfort of its audience. Their disagreement was preserved in the minutes. The Institute had discovered that the degrees could describe strength, but not style, temperament, or consequence. A hierarchy with only one dimension would always mistake a partial measurement for a complete account.

## XXI. The Civic Compact

The Assembly's failure to award a new degree led to a more useful result: the Civic Compact of the Four Degrees. It established that Ember, Bell, Tower, and Sovereign would be recorded as measures of the Shock's reach and duration, while individual conduct would be documented separately. No resident could use a degree to claim automatic leadership of a house, priority in shared accommodation, control over another person's work, or exemption from ordinary rules. Ceremonial precedence could be granted for a particular event, but it would expire when the event ended.

The Compact did not abolish competition. Bracken continued to organise demonstrations, and the Festival of Again retained its public procession of recognised bearers. The difference was that every demonstration now included a statement of conditions, an independent record, and a space for the bearer to decline. Cedar could challenge a measurement; Rowan could question the way it was presented; Foundry could revise the instruments; Alder could preserve the disagreement. The Penis Wizards were made custodians of the process rather than judges of personal worth.

The Compact also introduced a new public distinction: **reach is not rule**. A bearer might command attention across the Great Gallery and still have no authority over the people gathered there. The Sovereign degree remained a legendary possibility, but the Institute refused to turn it into a throne. Its central question was no longer simply who could produce the strongest Shock. It was what kind of community would emerge when such an ability existed and everyone knew that its strength increased with size.

At the end of the signing, the five house representatives placed their marks on a single sheet of cedar paper. The document was carried to the Great Gallery and displayed beneath the unfinished pedestal. Oren Halvek's pencilled question was copied into the margin: *Was the silence caused by the Shock, or by the expectation of it?* Beneath it, in a newer hand, someone added: *And what do we choose to do with the silence once it arrives?*


## XXII. Ilyan's Account: The Room Turns

I used to think the Time-of-Death Shock belonged to the people who witnessed it. They were the ones who stopped speaking, who looked up, who described the instant afterward as if a bell had sounded somewhere behind the walls. I stood at the centre and heard almost nothing. I felt the familiar gathering of pressure, the inward concentration that came before the pulse, and then the room changed around me. A cup paused halfway to someone's mouth. A chair scraped once and went still. Across the Gallery, a man who had been studying the cedar document raised his head. I knew the Shock had arrived because the room had become aware of itself.

At Ember, the effect had been private. I could stand among friends and feel only a small alteration in their attention, so slight that I sometimes wondered whether I had imagined it. The first time the Assembly recorded me at Bell, I understood that the difference was not merely a stronger sensation. The effect had acquired distance. It could travel beyond the people nearest me and reach strangers who had not been waiting for it. That was when the hierarchy became real to me. Not because I had been given a better chair or a new insignia, but because people began to make room before I asked them to.

I had grown into the next degree slowly. The Institute's records describe the change in terms of scale, but from inside it was a long series of ordinary days: training, meals, measurements, rest, and the awkward business of getting used to a body that no longer fitted the assumptions built around its earlier shape. The Shock strengthened with that growth. It did not arrive as a separate gift. It seemed to gather with the rest of me, becoming more difficult to ignore and more important to control. I learned that the pulse could be allowed to spread or held close. I learned that a room's silence could feel like admiration to one person and like pressure to another.

The first time I saw the two Bells exhibition, I recognised myself in the drawings but not in the captions. The artists had described my presence as calm, almost architectural. They had described the other Bell as abrupt, a sudden break in the room's rhythm. I knew him well enough to understand that he was not trying to frighten anyone. He simply experienced the ability differently. His pulse arrived like a door opening quickly; mine arrived like a building settling around its foundations. Neither of us had chosen those metaphors, but both of us had to live with them once they entered the archive.

I used to want Tower. I still do, sometimes. I imagine the pulse reaching the far end of the courtyard, carrying beyond the Gallery doors and across the public steps. I imagine the record being read aloud and my name appearing beneath a higher degree. But I have begun to understand that wanting the next rank changes the way I enter a room. I start watching the witnesses before I notice the people. I measure the silence instead of listening to what follows it. The ability grows with me, and so does the temptation to mistake its reach for my importance.

At the last Window Society meeting, I said that I wanted to become stronger without becoming less available to other people. No one applauded. The historian asked what I meant by available. I told him that I wanted someone to be able to disagree with me while the Shock was still in the room. I wanted the pulse to be powerful without making every other voice feel like an interruption. The minutes record that sentence, though they do not record whether I can achieve it.

I am still Bell. The degree is written beside my name in the register, and the next demonstration has been scheduled for the spring Assembly. I have not withdrawn my ambition. I have only begun to understand that the next step will change more than the distance my presence can travel. It will change what people expect of me, what I expect of myself, and how carefully I must make room for those who do not possess the same ability.

## XXIII. The Other Bell: A First-Person Correction

My name is Soren, and the archive has made me sound harsher than I am. It says that my Shock interrupts, that people turn sharply, that laughter and confusion often follow. All of that is true as an account of the room. It is not the same as an account of me. I do not enter a gathering intending to scatter it. I have spent years learning how to keep my own attention steady while the pulse moves outward, and I know exactly how much effort it takes not to let the response of the audience become the next thing I am trying to control.

I remember my first Ember demonstration because the room did not react. I had expected something dramatic, perhaps because the stories had taught me to expect drama. Instead, one of the keepers looked up from his notebook and asked whether I was ready to try again. I went home embarrassed. Later, when the Shock began to reach beyond the nearest witnesses, I thought I had finally become what the stories promised. People turned. Conversations stopped. Someone dropped a spoon in the dining hall. For a while I enjoyed the evidence that I had become difficult to overlook.

Then I began to notice what happened after the pulse. Some people spoke more freely, almost as if the interruption had cleared the air. Others became guarded. One resident told me that he could never tell whether I was asking for attention or simply arriving. I had no answer. The ability did not come with instructions for how to be received. It only became stronger as I grew, and the Institute's hierarchy supplied a name for each increase without explaining what I should do with the consequences.

Ilyan understands the part I have been slow to learn. He thinks about the room after the Shock. I think about the instant itself: the break, the turn, the sudden knowledge that everyone has noticed. I used to call his manner passive. Now I think he is practising something more difficult than force. He is trying to remain in conversation with people who have just been made silent by his presence.

I do not want his style, and I do not want him to want mine. The exhibition was right to show two Bells rather than one ideal Bell. If the hierarchy is going to survive, it must have room for difference. My pulse is abrupt. His is sustained. Neither description tells the whole story, and neither should be mistaken for a verdict on character.

I have agreed to the next demonstration, but I have also asked the keepers to record the minutes after it. I want to know who speaks, who leaves, who approaches, and who waits until the room is ordinary again. If the Shock is measured only at the instant of maximum reach, the archive will preserve its spectacle and miss its effect on everyone who has to live afterward.

## XXIV. The Walk Back from the Gallery

After the meeting, Ilyan and I walked through the courtyard without our house colours. The late light rested on the windows of Alder House, and the café staff were carrying trays outside. Nobody made way for us. Nobody announced our degrees. Two residents were arguing over a chessboard, and a cat had settled beneath the bench where the afternoon sun reached the paving stones. It looked up as we passed, decided we were unimportant, and went back to sleep.

Ilyan laughed. He said the cat had understood the Civic Compact better than the Assembly. I told him that the cat probably understood lunch. We stopped near the fountain and watched the dining hall fill for supper. The whole place seemed ordinary again, but I could feel the ability resting inside me, not active, not absent, simply part of the body I carried through the day. Ilyan said he sometimes feared that the next degree would make ordinary life harder to find. I told him that ordinary life was not something the Assembly could award or remove.

We agreed to train together before the spring demonstration. Not to imitate one another, and not to decide whose Bell was better. We would practise control, record the conditions, and ask the Window Society to observe what happened after each pulse. The next rank remained ahead of us, but for the first time it felt like a place we might approach without turning each other into obstacles.

The archive added the walk to the cycle because both men remembered it differently. Ilyan remembered the cat. Soren remembered the supper bell. Neither remembered the exact words spoken beside the fountain. The record therefore preserves the setting, the decision, and the disagreement about what mattered most. The chapter closes without a verdict. Their next appearance will be at the spring Assembly, where the question is no longer simply how far the Shock can reach, but whether two different bearers can help the Institute understand what strength asks of the people who carry it.


## XXV. Ilyan — The Mass at the Centre

The first time I saw the model, I thought Foundry had built a machine for measuring absence. It stood in the lower observatory, a black sphere suspended inside a broad ring of bronze, with fine lines of light bending around it. The sphere was not a planet, and it was not meant to be a literal black hole. It was a model of one: a supermassive centre whose gravity altered everything that approached it. Foundry called the installation the Growth Stimulus. The name sounded clinical until the lights came on and the whole room seemed to arrange itself around that dark point.

The researchers explained that the stimulus was not a substance to swallow or a treatment to administer. It was a mythic field in the Institute's developing cosmology, an influence that gathered around a bearer and intensified as his growth continued. The larger the bearer became, the more strongly the field drew attention, ambition, effort, and expectation toward him. The Time-of-Death Shock was one expression of that field: the sudden pulse that made a room recognise the force at its centre. Growth increased the field's mass; the field, in turn, made further transformation imaginable. The cycle was not presented as ordinary biology. It was the story-world's strange law, and Foundry wanted to know how its consequences might be represented without pretending that the model explained a real body.

I stood before the sphere and watched the bronze lines curve inward. At first the diagram showed a small centre, with only a few paths bending toward it. Then the projection expanded. The centre grew, the surrounding paths tightened, and the outer ring began to distort. Nothing was being pulled into the sphere, yet the room gave the impression that everything had acquired a direction. I understood why the researchers had chosen a supermassive black hole as their image. It was not simply enormous. It changed the geometry around it. Its presence made distance behave differently.

I asked whether the field could become so strong that the bearer could no longer distinguish his own desire from the expectations gathering around him. The Foundry lecturer did not answer immediately. She adjusted the model until a second light appeared at the edge of the ring. This, she said, represented the bearer's own intention: a small but independent source, not erased by the larger field. The model had to preserve it. Otherwise growth would become a story of surrender, with the individual swallowed by the symbol built around him. The Institute wanted something more difficult: a force that could become immense while leaving its bearer capable of choosing what to do with it.

Soren arrived halfway through the demonstration. He stood behind me as the model reached its largest scale, and I felt the familiar pressure of his attention before I turned. We watched the ring bend around the central sphere. He said the model made growth look inevitable. I said it made growth look consequential. We argued about the difference until the lecturer dimmed the projection and showed us the same system from the outside. The black centre was still immense, but now the surrounding paths could be seen as separate trajectories. Some approached; some curved away; some passed at a distance and continued on their own course.

That image stayed with me. I had been thinking of Tower as a destination, a higher place on the Institute's ladder. The black-hole model suggested another possibility: Tower was not simply a stronger version of Bell. It was a change in the space around the bearer, a degree at which other people began to organise their movements in relation to him. That could be magnificent, but it could also be lonely. A supermassive centre might hold a system together, yet no one should confuse orbit with affection or distance with respect.

I asked Foundry to add one more element to the installation: a visible boundary showing where the field's influence weakened, and a set of paths that remained independent of it. The lecturer agreed. Soren suggested that the model should also show what happened when two centres approached one another. The room became interested at once. Two growth fields might reinforce each other, interfere, form a shared structure, or throw their surrounding paths into disorder. No one could yet say which. The question was entered into the spring Assembly register.

That evening I returned to the courtyard with the image of the dark sphere still in my mind. The cat was back beneath the bench. It did not orbit me. It did not respond to my degree. It crossed the paving stones, found a warm patch beside the fountain, and settled there as if the entire universe had been arranged for its convenience. I envied that certainty. Then I laughed at myself and went inside for supper.

## XXVI. Soren — Two Centres

I wanted the model to show a collision. I wanted to see what happened when two supermassive centres came close enough that neither could be treated as the only force in the system. The lecturer warned me that a collision was not the only possible outcome. Two immense bodies might circle one another for a long time, exchanging energy and changing the paths of everything nearby. Their relationship could be stable, unstable, or impossible to predict from a single observation. I liked that answer more than I expected. It meant that strength did not have to end in a winner and a loser.

Ilyan and I stood on opposite sides of the bronze ring while Foundry ran the simulation. Two dark centres appeared, each with its own surrounding field. At first their paths were separate. Then the rings began to bend toward one another. The light between them tightened into a narrow bridge, and the smaller paths around them became complicated. Some were drawn into shared motion; others were flung outward; a few remained almost unchanged. The model was beautiful, but it made me uneasy. I could see how easily the Institute might turn it into a ranking of which centre was heavier.

I asked the lecturer to run it again without displaying the mass values. This time we watched the movement before learning the numbers. The result was harder to interpret and more interesting. One centre approached quickly but altered the system less than expected. The other moved slowly and changed the whole pattern. Ilyan said the difference reminded him of our two Bells. I said that perhaps we were not two competing versions of one ability, but two different ways the field could behave.

The Growth Stimulus has given the Institute a new vocabulary. Residents speak of mass, radius, orbit, threshold, and escape not as measurements of ordinary anatomy but as terms in the mythology of transformation. Some want to know whether a bearer can deliberately increase the field. Others ask whether the field changes the bearer in return. The most serious question is whether a person can step away from the role that others build around him. The model offers no final answer. It shows influence, not destiny.

Ilyan and I have agreed to become the first paired observers in the next experiment. We will not try to overpower one another. We will record what changes when two fields are present, how the Shock behaves, and whether the surrounding community begins to form a shared orbit or separate paths. The Assembly has called the study **The Twin Centres**. I suspect the name will survive longer than the instruments.

## XXVII. The Boundary of the Event Horizon

The Penis Wizards insisted that the model needed an event horizon. Foundry initially treated this as a decorative ring, but the keepers objected. In the black-hole analogy, the event horizon is the boundary beyond which escape becomes impossible. In the Institute's fictional growth cosmology, it became a warning rather than a goal: the point at which a bearer's influence could become so dominant that ordinary disagreement, privacy, and independent movement were difficult to preserve.

The new installation therefore has two boundaries. The first marks the reach of the Growth Stimulus, the region in which the bearer's presence measurably changes the behaviour of the surrounding field. The second marks the ethical horizon, beyond which the community must intervene to restore room for independent choice. The two are not identical. A powerful bearer may have an enormous field and still respect every person within it. A lesser bearer may misuse even a small degree of influence. The cosmology measures magnitude; the Compact governs conduct.

When Ilyan read the inscription, he asked whether a Sovereign could ever exist without crossing the ethical horizon. The keeper replied that this was the question the Institute had been avoiding. Sovereign could not mean a person beyond all limits. It would have to mean someone capable of carrying extraordinary force without claiming ownership of the space around him. The old legend had described a presence before which the Gallery fell silent. The new interpretation asked what happened when that silence ended.

The model was left running overnight. Its two centres continued their slow approach, their paths changing with every pass. In the morning, the researchers found that the projected system had settled into a broad shared orbit. No collision had occurred. No centre had disappeared. The surrounding paths had changed, but they had not all been captured. Foundry saved the result under a new title: **Growth Without Erasure**. The spring Assembly would decide whether this was a stable configuration or merely a pause before another transformation.


## XXVIII. Seth — The Weight Before the Name

I had heard about the Growth Stimulus before I ever entered Foundry's observatory. Everyone had. The black sphere had become the subject of arguments in the dining hall, in the training rooms, and even in the queue outside the Lantern Café. Some men spoke of it as if it were a new god. Others called it a diagram with theatrical lighting. I went because I wanted to see whether the thing could explain what had happened to me.

My own growth had not been a single transformation. It had accumulated in stages, each one leaving the previous version of me visible in the next. The first change was easy to dismiss. The second altered how I moved through doorways. By the third, people who had known me for years began to look twice. Then the Shock appeared. It was not a sound, although the old name made people expect one. It was a sudden change in the attention of a room, a pressure that travelled outward from me and made the ordinary arrangement of people feel briefly unstable.

The observatory model gave me a way to describe it. The black centre did not need to touch every object in the room to change its movement. Its influence was understood through the paths that bent around it. I watched the projected field expand and recognised something uncomfortable: people had begun to arrange their expectations around me before I had decided what I wanted to become. They asked whether I would reach Tower. They speculated about Sovereign. They spoke of my next demonstration as if my body were already a public event.

I told the Foundry lecturer that I wanted to know whether the field could be turned inward. She asked what I meant. I said that I wanted to grow without having every new stage immediately converted into a rank, a rumour, or a public demand. She adjusted the model. For a moment the sphere remained enormous, but the surrounding paths became quieter. The field still existed. It simply stopped announcing itself at every point.

That was the first useful thing I learned in the observatory: magnitude and expression were not identical. The Growth Stimulus might become supermassive, but its bearer still had to learn how to live inside it. I left with no new degree and no public demonstration. I left with a question that would become part of the Twin Centres study: can a field grow stronger while its bearer becomes more deliberate?

## XXIX. Nick — The Shape of the Orbit

I came to the Institute because I wanted to see the men everyone was talking about. I stayed because the place had begun to change the way I understood attention. Before the observatory, I thought the hierarchy was simple. A stronger Shock meant a larger presence; a larger presence meant a higher rank. I imagined the ladder as a set of steps, each one above the last. Then I watched Seth stand beside the black sphere and realised that the ladder was the wrong image.

The field was not a staircase. It was a system of relationships. A centre could become immense and still have paths moving around it at different distances. Some approached. Some curved away. Some crossed the field and left again. The model made room for something the rankings did not: a person could be affected by another's presence without being absorbed by it.

I began recording the paths. I used different marks for attraction, avoidance, shared movement, and independent passage. The first diagram looked like a map of the Institute after a major demonstration. The second looked like a family of stars. The third resembled two men trying to walk through the same narrow doorway without deciding who should go first. I kept that one.

Seth asked why. I told him that the drawing showed the problem more honestly than the grand diagrams. Two powerful people could share a space without either becoming the other's satellite. They could hesitate, make room, change direction, or walk through together. The field did not dictate every movement. It changed the conditions under which movement happened.

I have been invited to observe the next paired demonstration. My task is to record what happens around the bearers rather than trying to assign them a score. I will watch the people at the edge of the room, the ones who approach afterward, the ones who leave, and the ones who remain in conversation. The Institute has enough records of the instant when the Shock arrives. It needs a record of the orbit that follows.

## XXX. Neptune — The Deep That Recognises Weight

I am older than the Institute's language for growth. Before they named Ember and Bell, before they built the bronze ring and suspended their dark model above the floor, I knew the pressure of things that gather mass. I knew the ocean floor beneath the visible water, the trenches where light becomes memory, and the slow force by which a mountain changes the shape of a shore. The young men have chosen a black hole as their image. I do not object. They have found a useful likeness, though they have not yet understood its age.

They ask whether the Growth Stimulus is a blessing. I tell them that the sea does not bless a ship by becoming deep. Depth is a condition, not a promise. A great presence changes the water around it. It may shelter a harbour, disturb a current, or draw a vessel toward a coast it cannot safely reach. The bearer must learn the difference between being powerful and being navigable.

Seth listens without interrupting. Nick writes down the words that seem least important. I recognise the two habits: one is trying to understand what he carries; the other is trying to understand what happens to everyone nearby. Both are necessary. A field without a bearer is only a diagram. A bearer without witnesses becomes a legend written by strangers.

I leave them with the old sea's counsel: let the field become vast, but learn its tides. No one should mistake the deepest water for the whole ocean.

## XXXI. Odin — The Measure That Remembers

They brought me the Assembly register and asked whether Sovereign should be treated as the final degree. I read the names, the disputed observations, the accounts of the two Bells, and the new diagrams of the Twin Centres. The question was not about rank. It was about endings. The young believe a hierarchy must culminate somewhere, as if the highest word could close the matter.

I have seen kingdoms reach their greatest extent and then discover that extent is not permanence. I have seen a name survive the throne that carried it. I have seen a weapon become an heirloom, an oath become a law, and a law become a burden that later generations must learn to carry differently. The Institute's degrees will change in the same way. Ember may acquire a new meaning. Tower may become common. Sovereign may prove to be a threshold rather than a summit.

I told them to keep the register open. Every degree must preserve the account of how it was reached, who witnessed it, and what the bearer did afterward. The last part matters most. A powerful instant can be repeated in a story until it seems to contain a whole life. The record must resist that temptation. A bearer is not only the strongest event attributed to him.

Nick asked whether memory could become heavier than the field itself. I told him that it already had. A field changes the paths of those nearby; memory changes the paths of those who come later. The archive is the longer gravity.

## XXXII. Zeus — The Difference Between Power and Rule

They expected me to defend the throne. It is what mortals expect of Zeus: a declaration that the strongest must command. I have commanded enough to know the weakness of that conclusion. A throne can gather obedience, but obedience is not the same as recognition. A storm can empty a square, but an empty square is not a loyal city.

The Time-of-Death Shock interests me because it reveals power before power has been translated into office. The room turns. The voices stop. For one instant the bearer is the centre of the gathering. Then the instant passes, and the people must decide what it meant. Some will admire him. Some will resent him. Some will ask him a question. Some will go back to their supper. That final freedom is what makes the event political.

I told the Assembly that Sovereign should be difficult to attain, but not because the bearer must become impossible to challenge. It should be difficult because the title must describe a rare combination: extraordinary reach, deliberate restraint, and the ability to remain answerable to other people. If the title means that nobody may speak against its bearer, then it is not sovereignty. It is merely silence with a crown placed upon it.

Seth asked whether I would recognise such a Sovereign if he stood before me. I said I would recognise the field first, then wait to see what the man did when the room began speaking again.

## XXXIII. The Older Gods at the Twin Centres

The three older gods met the younger researchers in the observatory after the final projection had been switched off. Neptune stood beside the dark sphere, Odin beside the register, and Zeus beneath the bronze ring. They did not agree about what the Growth Stimulus meant. Neptune saw a depth whose currents had to be understood. Odin saw an inheritance whose records would outlast its first bearers. Zeus saw a new form of authority that could become either legitimate or oppressive. Their disagreement was preserved as a three-part commentary rather than reconciled into a single doctrine.

Seth and Nick presented their observations. Seth described the pressure of becoming the centre of expectation. Nick showed the diagrams in which two fields altered one another without eliminating their separate paths. The gods studied the drawings in silence. Then Neptune asked for the version in which the paths curved away. Odin asked for the earliest sketch, before the labels had been added. Zeus asked to see the moment after the Shock, when the audience began to move again.

The request changed the experiment. Foundry had been preparing to measure the maximum reach of the paired fields. Now the researchers would also record the return to ordinary movement: who approached, who withdrew, who spoke first, and whether the two bearers could remain together without the gathering forcing them into a contest. The next demonstration would not be judged only by the magnitude of the event. It would be judged by the quality of the world that remained afterward.

The black sphere was illuminated once more. Two centres appeared, each surrounded by a field. Their paths approached, curved, and settled into a broad orbit. At the edge of the projection, a small point continued along its own route, untouched by either centre. Nick asked whether that point represented freedom. Odin answered that it represented a fact the model had not yet explained. Neptune called it a current. Zeus called it a choice. Seth watched it until the lights went out.

The archive preserved all three answers. The point remains unnamed.


## XXXIV. Seth and Nick — The Point Beyond the Orbit

Seth noticed the point again when the observatory was nearly empty. The bronze ring had been lowered, the dark sphere had gone quiet, and the projection had returned to its simplest form: two centres, two fields, a broad orbit, and the small mark at the edge that none of the instruments could explain. It was not moving away. It was not approaching. It continued along a route that seemed to have been present before the researchers built their model. Seth leaned closer to the glass and asked Nick whether he had changed the scale.

Nick shook his head. He had checked the calibration twice. The point remained the same distance from the outer line, though the line itself shifted whenever the two fields changed. He had tried removing the bearers from the projection, but the point remained. He had tried removing the orbit, and it remained. He had tried displaying the earliest measurements, before the Assembly had agreed on the names Ember and Bell. The point was there too, faintly recorded in the margin of an observation that had originally been dismissed as instrument noise.

“That means it was never outside the experiment,” Seth said. “We only decided what counted as inside.”

Nick turned the register toward him. The old observation had been written by a junior recorder who had since left the Institute. Beside the faint mark was a note: *unregistered movement, no apparent source*. The recorder had not called it a third centre. He had not called it an error. He had simply preserved what he saw. Seth liked the restraint of that. It was the kind of record that could survive a fashionable explanation.

They invited Neptune, Odin, and Zeus back the following afternoon. The gods studied the projection without speaking. Neptune asked whether the point followed a current. Odin asked whether the original recorder had left any other notes. Zeus asked whether the point could be approached. Nick answered that the instruments could follow it but could not establish that it was a body, a bearer, or even a location. The projection represented a relation, not necessarily an object. It might be a route through the field, a witness beyond the model, or a limit in the way the Institute understood its own measurements.

Zeus was dissatisfied. “If it has no name, how do you know it matters?”

“Because it remains when we remove the things we thought made it,” Nick said.

Odin looked at the old register. “Then the first duty is not to name it. The first duty is to preserve the observation.”

Neptune stood beside the dark sphere. “A current is not diminished because a sailor has not charted it. But a sailor who names every current before he has felt it will mistake the map for the sea.”

Seth watched the two centres rotate. Their fields altered one another, but neither absorbed the other. The unnamed point kept its route. He thought about the gatherings after a Shock, when people moved back into ordinary conversation and the bearer had to discover what remained of his influence once the silence had ended. The point seemed to belong to that interval: the movement that continued without permission from the centre.

The next demonstration was changed. The Institute would not attempt to draw the point inward, assign it a rank, or treat it as a hidden bearer. Instead, the Assembly would invite independent observers to record what happened around the paired fields. Some would watch the bearers. Others would watch the people at the edge of the room. A few would record the exits, the unoccupied chairs, and the routes taken by those who chose to leave. The result would be a map of the whole gathering rather than a portrait of its strongest members.

Before they left, Nick added a new line to the register: *The point remains unclassified. Its persistence is established; its nature is not.* Seth read it and nodded. The sentence did not solve the mystery. It gave the mystery a place where it could remain without being turned into somebody's possession.

The observatory lights went out one by one. In the final pane, the two centres faded first. The orbit disappeared after them. The small point remained for another moment, moving steadily through the dark, and then the glass reflected only the two men standing beside the register.
