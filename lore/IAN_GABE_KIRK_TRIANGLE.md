# Ian Kistner, Gabe, and Kirk — The Threefold Attraction

**Record type:** Fictional relationship arc and continuing story chapter  
**Status:** Incubating canon  
**Setting:** Shared story-world branch; exact local setting and wider in-world name remain open  
**Continuity:** Adult characters; developing relationships; no final pairing predetermined

## The first arrangement

The three of them had not set out to become a triangle. Ian Kistner and Gabe already had a way of finding each other in a room. Their conversations carried on after everyone else had moved to another subject, and even when they were apart, each seemed to retain an awareness of where the other was. It was not always obvious whether this was attraction, habit, or the beginning of something neither had yet named.

Kirk noticed before either of them acknowledged it. He knew Gabe well enough to recognise the difference between ordinary friendliness and the particular attention Gabe gave Ian. At first Kirk treated the matter lightly. He joined their conversations, teased them about their growing familiarity, and watched to see whether either would admit to being affected.

But Ian began to interest him in his own right. Kirk found himself looking forward to Ian's arrival, listening more closely when he spoke, and discovering that the conversations were better when all three were present. His attention no longer travelled only through Gabe. It had acquired a direction of its own.

Gabe noticed that change too. He was accustomed to understanding the space between himself and Kirk, and he had begun to understand the space between himself and Ian. What he had not anticipated was the possibility that those two spaces might become connected. Sometimes he felt like the person holding the group together; at other times he wondered whether Ian and Kirk were discovering a rapport that existed independently of him.

## Three distinct bonds

Ian and Gabe have the first recognisable current: an attraction expressed through sustained attention, private humour, and the small decisions that make two people keep choosing one another's company. Neither needs to declare it for it to affect the room.

Gabe and Kirk have the weight of familiarity. Their connection includes history, ease, loyalty, and the vulnerability of being known. Their closeness can be reassuring, but it can also make new feelings difficult to interpret. Kirk is not simply a rival to Ian, and Gabe is not a prize to be won.

Kirk and Ian develop a less expected current. They test one another through conversation, discover shared humour, and become increasingly conscious of the other's presence. Their rapport unsettles the arrangement because it cannot be explained as an extension of Gabe's interest. It belongs to them.

The three bonds do not have to be equal at every moment. Their balance changes with circumstance, attention, and choice. The story should allow attraction to be uneven, reciprocal, uncertain, surprising, and capable of changing without treating any character as disposable.

## The evening that changes the arrangement

One evening, the three remain together after the others have left. Gabe sits back, comfortable enough to let the conversation wander. Kirk has taken the chair beside him. Ian sits opposite them, close enough to be part of the same conversation without having to compete for it.

Gabe notices that Kirk has been listening to Ian with unusual concentration. Ian notices that Kirk's jokes are increasingly directed at him. Neither seems especially interested in hiding it.

“You two are getting along rather well,” Gabe says.

Kirk looks over. “Is that a problem?”

Gabe considers the question. “I haven't decided.”

Ian smiles, but does not rescue either of them. He has begun to understand that Gabe's uncertainty is not necessarily rejection, and that Kirk's attention is not merely a performance.

For a moment the conversation stops. The silence is not hostile. It is the first time the three have allowed the possibility to remain in the room without immediately turning it into a joke.

Kirk breaks the pause by asking Ian whether he will be around the following evening. Ian says he will. Gabe looks between them, then says he expects to see them both.

It is a small exchange, but it changes the arrangement. The next meeting is no longer something that happens because Gabe and Ian happen to be together, or because Kirk happens to join them. All three have chosen to return.

## Continuing development

The triangle is a living relationship arc, not a contest with a predetermined winner. Its scenes should build through ordinary shared life: meals, visits, work, teasing, private conversations, invitations, pauses, and the gradual recognition of what each person wants. Let the emotional stakes emerge through behaviour rather than explaining every feeling in advance.

Preserve each character's independent agency. Do not assume a real-world person's private feelings or treat fictional developments as evidence about an actual person. Within the story, allow each of the three to initiate, hesitate, misunderstand, clarify, and change their mind.

The Familiarity Paradox can operate as a quiet structural principle: each person becomes aware of being perceived by the other two, and that reciprocal awareness changes the atmosphere they create together. Their shared plane of attention does not erase their separate identities. It makes the space between them increasingly meaningful.

## Avoidance as the triangle's central tension

**User-established character canon (24 November 2026):** Ian is always somewhat avoidant, and Gabe is much the same. This is not a claim that either man lacks feeling. Both can be drawn toward someone and still hesitate when the attraction asks to become explicit. Their attention may be clear while their intentions remain difficult to read. A warm exchange can be followed by a pause; an invitation can be delayed; a man may retreat into humour, ordinary business, or silence just when the other expects him to move closer.

Do not make Ian and Gabe identical. Their shared tendency is established; the precise shape, triggers, and habits of each man's avoidance are still to be discovered. Let their differences emerge through scenes rather than assigning an ungrounded psychological explanation. Neither should be reduced to a cold or manipulative archetype. Their avoidance can protect privacy, preserve control, or simply reflect uncertainty, but its meaning must be earned by behaviour.

This changes the triangle's pacing. Kirk may be the one who notices the pattern, but he is not automatically its interpreter, cure, or pursuer. The three can misread one another because attention and action do not always arrive together. A moment of closeness may be followed by distance without cancelling what happened. Build progress through small acts of return, reliable presence, and occasional plain speech rather than forcing a sudden confession or resolution.

Gabe's existing directness with Dorian remains valid: an avoidant tendency does not mean he never initiates. It means that choosing closeness can be difficult and uneven, especially after the initial moment of courage. Ian's specific pattern is not yet established beyond the user's description of him as avoidant.


## Shared temperament — avoidant kindness

**User-established canon note (24 November 2026):** Ian and Gabe are both characteristically avoidant. Their avoidance is part of how the relationship develops, not a diagnosis and not evidence that either man is indifferent. Both can be kind, attentive, and sincerely interested while struggling to stay present when a moment requires direct emotional disclosure. They may show care through practical attention, humour, remembering a small preference, or simply returning to the room; when asked to define what is happening, they may become quiet, redirect the conversation, delay an answer, or take space.

The similarity creates both comfort and difficulty. Ian may recognise Gabe's retreat without demanding an immediate explanation. Gabe may understand why Ian leaves a vulnerable question unanswered. But mutual understanding can become mutual permission to avoid the very conversation that would let the relationship move forward. Neither should be written as a mind-reader: they can misread silence, worry that they have pressed too hard, or assume the other wants distance when he is actually waiting for reassurance.

Their patterns remain distinct. Ian's particular habits and reasons are still open for development. Gabe's avoidance must coexist with his established ability to initiate attraction, enjoy being seen, set boundaries, and choose the pace of a connection. The story should let both men act directly at meaningful moments; avoidant does not mean permanently passive.

Kirk encounters this pattern from a different position. He may be more direct, or he may have his own hesitation; do not settle that trait without writing it. His response can expose the limits of the pair's shared retreat: he may ask a plain question, leave space for an answer, or refuse to keep guessing indefinitely. The triangle's movement should come from specific choices and consequences rather than a formula in which one man pursues and the other two withdraw.


## The walk after the table

Gabe left before the room had emptied. He had checked the time twice during the meal, though he had not said what he needed to do afterward. When he finally stood, he gathered his coat and thanked them both for the evening.

“I'll see you,” Kirk said.

“Of course.”

Ian looked up. “Goodnight, Gabe.”

Gabe paused as if he might add something, then only smiled. “Goodnight.”

The door closed behind him. His chair remained slightly turned toward the table, and the conversation he had left behind seemed to occupy it for another moment.

Kirk reached for the empty plates. Ian began collecting the cups.

“You don't have to do that,” Kirk said.

“I know.”

“Then why are you?”

Ian looked at the cups in his hands. “Because they're here.”

Kirk laughed. “That's a very complete answer.”

They carried everything to the side counter. Kirk rinsed the plates while Ian dried them, working without needing to decide who would do which task. The water ran hot, and a little steam gathered against the window. Beyond it, the path was dark. A lamp near the entrance had begun to attract small insects, each one appearing briefly in the light before disappearing again.

When the last cup was put away, Kirk took his coat from the back of the chair. Ian was still standing by the counter.

“Are you going?” Kirk asked.

“I was.”

“That isn't quite the same thing.”

Ian looked at him. Kirk did not soften the question with a joke. He had noticed that Ian often gave an answer about what he had intended to do, when the question concerned what he wanted now. Kirk was not angry about it. He was beginning to find it difficult to pretend he had not noticed.

“I could walk with you,” Ian said.

“You could.”

“If you want.”

“I asked.”

Ian lowered his eyes for a moment, then nodded. “All right.”

They stepped outside together. The air was cool enough that Kirk pulled his coat closed. Ian walked beside him with his hands in his pockets. They followed the path past the darkened windows and the low wall around the garden. Somewhere beyond the residences, a door shut and a burst of laughter rose, then faded. The evening had not become private merely because the two of them were alone. Other people were still moving through it, going home, collecting food, returning books, and making plans for the next day.

Kirk slowed near the garden gate. “You and Gabe have been spending time together.”

“Yes.”

“I like seeing you together.”

Ian glanced at him. “Do you?”

“I do. I also like spending time with you.”

Ian's pace changed, almost imperceptibly. He looked toward the path ahead.

Kirk continued. “Those aren't the same statement. I don't want you to think I'm only interested because Gabe is interested.”

“I didn't think that.”

“Good.”

They walked a little farther. Kirk could hear the gravel shift beneath their shoes. He had expected Ian to make a joke or turn the conversation toward something easier. Instead Ian stayed beside him, quiet but present.

“Sometimes I can't tell whether you're comfortable with me,” Kirk said.

Ian stopped. Kirk stopped too, leaving enough room between them that neither had to step back.

“I am,” Ian said.

“You look comfortable until I ask you something.”

“I know.”

“Then you disappear while you're still standing there.”

Ian gave a short, embarrassed laugh. “That's fair.”

Kirk leaned one shoulder against the low wall. “I'm not asking you to explain your whole life. I just don't want to keep guessing whether you want me here.”

Ian looked at him properly then. His expression was thoughtful, and for once he did not reach for an answer that would close the subject without deciding anything.

“I want you here,” he said.

Kirk held his gaze. “All right.”

“I don't always know what to do after I say something like that.”

“You don't have to do anything immediately.”

“That helps.”

“But it doesn't mean I can guess forever.”

“I know.”

The answer came quietly. Ian seemed to hear its weight after he had spoken it. He took one hand from his pocket and rubbed his thumb across the side of his finger, then let the hand fall again.

Kirk did not move closer. He waited.

“I'd like to see you again,” Ian said. “Just us. Not because Gabe is there. Not to make anything difficult for him. I want to know what it's like when we're not all having the same conversation.”

Kirk smiled, but did not turn the invitation into a victory. “I'd like that.”

“Would you?”

“Yes, Ian.”

Ian's shoulders eased. The change was small, but Kirk saw it. He had not asked for a promise about the three of them, or a declaration about what the evening meant. He had asked for another walk, and Ian had answered.

They reached the end of the path where it divided toward the residences. Kirk stopped beneath the lamp.

“Tomorrow?” he asked.

Ian hesitated. The old retreat appeared in the pause, the instinct to leave the next decision for a later moment. Then he looked back at Kirk.

“Tomorrow. After supper?”

“After supper.”

“I'll come find you.”

Kirk nodded. “I'll be here.”

They did not kiss. Neither man tried to turn the agreement into more than it was. Kirk touched Ian's arm once, briefly, and Ian returned the gesture with his hand against Kirk's shoulder. It was a small contact, freely given and received, and it lasted long enough for both men to notice it.

At the path's division they went in separate directions. Ian looked back once. Kirk was still beneath the lamp, not waiting as though Ian owed him another word, but watching him go. Ian raised his hand. Kirk raised his in return.

The next evening had acquired a shape. Gabe had not arranged it, and neither man had asked him to. That did not make Gabe less important. It meant only that Kirk and Ian had begun to discover what belonged to them.
