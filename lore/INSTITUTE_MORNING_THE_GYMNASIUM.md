# The Institute — A Morning in the Gymnasium

The morning began before the Institute was properly awake.

A pale blue light rested against the upper windows of the gymnasium, catching the timber beams one by one. The building had a particular sound at that hour: ventilation moving somewhere behind the walls, the faint metallic settling of equipment, the occasional footstep from the covered passage outside. Beyond the glass, the grounds were still damp. The residences stood across the courtyard, their windows gradually illuminating as men began to get up, shower, dress and make their way downstairs.

The first man through the gym doors was already dressed for training. He carried a towel over one shoulder and a large water bottle in his hand. He knew which lights took a moment to warm up, which lockers stuck in cold weather, and which bench had a slight movement in its frame. He set his things down, looked across the room, and stood for a moment beneath the high windows. There was no particular reason to hurry. He had come to enjoy this part of the day, when the room belonged to whoever happened to be there.

He began with his shoulders. A slow roll, then another. His arms came forward and opened again as he settled into his stance. The mirror caught the movement and returned it, first as a familiar outline, then as a more substantial figure when he stepped into the light. He watched his own posture, adjusted his feet, and began the first set.

By the time the second man arrived, the room had acquired its rhythm.

The newcomer greeted him with a nod and went to the racks. They did not need to arrange a session together. They had trained in the same room often enough to know when the other preferred to concentrate and when a conversation would be welcome. The first man finished his set, returned the weights carefully, and moved aside. The second began his own preparation, testing his grip and setting his stance. Their movements were different. One was deliberate and economical; the other seemed to gather momentum from the room itself, warming to the work as the plates came into position.

More men arrived in ones and twos. A resident from the upper dormitory came down with his hair still damp. Two friends entered in the middle of a discussion about a lecture they had attended the previous evening. A broad-shouldered man in an old university shirt stopped to help move a bench before beginning his own routine. The room filled without becoming crowded. People made space for one another as naturally as they changed weights or adjusted a cable.

The gymnasium was large enough to hold several different kinds of attention. Near the windows, a man worked through a careful sequence of mobility exercises. At the far end, another studied his form in the mirror between sets. Two friends took turns spotting each other, exchanging brief instructions and occasional jokes. Someone had left a notebook open on the low table beside the water station. It contained a record of equipment repairs, training observations, and a few sketches of the old timber roof. The Institute had a habit of allowing one interest to find its way into another.

The Coach arrived without ceremony. He paused at the entrance, took in the room, and greeted the men he knew. He was a substantial figure, broad through the shoulders and chest, with the easy bearing of someone who had spent years among athletes. His presence changed the room slightly, though not because anyone stopped what they were doing. He knew how to enter a place without making it revolve around him. He spoke to one man about a course, asked another whether the repaired cable was working properly, and listened while a third described an idea for a new training programme.

He eventually reached the man who had arrived first.

"How's it feeling this morning, big man?"

"Good. Better than yesterday."

"Then let's keep yesterday in the record and see what today gives you."

The Coach said it as though this were the most ordinary thing in the world. He took the notebook from the table, made a small annotation, and returned it to its place. The man resumed his training. Nothing dramatic happened, but the day had acquired a point of continuity: yesterday had not disappeared. It was present in the way he moved, in the weight he selected, and in the small confidence with which he approached the next set.

A little later, one of the mirrors seemed to hold the previous position of his arms after he had lowered them. It lasted no more than a breath. The reflection settled, and the room returned to itself. He glanced at the glass again, then at his hands. He did not call anyone over. The Institute was full of small irregularities that became less mysterious when given time, and he had learned not to interrupt a good morning merely to explain one.

When the session ended, the men dispersed gradually. Some went to the changing rooms, where showers ran behind tiled walls and the mirrors slowly cleared of steam. Others collected their bags and crossed directly to the dining hall. The first man took a longer route through the covered walk. His shirt sat differently across his shoulders after training, and he noticed the change when he passed the dark window of the workshop. He stopped briefly, not to admire himself, but to see how the fabric fell when he relaxed his arms. Then he continued.

Breakfast was already underway. The kitchen had prepared eggs, potatoes, fruit, toast and a large pot of coffee. A few men were discussing the afternoon's lecture; someone at the end of the table was trying to recruit a partner for a project in the woodshop. The man from the gym found a place beside his training partner. They talked about the next session, then about something else entirely. Their morning had become part of the larger day without needing to remain its subject.

From the dining hall windows, the gymnasium could still be seen across the courtyard. Its upper windows held the same pale light. A man was inside putting away the last of the equipment. The room was emptying, but it was not finished. Later, someone else would enter. The benches would be moved, the lights would change, and another sequence of effort and recovery would begin.

The Institute did not measure its life by how long anyone remained in one room. Its continuity came from the way one room prepared the next. A man carried the morning into his classes, his conversations, his work and his return to residence. The gymnasium kept its place in the centre of the grounds, not because everyone had to pass through it, but because it had become one of the places where the Institute learned its inhabitants.

By evening, the same mirror would hold a different light. The same man might return, or he might not. His place in the morning would remain available without being reserved for him. The building remembered the movement, but it did not demand its repetition.
