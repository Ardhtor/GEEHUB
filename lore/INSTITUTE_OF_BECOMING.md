# The Institute of Becoming

**Status: Incubating story branch within the Tractus.**  
**Continuity contract:** [Tractus Continuity Contract](../docs/TRACTUS_CONTINUITY_CONTRACT.md)

The Institute of Becoming began as an idea the Coach wrote in the margin of his clipboard. He had been asked to explain what his coaching was supposed to accomplish, and the usual answers—discipline, improvement, measurable progress—seemed to him too narrow for the people who kept arriving at his door. He wanted an institution where a person could discover what he was capable of becoming without being made to feel that his present condition was a failure. The Institute would provide instruction when instruction was wanted, company when company was needed, and enough room for ambitions to develop into forms their owners had not yet imagined. The Coach called this making room. The phrase became the first unofficial motto of the place.

The original gymnasium remained at the centre of the grounds, although it no longer defined the limits of the institution. Its old timber roof, polished floor, broad doors, and high windows were preserved as a point of origin while new buildings gathered around it. Residential quarters opened onto covered walks; lecture rooms looked across gardens and courtyards; workshops occupied the lower floors of a long brick range; and a dining hall was built large enough to accommodate the ordinary rhythms of the entire community. The architecture developed by use rather than by a single master plan. When a room became crowded, the Institute considered what kind of space was missing. When a workshop attracted people from another department, a connecting passage appeared in the next building proposal. The grounds acquired the character of a place that had learned to anticipate its inhabitants without deciding their lives for them.

## The Coach

The Coach became the Institute's most recognizable figure, though he resisted being treated as its sole authority. He was a large, broad, increasingly imposing American man with a warm voice and an easy familiarity that made new arrivals feel as though they had already been expected. He called people buddy, chief, and big man, and he had a habit of treating extraordinary developments as ordinary matters that could be accommodated with a little thought and a better chair. His office grew with him: the desk became heavier, the chairs deeper, the door wider, and the ceiling higher. None of this was announced as a spectacle. The building simply kept making room for the person who had helped it begin. The Coach's real influence, however, came from the way he listened. He asked what someone wanted to make, where they hoped to go, and what had prevented them from beginning. He did not always provide an answer. Often he would nod, look across the grounds, and say that they would have to find a place for it.

The dining hall became the Institute's first enduring social centre. The Coach disliked the hurried cafeteria model, where people collected a meal and disappeared before anyone had learned their names. He wanted long tables, generous portions, open kitchens, and enough time for conversations to become part of the day. Meals brought students, instructors, workshop residents, visitors, and groundskeepers into the same room without requiring them to share an occupation. Someone might arrive to discuss a lecture and leave having agreed to help repair a greenhouse; another might come for supper and discover a society devoted to local history, furniture-making, or the study of changing proportions in architecture. The Institute's culture of abundance was not measured only by the amount of food on the tables. It was measured by the willingness to make another place, offer another serving, and let an incidental encounter become consequential.

The inhabitants were never intended to be extensions of the Coach. They developed their own interests, friendships, disagreements, habits, and reputations. Some were students; some taught; some maintained the grounds or worked in the kitchens; others arrived for a short course and stayed because they had found a community in which their work could continue. The Institute's first generation of residents began to establish clubs and informal departments, and those groups gradually changed the institution's calendar. There were open workshops, shared suppers, evening walks, lectures followed by long discussions, and seasonal gatherings in the gardens. A student who had become fascinated by the original gymnasium began documenting its alterations. A cook proposed a regional supper series. A group of residents started keeping a record of the buildings that had changed in response to daily use. Each project gave the Institute another way to remember itself.

The Institute belongs to the wider Tractus rather than replacing any of its established places. Its relationship with Northgate University, the Dad Convent, and the other campuses and gathering places should develop through visits, correspondence, shared events, and people who carry their experiences between institutions. These connections are passages: each place retains its own character while gaining something from the encounter. The Institute's history is therefore open, but it is not without a past. The old gymnasium remains visible in the foundations; the Coach's original note remains in the archive; and every new room inherits the reasons the earlier rooms were built.

The first principle of the Institute is that development should not require erasure. The second is that hospitality is a form of infrastructure. Its third principle, which the Coach added after watching the first residents begin building institutions of their own, is that no place should become so complete that it has no room for what comes next. The Institute continues to expand through the choices of its inhabitants, and its architecture records those choices without pretending that every experiment has become permanent. It is a place where people can arrive with an unfinished idea, find the company and materials to give it form, and leave behind a passage that someone else may continue.

At the entrance, beneath the name cast into a broad oak lintel, the Coach eventually had three words engraved:

**MAKE ROOM. KEEP GOING.**
