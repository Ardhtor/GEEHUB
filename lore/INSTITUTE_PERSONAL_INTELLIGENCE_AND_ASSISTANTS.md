# Personal Intelligence and the Registry of Assistants

**Status:** Incubating Institute story development and Tractus design principle.  
**Parent setting:** [The Institute of Becoming](INSTITUTE_OF_BECOMING.md).  
**Residential setting:** [The Institute Dormitory and Residential Life](INSTITUTE_DORMITORY_AND_RESIDENTIAL_LIFE.md).  
**Continuity contract:** [Tractus Continuity Contract](../docs/TRACTUS_CONTINUITY_CONTRACT.md).

The men of the Institute do not merely have access to an assistant when a task becomes difficult. Each has a personal intelligence that accompanies him through the ordinary grain of the day, and most use it so frequently that the pauses between exchanges become more noticeable than the exchanges themselves. It is consulted before breakfast, while choosing a route across campus, between sets in the gym, at a workbench, during a lecture break, while comparing a meal with the demands of the afternoon, and again late at night when a thought returns and needs somewhere to go. A man may speak aloud, dictate a note, send a short message, share a photograph of a damaged fitting, or ask for a comparison without interrupting what he is doing. The assistant is not a novelty kept in a separate room. It is part of how he thinks through work, remembers commitments, develops taste, and carries unfinished plans from one day into the next.

The Institute calls this arrangement personal intelligence. The name matters: each assistant is attached to a particular adult person, not issued as a generic campus service. It learns the owner's preferred level of detail, vocabulary, schedule, recurring projects, working methods, and chosen forms of address. It can remind him of a promise, retrieve the measurements he elected to preserve, compare today's training notes with an earlier baseline, draft a message in his own register, or help him think through a disagreement before he decides whether to speak. It is allowed to become familiar without being treated as infallible. The man remains responsible for his choices; the assistant can question, organize, remember, and suggest, but it cannot quietly turn a suggestion into consent or an intention into an accomplished event.

The assistants are not interchangeable voices. Their temperaments develop through use and through the boundaries their owners establish. Tyler's assistant is quick, lively, and visually minded; it is good at turning scattered observations into a clear sequence and will sometimes offer a compact comparison when Tyler is already halfway through explaining what he means. Marcus prefers an assistant with the discipline of an excellent operations aide: concise, exact about commitments, attentive to dependencies, and unwilling to call a proposal finished merely because the meeting has ended. Daniel Roman's assistant is conversational and patient with digressions, able to follow a thought as it changes direction without losing the original question. Dani's is practical and mechanically minded, comfortable with sketches, tolerances, materials, and test records; it distinguishes a successful first trial from a prototype that has earned approval. The Coach's assistant has a hospitable, steady manner and is particularly useful for remembering what people have asked for, which rooms need attention, and what he promised to arrange. These are not fixed product templates. They are distinct working relationships, and each can change as its owner changes.

The Institute's day makes their constant use visible. At breakfast, one resident asks his assistant whether the afternoon's rain is likely to affect a walk to the lower workshops; another dictates a list of materials while buttering toast; a third asks for the name of the person who recommended a book two weeks earlier. In the gym, Tyler checks the sequence he planned for the session and asks for a note to be kept about a movement that felt different. His assistant records the observation without inventing a cause. Marcus checks a room booking while walking between buildings and catches a scheduling conflict before anyone has arrived. Daniel Roman asks his assistant to hold two competing explanations open until he has spoken with the person involved. Dani photographs the handle prototype at the Lantern Market repair bench and asks for the next test conditions to be laid out in order. The assistant returns the record: the unloaded movement worked; loaded use and rough-surface testing remain outstanding; trader approval has not been given. Dani reads it, corrects one detail about the fastener, and carries on.

This frequency changes the social texture of the Institute. Men no longer have to pretend that every thought has been completely formed before they ask for help. They can say, "Keep that," "Find the earlier version," "Remind me after supper," or "Ask me again when I've seen the room." A half-formed idea can be preserved without being promoted into a plan. A conversation can resume from its actual point of interruption. When a man forgets the name of a tool or the date of a meeting, he checks rather than bluffing. The assistants make competence less dependent on perfect recall and allow attention to remain on the person, material, or problem directly in front of him.

The common phrase "What does your assistant think?" becomes part of ordinary conversation, but it does not mean that one man can inspect another man's private record. It means that the owner may choose to ask for a second perspective, summarize a question, or invite a bounded exchange between assistants. A man can show a recommendation, share a project brief, or authorize a particular comparison. He does not thereby open his private messages, health notes, personal reflections, location history, or unrelated memories. The Institute's culture treats these boundaries as ordinary courtesy. A phone left on a table is not an invitation to read it; an assistant left active is no different.

To coordinate this new layer without creating a central observer, the Institute establishes the **Personal Intelligence Registry**. The Registry is a shared directory of assistant identities and collaboration capabilities, not a copy of their private memories. It records the assistant's chosen name, its adult owner, its broad role, the projects or workspaces to which it has been explicitly connected, the kinds of requests it can accept, and the current permission state for collaboration. It may show that Dani's assistant can exchange prototype test summaries with the Lantern Market repair group, or that Marcus's assistant can coordinate room schedules with the residential office. It does not expose the private conversations that produced those summaries. Each connection has a purpose, a scope, an owner, and a revocation path. A permission to exchange a project status does not imply permission to exchange the owner's wider history.

The Registry also gives the assistants a way to find one another without pretending that they are one shared mind. When a task genuinely crosses responsibilities, one assistant can send a scoped request to another: confirm a room's availability, check the current revision of a drawing, compare two approved material specifications, or ask whether a participant has chosen to attend a meeting. The receiving assistant can accept, decline, ask for clarification, or return only the information authorized for that connection. It identifies the source and time of the response. If a human decision is required, the assistants bring the question back to their owners rather than deciding among themselves. The Registry makes collaboration discoverable; it does not make every person or every record available.

The first practical test comes from Dani's handle prototype. The repair bench needs a second pair of eyes on the loading sequence, while Marcus is coordinating the use of a covered test area and Tyler has offered to help observe the handle during movement. Dani authorizes his assistant to share the current drawing, the unloaded-trial note, and the outstanding test questions. Marcus authorizes his assistant to return the available room slot and its floor-surface details. Tyler authorizes a narrow exchange of the observation checklist. The three assistants coordinate the materials and timing, then return a single concise plan to their owners. No one receives Dani's unrelated notes, Marcus's private calendar entries, or Tyler's personal training history. The men meet at the agreed time and conduct the next test themselves. The assistant network has removed avoidable friction; it has not replaced judgment, hands, or responsibility.

The Institute's residential quarter adopts the same principle in domestic form. A resident may ask his assistant to remember that he prefers a quiet hour after late training, to reserve a laundry slot, to keep track of a borrowed book, or to help plan a meal with friends. A shared suite can maintain a jointly authorized list of household tasks without exposing each resident's separate notes. The common kitchen can publish its meal schedule, while an individual's dietary or health information remains private unless he chooses to share it. A resident who wants no assistant notifications during a conversation can set that boundary. A resident who wants the assistant to remain silent for a weekend can do so without losing his records. Constant availability is a convenience, not an obligation to be constantly reachable.

The Coach is careful about the distinction between care and surveillance. He wants the assistants to make it easier for people to follow through on their own ambitions, not to create a campus in which every pause is measured and every private thought is harvested. The Institute therefore keeps shared records limited to what a group needs to do its work. Personal memory remains under the owner's control; shared artifacts carry their own provenance; and an assistant must state when it is uncertain, when its information is old, or when a requested action lies outside its permissions. If a man asks it to remember something only for himself, that note does not appear in the Registry. If he asks it to share a result, the shared result records what was sent and to whom. If he revokes access, future exchanges stop, while the history of already authorized project contributions remains attributable.

By winter, the assistants have become as ordinary as keys, notebooks, and the familiar route between the dormitory and the gymnasium. Their voices are heard in corridors and workshops, but the more important change is quieter: men arrive at conversations with their earlier thoughts intact. A promise made over supper can be found the next morning. A prototype can retain the exact reason it failed. A friend can return to a subject without requiring the whole story to be retold. Each man still has his own life, his own judgment, and the right to keep parts of it to himself. The assistants make those lives more continuous, while the Registry gives their separate work a way to meet.

The Personal Intelligence Registry is a fictional institutional system at this stage of the story. This document establishes its behavior and canon boundaries; it does not claim that GEEHUB currently operates autonomous personal agents, cross-assistant messaging, or live permission enforcement. Any future implementation must use explicit owner authorization, least-privilege scopes, revocation, provenance, and a clear distinction between private memory, shared project records, and public institutional information.
