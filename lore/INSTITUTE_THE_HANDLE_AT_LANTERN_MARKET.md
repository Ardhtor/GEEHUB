# The Handle at Lantern Market
## An Institute of Becoming passage — 21 November 2026

The rail held.

Tyler checked it before the training room had filled, first with his hand, then by hanging a folded towel from the lowest hook and pulling it gently in the direction a person might pull while reaching for the shelf. The bracket stayed against the cedar backing. Dani arrived while Tyler was making a note on the facilities sheet, his gym bag hanging from one shoulder and his hair still damp from the walk across the courtyard.

“You came back,” Tyler said.

“I said I would.”

“You could have waited until the evening.”

Dani put his bag down. “Then you would have had to check it twice.”

Tyler looked at him for a moment, trying to decide whether the answer was a joke. Dani's expression gave him no help. Then the corner of his mouth moved, and Tyler laughed. The rail was not the only thing that had become familiar. Their exchanges had begun to arrive with less preparation. Tyler did not have to arrange every question in advance, and Dani had stopped giving him the short answer merely because a longer one was possible.

Daniel Roman was already in the lower repair room. He had brought the measurements from the changing-room review and spread them beside a drawing of a removable bench support. The room occupied the ground floor of the Institute's older workshop range. Its windows were tall but set high enough that the morning light entered as a pale band across the benches. A smell of cedar dust and machine oil remained in the boards, and the floor carried the marks of past work: small dents where a piece had been dropped, a dark ring from a tin of screws, and a pale rectangle where a cabinet had once stood.

The Coach came through the wide doors while Daniel was explaining a bracket. He was a large man, broad through the chest and shoulders, with a warm voice that reached the back of the room without requiring him to raise it. He glanced at the rail drawing, then at the three men.

“Morning, fellas. I hear the changing room's behaving itself.”

“For now,” Tyler said.

“That's how buildings start. First they behave, then they get ideas.”

Dani leaned against the bench. “We were going to Lantern Market.”

The Coach looked up. “Were you?”

“There's a cart down there that needs a better handle. We haven't seen it yet. We only heard about it from the market repair stall.”

Daniel turned a page. “Elias sent a note through the city workshop exchange. The handle is too low under load. He's been wrapping it with cloth to change the grip, but the wrapping keeps slipping.”

The Coach placed his hands on the edge of the bench and read the note. “So you're going to look at it?”

“Ask first,” Dani said. “Then look.”

The Coach nodded as though the distinction pleased him. “Good. Take your measurements, get some lunch, and don't promise him a new cart before you've understood the old one. I'll come along. I've been meaning to see how the market's using the new covered stalls.”

They left the Institute by the west gate. A public shuttle carried them to the city interchange, where the road descended toward Lantern Market through older streets of brick shops and narrow stone-fronted houses. The day had cleared after the previous evening's rain, but the awnings still dripped at their edges. Water ran in narrow threads beside the curbs. The air changed as they approached the market: coffee, wet timber, crushed rosemary, orange peel, warm bread, and the faint metallic smell of tools laid out on canvas.

Lantern Market was not a single hall. It was a network of covered lanes and small open squares, with permanent stalls along the sheltered sides and temporary tables occupying the wider spaces. The market had been built in layers. The first stone arcade was older than the tram route; newer timber roofs crossed between buildings where rain had once made the passages unusable. A bell over the eastern entrance rang each time a delivery trolley came through. Someone was arranging pears in a shallow wooden tray. A man in a green apron was lifting the lid from a pot of beans. At the far end, a woman was sweeping leaves from the threshold of a shop that sold repaired lamps and old kitchenware.

Elias was standing beside a handcart with two crates strapped to its frame. He was an adult man with a narrow face, thick forearms, and a short beard beginning to grey at the chin. His sleeves were rolled unevenly, one cuff higher than the other. A strip of blue cloth had been wound around the cart's crossbar, but the material had shifted toward the left end. He greeted the Coach first, then looked at Dani and Tyler.

“Which one of you is the engineer?”

Daniel raised his hand. “Depends what you're asking.”

“I'm asking which one will tell me I need a new cart.”

“Not before we've looked at it,” Daniel said.

Elias seemed satisfied with that. He showed them the cart and explained how he used it. The crates travelled from a storage room behind the market to his stall, then back again at the end of the day. The lane outside his storage room sloped slightly. The cart had two large wheels and a supporting foot; its frame was sound, and the axle had been recently serviced. The problem appeared when it was loaded. The handle was comfortable enough when the cart was empty, but once the crates were stacked, he had to bend farther than he liked and pull with his wrists turned inward. If he raised his hands, the cart's nose lifted too quickly. If he lowered them, the crossbar pressed against the heel of his palm.

Dani did not reach for the cart. He watched Elias move it first. Elias pushed it away from the stall, turned it around a basket stand, then brought it back through the narrow gap between two tables. The cart passed, but the turn forced Elias to take a second step and swing his right shoulder around the frame. Dani watched his feet rather than the wheels. When Elias stopped, Dani pointed to the blue cloth.

“Does that stay where you put it?”

“For about ten minutes.”

“Does it help?”

“Until it doesn't.”

Tyler had already taken out his notebook. “Could we measure the handle height with the cart loaded the way you use it?”

Elias looked from the notebook to the crates. “You can. Don't move the fruit.”

“We won't.”

They measured the grip height, the distance from the axle to the crossbar, the width of the narrowest passage, and the room Elias needed to turn without catching the rear corner against a stall. Daniel crouched beside the mounting plates and examined the fasteners. The cart had been repaired before, but the repairs were visible rather than hidden: two different bolts, a replaced wooden crosspiece, and a small plate on the right side where the original bracket had cracked years earlier.

“It's not the cart,” Dani said after a while. “It's asking him to lift when he needs to pull.”

Elias folded his arms. “That's what I've been telling people.”

“Then we should probably listen.”

Marcus, who had been looking at the crates, came around the other side. “Would a second grip help? One higher, one lower?”

“Maybe,” Daniel said. “But we need to know how it mounts. If we add a second bar without understanding the frame, we could make the turn worse.”

Tyler marked the turning arc on his paper. “And if we make it too wide, he loses the gap.”

The Coach stood beside the stall, giving the cart room. He had not taken over the conversation. He looked at Elias and asked whether the proposed handle would be useful if it could be removed at the end of the day.

Elias thought about it. “I don't mind leaving it on. But I want to be able to take it off if I need to get the cart into the back room. The door is narrow.”

That changed the design. The men stopped discussing a permanent replacement and began discussing a removable assembly. Daniel sketched two narrow side plates that could use the cart's existing mounting points, with a crossbar that could be pinned in one of two positions. Tyler measured the available clearance. Dani took the empty cart through the turn again, this time slowly, while Elias watched from the stall entrance. The first position seemed comfortable for pulling on the level lane. The second allowed a more upright grip when the cart had to cross the raised stone at the storage-room threshold.

They did not promise that the sketch would work. Daniel wrote down the dimensions and made a note to inspect the mounting plates in the Institute workshop before choosing the pin size. Tyler photographed the existing arrangement with Elias's permission. Dani asked whether the blue cloth could be kept as a reference for the grip diameter. Elias untied it and handed it over.

“Don't lose that,” he said. “It's the most reliable part of the whole thing.”

“Then it goes in the evidence bag,” Tyler said.

Elias laughed. “You people really do write everything down.”

“Only the things that can fall on us,” Tyler said.

Dani looked at him. “Good policy.”

They ate at a small table beneath the western awning. The food came from three neighbouring stalls: flatbread still warm from the oven, lentils with garlic and lemon, roasted peppers, a tray of potatoes with herbs, and a bowl of sliced pears. The Coach ordered more than the table needed and pushed the extra plates toward the market workers who had finished their morning deliveries. Nobody made a ceremony of the offer. A man sat down, took a piece of bread, and continued telling Daniel about a repair to the old pump house.

During lunch Elias explained that the market had no common place for small repairs. Stallholders usually took broken equipment to whichever workshop they knew, and some repairs were postponed because the item was too awkward to transport or because the owner could not spare the time away from the stall. There were carpenters, metalworkers, and mechanics in the surrounding streets, but their hours did not always overlap. The market had tried a repair day once, years earlier, but nobody had been responsible for keeping the tools together or recording what had been changed.

Dani listened, turning the blue cloth between his fingers. He was not especially talkative over lunch. He folded the bread wrapper into a narrow strip and moved it away from the edge of the table. When Elias described the old repair day, Dani asked what had made it stop.

“Everybody brought something,” Elias said. “Nobody knew who was supposed to take it away afterward. We had a pile of half-fixed things and one man who'd lost his spanner.”

Marcus laughed. “That's a strong way to end a committee.”

“It wasn't a committee,” Elias said. “That was the problem.”

The Coach looked toward Dani. “You thinking about something?”

Dani glanced down at the sketch Daniel had left beside the plates. “The Institute has a workshop. The market has the repairs. We could bring a small bench here once a week. Not a whole department. Just a place to look at things, measure them, decide whether they're safe to repair, and send the bigger jobs to the right people.”

Daniel nodded. “A proper intake sheet. Owner, purpose, existing condition, proposed work, test required, and who takes it back.”

Tyler added, “And no one leaves an item without knowing where it is.”

Elias considered them. “You'd come here?”

“Some of us,” Dani said. “Not every job needs the same people.”

The Coach smiled. “Now that's a project.”

They returned to the Institute with the measurements, the blue cloth, and a promise to bring back a tested handle assembly before anyone attached it to the cart. The journey back was quieter. Marcus had bought a paper bag of apples for the workshop. Tyler was reading through his notes, checking that he had not copied a number into the wrong column. Dani sat opposite him, one hand resting on the strap of his gym bag. When the shuttle turned sharply, the bag shifted. Tyler caught the loose end before it slid to the floor and passed it back without interrupting his reading.

Dani took it. “Thanks.”

“Your notebook's about to fall out.”

“That's yours.”

Tyler looked down. The notebook was indeed halfway out of his own bag. Dani held the strap while Tyler pushed it back inside. Neither made much of the exchange, but it had become easy for them to notice small things about one another without announcing that they were doing it.

Back at the Institute, the workshop was still open. Daniel marked the cart's mounting points on a full-size drawing. Tyler laid out the measurements and the photographs. Marcus found a length of steel stock in the materials rack and brought it over, then waited while Daniel checked whether it was suitable. Dani went to the equipment store and returned with the old blue cloth wrapped around a clean piece of paper so that it would not pick up machine oil.

Above the workbench, a large sign had been leaning against the wall for months. Its lettering was old and uneven, but the words could still be read: **NOTHING PERSONAL**. Someone had originally painted it for the repair room, as a reminder that a failed design was not a judgement on the person who had made it. The sign had never been hung because the workshop doors had been moved during the last renovation. The Coach looked at it, then at the empty wall above the new bench.

“Seems like it's found its place.”

They cleaned the sign, tightened the old frame, and fixed it above the main worktable. It was large enough to read from the open doors. Dani stood back to check that it was level. Tyler moved the ladder half a foot so the frame could be reached without leaning. Daniel wrote the date of installation on the reverse, along with the names of the men who had helped hang it. The front remained plain. Nothing personal.

The first handle assembly took most of the afternoon. Daniel's side plates used the existing mounting points and held the removable crossbar in two indexed positions. The pin was captive so it could not be misplaced during a changeover. Tyler checked the dimensions against the cart photographs and noticed that the lower position would bring the grip too close to the frame when the cart turned sharply. They moved the lower hole upward and repeated the drawing. Dani tested the position against his own reach, then stepped aside so Marcus could try it. The design was not being made for Dani's body. It was being checked by several people before it returned to Elias for a test with the person who used the cart every day.

The first mock-up passed the unloaded movement test on the workshop floor. It did not yet pass the full review. Daniel wanted a load test, a check on the existing mounting plates, and a trial over a rough surface before the assembly could be treated as ready. Tyler added those conditions to the sheet. The Coach read it and nodded.

“That's the part I like. You can be excited about an idea and still know what you haven't checked.”

Dani wiped a line of metal dust from the bench with the side of his hand. “We'll finish the tests tomorrow.”

“And then?” Marcus asked.

“Then we take it back to the market.”

The Coach looked at the men around the table. “And the repair bench?”

Dani glanced at Daniel. “We can run the first one with Elias. One afternoon. A few items. We see what works and what doesn't.”

Daniel had already begun drafting the intake sheet. Tyler was writing the conditions for returning an item to its owner. Marcus had taken the apples from the paper bag and set them in a bowl near the door. Nobody had declared a new department. There was no plaque, no ribbon, and no announcement that the Institute had solved the market's maintenance problem. There was a tested mock-up, a list of work still to do, a possible afternoon in the market, and a large sign on a wall that had finally acquired a purpose.

Later, when the workshop had emptied, Tyler found Dani checking the pin one last time. He stood beside him and watched the pin enter the upper hole, turn, and seat against the stop. Dani checked it twice, then let go.

“You do inspect things twice,” Tyler said.

“Only the things that can fall on me.”

Tyler smiled. “Good policy.”

They left the workshop together. Outside, the rain had passed completely. The covered walk was drying in patches, and the last water drops fell from the roof into the narrow drain beside the cedar beds. Dani held the door while Tyler carried the notebook through. Neither hurried. The training floor had closed for the evening, but the dining hall was still open, and the Coach had left a note on the workshop table saying that the first market repair afternoon should be scheduled only after Elias had approved the test.

The Institute had gained no new building that day. It had gained a route, a possible partner, a working design question, and a reason to keep one bench clear for people who had not yet arrived. Dani put the blue cloth in the drawer with the drawings. Tyler wrote the next test on the board. Daniel left a space for Elias's signature. The Coach turned off the last lamp and stopped beneath the sign.

“Nothing personal,” he said, reading it once more.

Then he shut the workshop door, leaving the new work ready for the morning.
