# The Institute — The Passage Between Buildings

The first thing the five men discovered after the Living Ledger acquired its event adapter was that the Institute did not experience its life in departments. The gymnasium had its own hours, the dining hall its own service, the residences their own routines, and the workshops their own queues, but the men moved between them without noticing the boundaries. A training session ended with a conversation about a broken bench. The broken bench became a workshop request. The workshop request delayed a delivery. The delay changed where the evening meal was served. A man who had come to the gym for an hour found himself helping carry tables across the courtyard. The institution was already connected. Their software was the part that had not caught up.

They met in the long room above the workshop, where the windows looked down over the covered walk between the gymnasium and the residential buildings. The rain had left the bricks dark and the timber railings almost black. Below them, two residents crossed with gym bags over their shoulders, one carrying a paper parcel from the kitchen. The Coach had arranged coffee, fruit, toasted bread and a large plate of eggs at the end of the table. He listened while the men described the difference between recording an event and allowing its consequences to reach another place.

Luke had brought a simple event form. It asked for an identity, an occurrence time, a place, participants, a kind, and a description. He had removed the temptation to make every event fit a narrow template. A training session could be recorded as training; a meal could be a meal; a repair could remain a repair. The event could carry a small payload when a more specific system needed additional information, but the common record did not need to know every detail of every activity.

Tyler examined the identifier rules. The same event could be submitted twice if a browser retried after a connection interruption, but it must not appear twice in the shared history. A retry with the same identity and identical content should return the original event. A retry that reused the identity for different content should fail loudly. He wrote the distinction on the whiteboard, then underlined it. The Institute could tolerate a delayed update. It could not afford a quiet contradiction in its memory.

Chase insisted that the source be visible. A resident's account, an instructor's observation, a maintenance report and an automated camera measurement could all refer to the same occurrence, but they were not interchangeable. The source had to travel with the event. If a later system derived a consequence, it would have to say which event and which rule had produced it. A maintenance queue could be updated by a repair report; it could not invent a completed repair merely because the request had been seen.

Gabe began drawing the route of an event through the campus. He used small circles for places and lines for passages. The gymnasium, changing room, dining hall, residences, library, courtyard, baseball field and workshops each had a stable identity. Northgate University and the Dad Convent appeared beyond the Institute's grounds, connected by visits and correspondence rather than by a claim that their buildings belonged to the same campus. A passage could carry a person or an artifact between places without dissolving the distinction between them.

Andrew kept returning to the body. He wanted the system to understand that a training event did not end when a man put the weight down. There might be a recovery note, a change in his next session, a different fit in his shirt, or a decision to take the covered walk more slowly. Those consequences belonged to different records, and none should be invented automatically. The event system could make the relationship available; the man, his account, or a clearly identified observation would supply the evidence.

They chose a small test that afternoon. Two residents, Luke and Andrew, completed a shared movement session in the gymnasium. The session was recorded once, with both men as participants and the gymnasium as its place. Andrew added his account. Luke added a note about the equipment arrangement. The event appeared in the canonical activity stream and produced one artifact with the same identity. When the test submitted the same event again, the history remained unchanged. When they altered the description but kept the old identity, the writer rejected it.

The result was modest, but it gave them a reliable foundation. They could now record an Institute occurrence without creating a private ledger that would later have to be reconciled with the Tractus. The next task was not to make every building write to every other building. It was to create small, rebuildable projections that listened to the same canonical history: a residence daybook, a gym session view, a workshop queue, a place timeline and a character history. Each projection would remember the last event it had processed and could be reconstructed if it became inconsistent.

The Coach asked them to demonstrate the idea without a screen. Andrew described the session. Luke described the equipment note. Chase asked where each account came from. Tyler explained how the duplicate retry had been handled. Gabe pointed to the route on the whiteboard. The Coach looked through the window towards the residences.

"All right," he said. "Now show me where the men go after they leave here."

They went downstairs. The covered walk was wide enough for two men to pass without turning sideways, and its roof made a low, steady sound as drops fell from the eaves. At the gym entrance, a resident was waiting for a friend who had gone to collect a clean shirt. A little farther along, the workshop doors were open. A carpenter had placed two unfinished shelves against the wall, with a note asking whether the dining hall wanted them before the next supper. The dining hall itself smelled of onions, pepper and warm bread. Someone had put a pot of beans on early, and the kitchen staff were discussing whether the evening meal should be served in the main room or the courtyard.

In the residence common room, three men were arranging a film night. One wanted something frightening, another preferred an old comedy, and the third had brought a list of films that were all too long for a weekday. Their disagreement had no importance to the event system until someone chose to record it, but it mattered to the life of the place. The Institute was not a diagram waiting for data. It was already full of preferences, interruptions, jokes, small obligations and plans that would never become formal projects.

At the end of the walk, the Coach stopped beside the noticeboard. A visiting lecturer from Northgate had left a card announcing a talk on the history of athletic architecture. Someone had written beneath it that the Institute's original gymnasium ought to be included. The Coach read the note twice, then asked who had written it. Nobody knew. He left it there. The unknown author could come forward if he wished; the Institute did not need to turn every anonymous contribution into an identity.

That evening, the five men returned to the programming room. They divided the next work into separate, testable pieces. Luke would define the event contract and the first projection interface. Tyler would write replay and duplicate-processing tests. Chase would specify provenance and the distinction between observation, account and derived consequence. Gabe would preserve the place graph and recurring routes. Andrew would define the training and recovery projection, with first-person accounts kept beside measurements.

They did not call the work complete. The event adapter was a new bridge into the existing Tractus writer; the campus projections still needed to be built, connected to their consumers and tested against replay, interruption and conflicting updates. The visual interface would follow the same rule. It should reveal a place and the changes that had reached it, not pretend that every connection was already live.

Outside, the dining hall filled gradually. The film-night argument continued in the common room. A resident carried a plate of bread towards the workshop, where the carpenter was still measuring the shelves. Andrew noticed that the man had taken the longer route because the gym corridor was busy. He mentioned it to Gabe, who added another line to the map.

The Institute had always been connected by its people. The next stage of the Tractus would make those passages legible, durable and available to the next thing they built.
