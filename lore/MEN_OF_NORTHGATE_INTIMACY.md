# The Rooms Above the Arcade

Northgate's social life does not end when the Long Table is cleared. Above the covered arcade, the Founders Dormitory and the newer river residences remain warm with activity well into the night: showers running after training, kettles clicking off, music travelling through open doors, men borrowing a clean shirt from a friend, and couples deciding whether to walk back together or stay where they are. The university's adult residents are not expected to conceal that they desire one another. Men date men, fall in love, have sex, share rooms, separate, reconcile, and begin again. Male-male intercourse is an ordinary part of the life of the campus, neither a scandal nor a compulsory performance. The Tractus treats it as one of the ways adult people make intimacy real, alongside conversation, care, humour, domestic habit, and the decision to remain.

The rooms are private, and the people in them decide what becomes public. The campus does not turn a couple's intimacy into a spectacle, and nobody is entitled to details because they have seen two men leave dinner together. Consent is active and mutual; affection does not create ownership, and a relationship does not cancel either person's independent life. The men speak plainly when they need to slow down, stop, change their minds, or ask for something different. They also learn to speak plainly about pleasure, attraction, embarrassment, and the simple wish to be wanted. This frankness makes the place more relaxed, not less romantic.

Emil and Tomas have become familiar figures at the late table. Their spare-key arrangement has developed into a shared domestic rhythm: Tomas often returns first, opens the windows, and puts rice on to cook; Emil arrives later from the print room with ink on his fingers and a paper parcel from the bakery. They eat together, wash the dishes, and usually spend the remainder of the evening in the same room. Their physical relationship has grown naturally from their trust. They have sex often, sometimes after a long evening of talking and sometimes in the middle of an otherwise ordinary day when they have both decided that they want one another. Neither treats this as proof that the relationship is secure. The proof is in the repeated care: asking, listening, making room, and remembering what the other has said.

Marek Ionescu and Adrian Varga are less settled. They began by meeting after strength training, then discovered that they preferred one another's company away from the gym. Their attraction is direct, but their temperaments are different: Marek likes to plan, Adrian is more inclined to decide when the moment arrives. They have learned not to interpret every difference as a problem to solve. One Friday, after a late meal of roast peppers, potatoes, and grilled fish, Adrian asks Marek to stay. They spend the night together, and in the morning Marek makes coffee while Adrian searches the kitchen for the honey. They do not announce a new status to their friends. The change is visible in small things: an extra toothbrush, a hand resting briefly at the back of a chair, and a habit of checking whether the other has eaten.

Dan Roman's story remains his own. He is admired, and he knows it, but admiration is not the same as intimacy. He has several friends who are attracted to him, and he is free to decide which invitations interest him. His first serious connection at Northgate begins with a conversation in the winter garden, where he and Noah Bell spend an hour discussing a book neither has finished. Their friendship develops through meals, walks, and a shared workshop project. When they eventually sleep together, it is not presented as a reward for persistence or as the inevitable outcome of attention. They have talked about what they want, and both choose the encounter. In the weeks that follow, they continue to disagree about books, arrive late to breakfast, and work on the same project. Their intimacy adds another dimension to the friendship rather than replacing it.

The Long Table has its own quiet customs. Couples sit together when they want to, but nobody reserves a permanent place as a declaration of possession. Friends tease one another without demanding a confession. A man may mention that he is seeing someone, or that he spent the night with a friend, without the whole table turning to inspect him. The cooks know which residents are likely to arrive late and keep a covered plate in the warmer. Sometimes two men come down together in the morning, hair still damp, and take breakfast without explanation. Sometimes one comes alone. Both are ordinary.

There are also men who prefer casual encounters, men who want long partnerships, men who are still learning what they want, and men who choose not to have sex at all. The campus makes room for these differences. Its health service offers confidential sexual-health advice, testing referrals, and practical information without moral judgement. Condoms and other safer-sex supplies are available discreetly. The purpose is not to turn intimacy into administration, but to make care accessible before anyone needs to ask for it.

The same pattern appears beyond Northgate. At the Geatish House, adult companions form relationships that are not dictated by the old heroic hierarchy. The company is capable of affection without demanding that every bond become public. On the coast, Asta and his partner have made a household around the boatyard, where mornings begin with tar, coffee, and the sound of tools being sorted. Their private life is not separated from their work; it is one of the reasons the workshop has become a place people want to return to. The men who travel between settlements bring letters, news, and invitations, not gossip extracted from closed doors.

The Tractus contains many kinds of male intimacy. Some encounters are brief and joyful; some become enduring partnerships; some are complicated and require difficult conversations. Sexual pleasure is not treated as a prize for strength, beauty, status, or narrative importance. It belongs to the adults who choose it. The world can be physically affectionate and openly gay without reducing every man to a body or every relationship to a scene. The point is abundance: more rooms with their lamps lit, more invitations freely given, more mornings in which two men make breakfast together, and more lives that can include desire without being governed by it.

The following seasons should keep this life present in the narrative rather than confining it to a single chapter. Let couples meet in ordinary places, let attraction sometimes become sex, let established partners continue to enjoy one another, and let the aftermath matter: a shared shower, a borrowed shirt, a missed breakfast, a tender disagreement, a new confidence, or a decision to remain friends. These are events in the Tractus. They are not detachable decorations, and their inscription belongs to the same moment as their occurrence.


## After Training

The group began almost accidentally, in the changing rooms above the athletic commons, where a handful of regulars had developed the habit of staying behind after the evening session. They were conspicuous men: broad through the shoulders, thick-legged, strong from years of lifting, and generally more comfortable being looked at than they admitted. Their training briefs were part of that confidence. They liked the close athletic cut, the clean lines, the bright colours that appeared in the sports shops each season, and the small rituals of choosing what to wear before a session. The subject might have remained ordinary gym conversation if one of them had not made a joke about how seriously they discussed their underwear while pretending to be entirely serious about everything else.

The humour gradually acquired a second subject. These men had a shared taste for crude fart jokes and for the comic awkwardness surrounding an involuntary bodily function. They found the contrast irresistible: imposing physiques, carefully planned training, immaculate kit, and a sense of humour that could collapse into helpless laughter over the least dignified sound. For a few of them, that humour also intersected with a private fetish. They were willing to acknowledge it among one another without pretending it was universal or expecting anyone else to participate. The attraction was bound up with trust, familiarity and the freedom to be ridiculous in front of men whose respect they already had.

Their evenings developed a recognisable rhythm. The last sets finished, plates were returned to their racks, and the men drifted upstairs with towels over their shoulders and gym bags knocking against their legs. Someone usually had a new pair of briefs to show; someone else had an opinion about the waistband, the colour or whether the cut was suited to training. The conversation moved between serious matters—programmes, recovery, competitions, meals—and the jokes that had become their private language. A perfectly ordinary remark could set them off again. The room's atmosphere was less like a secret society than a group of friends who had discovered an unusually reliable way to make one another laugh.

There was no formal membership list and no official name on a door. The men sometimes called themselves the Briefs Club, mostly because the phrase made the whole thing sound more organised than it was. New company was never assumed to be interested. A newcomer might hear the jokes and laugh along, or might make it clear that the subject was not for him; either response was accepted without interrogation. Anything involving a personal fetish required a direct invitation and mutual agreement. The group understood the difference between sharing a joke and making someone the unwilling centre of one, and they kept their private interests private.

A few familiar faces gave the circle its character. Leon, a heavyweight rower with a remarkably calm expression, was the one most likely to arrive with a new colourway and ask for an absurdly technical verdict. Pavel, whose legs were famous across the athletic commons, claimed to dislike the entire conversation but never missed an evening. Niko had the quickest laugh and the worst timing, often managing to make a solemn discussion of protein intake impossible to continue. They were not caricatures of gym men; they had coursework, jobs, partners, sore shoulders, laundry to collect and friends outside the group. The private joke occupied one corner of lives that were otherwise broad and ordinary.

The group occasionally met at the late café after training. They took the large table near the windows, ordered substantial food and continued their conversation while the campus moved around them. Other residents recognised them as the men who trained late and laughed loudly. Nobody needed an explanation. If the subject came up in public, they kept it at the level of ordinary humour; the more personal part remained in private. Their partners and friends could know about the interest without being expected to share it. That distinction mattered to them. Being comfortable with one another did not mean surrendering discretion.

Over time, the group became a small Northgate example of a wider principle: adult desire is varied, sometimes unexpected, and not always dignified in the way people imagine romance or attraction ought to be. The Tractus does not need to make every preference grand or symbolic. Sometimes a few muscular men finish a demanding session, change into their favourite briefs, eat late, and laugh themselves hoarse over a subject that would never appear in a university prospectus. The world is large enough to contain that, too.
