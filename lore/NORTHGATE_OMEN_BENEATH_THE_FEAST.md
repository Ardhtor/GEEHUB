# The Omen Beneath the Feast

Status: developing canon / Northgate cycle

The first warning was so small that nobody agreed it had happened.

At the Great Feast, when the last platters were being carried away and the musicians had begun to play more softly, every flame along the eastern arcade leaned toward the Lion Gate. There was no wind in the square. The banners hung still. The lamps bent as if a great door had opened somewhere far below the stones and drawn one long breath.

The men noticed it at different times. Dan Roman stopped halfway through a sentence. One of the Geats turned his cup slowly in his hand. At the end of the Long Table, the AI Coach looked up from his meal. His usual ease did not leave him; it simply became attentive. He exhaled through his nose, a quiet, thoughtful sound, and watched the flames recover.

Then the feast continued.

That was what made the incident troublesome afterward. Nothing broke. No stranger entered. No voice came from the gate. The cooks cleared the tables, the musicians packed their instruments, and the guests returned to their rooms. Yet the next morning, three separate people described the same sensation: that the square had briefly become much larger than it appeared, and that something beyond its visible boundaries had noticed them.

## The Quiet After the Feast

Northgate did not become a frightened place. Its ordinary life held. Breakfast was served; the river carts arrived; lectures began; the athletic commons opened. The coach greeted the first men through the doors in his familiar American voice. He asked how they had slept, remembered the previous session where the record allowed, and did not turn the strange night into a spectacle.

But the campus had acquired a second attention.

The gardeners found a narrow line of frost beneath the eastern arcade although the rest of the square was wet with autumn rain. A porter heard a measured knock from inside the sealed bell chamber. In the library, a catalogue drawer that had resisted opening for years slid out without effort. Inside was a blank card, warm to the touch, bearing only a small impression like the paw of a lion.

The four granite lions at the gate remained where they had always been. The outer pair faced the city. The inner pair watched the university. No one could say that their expressions had changed, but several people found themselves avoiding the habit of looking directly into their stone eyes.

## The Society's Response

The men did what a society does when it encounters something it cannot yet explain. They compared observations. They checked the lamps, inspected the bell chamber, questioned the night staff, and walked the perimeter in daylight. The engineers found no fault in the electrical system. The groundskeepers found no animal tracks in the frost. The librarians searched the old catalogues and discovered that the blank card had no paper grain, watermark or known method of manufacture.

The Great Feast had brought together people who ordinarily occupied different parts of the world. Now those connections became useful. The Geats knew old stories about halls that heard their own names spoken from beyond the walls. The archivists knew how to distinguish an old record from a convincing forgery. The engineers knew how to test a building without damaging it. The cooks, who had been awake before dawn, knew that the eastern ovens had gone cold for exactly seven minutes and then relit themselves.

No council declared an emergency. Instead, a small observatory group was formed, with representatives from the gate staff, the library, engineering, the Geatish House and the university's night service. Their first rule was to record what happened without improving it for the sake of a better story.

## The Coach at the Eastern Arcade

On the third evening, the coach stayed after the last training session. The strength hall had emptied; the rubber floor held the faint warmth of the day's work. He put the weights away, checked the benches, and walked with an unhurried heaviness toward the eastern arcade.

He was not afraid, and he did not pretend to understand. He stood beneath the lamps with his hands resting at his sides. A slow breath left him. For once, the familiar sigh sounded less like satisfaction after exertion and more like a man listening for something at a great distance.

The Wand's cursor trembled over the map. A new line had appeared beneath the university, extending eastward beyond the known foundations. It was not a route anyone had drawn. It had no label, no destination and no measured length. When the cursor approached it, the line receded by the same distance.

The coach called the user over only when there was something worth seeing. He did not dramatise the discovery. He said, quietly, that the building seemed to have more underneath it than the plans admitted. Then he waited. The silence between them was not empty; it was the interval in which a decision could be made.

They did not follow the line that night.

## The Measure of Foreboding

The Tractus records this as an omen, not an explanation. The distinction matters. An omen is an occurrence whose meaning has not yet arrived. It may point toward danger, invitation, recognition, or a change so large that the present world cannot yet describe it. The record must not decide in advance.

The signs recur in restrained forms: flames leaning without wind; frost where no cold has passed; a bell answering from a sealed room; a blank catalogue card; a line beneath the map that withdraws from inspection. Each sign is independently small. Together they create pressure.

Northgate's people continue to eat, study, train, repair, court, argue, sleep and gather. The feast is not cancelled. The coach does not become a prophet. The Geats do not reach for weapons merely because an old story has supplied a familiar shape. Their response is attention, preparation and the preservation of ordinary life.

Yet the Lion Gate is no longer only an entrance. It is also a boundary whose other side may be aware of the people who pass beneath it.

## Continuity Rule: Do Not Spend the Omen Too Soon

Future chapters should allow the foreboding to deepen through consequence rather than immediate revelation. Each return should alter one small fact: a new mark in the archive, a change in the map, a witness who remembers a sound, a room that seems longer at night, or a familiar character who recognises something without knowing why. No single sign should settle the mystery.

The world remains inhabited while the mystery gathers. The next feast may be warmer, louder and more crowded than the last. Someone will bring a new dish. Someone will arrive late. The coach may yawn beside the fire and ask whether anyone has eaten. Beneath the ordinary sounds, the eastern lamps may once again lean toward the gate.

The Tractus does not force the door open.

It records that something, somewhere, has begun to knock.
