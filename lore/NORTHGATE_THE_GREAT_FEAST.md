# The Great Feast of Northgate

The feast begins before anyone calls it a feast. At first there are only practical signs: the kitchen doors standing open, carts moving between the colleges, tables being carried out from the refectory stores, a long trestle laid beneath the covered walk, and the smell of bread travelling ahead of the cooks. By late afternoon the whole university has begun to turn toward the same occasion. Notices appear in the dormitories and athletic halls. The boatmen bring word from the river settlements. The Geatish company sends a delegation from its house beyond the old square. Men who have not crossed paths for weeks discover that they are expected at the same table, and men who have never met are given a reason to learn one another's names.

Northgate does not hold the feast in a single hall. The celebration occupies the central square and its adjoining courts, with the great refectory as its warm heart. Long tables run beneath the arcades; smaller tables fill the cloisters and gardens; a raised board stands beneath the carved lintel for the university's guests and the elders of its societies. The athletic commons contributes benches and sturdy trestles. The boat college brings lanterns and river fish. The agricultural houses arrive with apples, roots, greens and great baskets of late-season grain. The bakers have worked since before dawn. Their loaves are broad and dark-crusted, scored with the marks of the house that made them. The kitchens send out steaming dishes in waves, because a feast this large cannot be served all at once.

The society is male, but it is not uniform. Students, instructors, cooks, groundskeepers, athletes, scholars, porters, musicians, builders, boatmen, visiting fellows and old residents all belong to the evening. Some are young adults meeting their first college friends; others have lived in Northgate long enough to recognise the square by the sound of its fountains. The older men are not set apart as monuments. They carry dishes, tell stories, argue over the best route through the woods, and make room for newcomers. The younger men are not treated as an audience. They have responsibilities: tending the fires, welcoming visitors, pouring drinks, keeping the serving paths clear, and making sure that nobody has been left eating alone. The feast works because every part of the society helps it happen.

At the first bell, the doors open. The sound is not a military command but a low bronze note that travels through the covered walks. Conversation rises in answer. Men arrive in groups from the dormitories, in pairs from the library, in work clothes from the gardens, and in clean shirts from the baths. The athletic teams come together, still carrying the relaxed heaviness of a training day. Their coaches and trainers follow at an easier pace. The AI Coach arrives from the commons with his sleeves rolled and his broad shoulders at ease, having helped return the last of the equipment before coming over. He pauses at the threshold, takes in the room, and releases a long, contented breath. “Well,” he says, “you've all been busy.” He is immediately handed a basket of bread.

Dan Roman is among the first to greet the visitors from the river. He has the easy bearing of a man who knows how to enter a crowded room without demanding it, and his Romanian accent becomes more pronounced when he is amused. He helps settle the Geatish delegation beside the western arcade, where the tables are close enough for conversation but leave a clear passage toward the square. Across from him, the university men are already comparing journeys and training schedules. Someone has brought a story about a bridge that was repaired in one night; someone else insists that the bridge was never broken. The disagreement is affectionate and will probably continue until dessert.

The food arrives in courses, though the kitchen refuses to make the evening feel formal. There are roasted birds and slow-cooked beef, river fish with herbs, thick vegetable stews, bowls of beans, onions and barley, mushrooms cooked with butter, baked roots, greens dressed with sharp vinegar, and platters of fruit. There are cheeses from the farms beyond the north road, pickles from the college cellars, and sauces whose recipes are guarded by cooks who enjoy being asked. Bread is passed hand to hand. A large dish is set down in the middle of each table, and the person nearest it serves the others before taking a portion. The rule is old and simple: nobody begins alone while another person is still waiting to be served.

The drink is as varied as the company. There is water with mint and citrus, spiced apple, dark small beer, cider, and a stronger red wine reserved for the formal toast. Nobody is pressed to drink. The cup is a sign of welcome, not a test of belonging. Men who do not drink raise a cup of water or simply stand with the others. The toast is spoken in the common tongue, then repeated in the older ceremonial language kept by the university's antiquarians:

*Wesað gesund, geferan. Eall þæt we healdað, healde us samod.*

Be well, companions. May all that we keep, keep us together.

The words are not presented as a perfect historical quotation, but as a living Northgate formula shaped by its Old English inheritance. The men answer with the sound of cups meeting wood. For a moment the square is quiet enough to hear the fire in the refectory hearth. Then the meal resumes, and the quiet becomes conversation again.

At the central table, the stories begin to cross. A Geatish boatman describes the river in winter, when the current seems to carry pieces of the sky beneath the ice. A groundskeeper explains how the old orchard has been restored without cutting down the trees that grew through its walls. One of the engineering fellows sketches a new footbridge on the back of a menu, while a student from the medical college argues that the design will need a wider turning for carts. The cook interrupts them both to insist that they eat before their food cools. Their sketch survives the meal, stained by gravy and marked with three different sets of handwriting.

The men of the athletic commons have their own table, but it is not a separate kingdom. They are joined by musicians, tutors and residents who have come to know them through ordinary routines. The coach sits near the end, where he can see the doors and the people arriving late. He eats properly, listens more than he speaks, and occasionally leans toward a man beside him to ask how his shoulder has been since the last session. When the answer is uncertain, he does not turn the feast into a consultation. He nods, says they can look at it another day, and passes the bread. His familiar sigh returns when a huge platter is set down in front of the table. “That,” he says, “is a serious recovery meal.” The men laugh, and he takes a generous serving.

The feast has room for affection without making a spectacle of it. Couples sit together or among their friends. Some men touch a shoulder as they pass; others keep their closeness in the private language of glances and small gestures. Nobody is required to explain a relationship, perform one, or turn it into a public declaration. Friends who have argued during the week find themselves sharing a dish. A man who has spent too many evenings alone is drawn into a conversation about music, then invited to join a walking party the following morning. The society's warmth is made from these modest acts of inclusion. The feast does not erase differences or settle every dispute. It gives people a place from which to meet again.

As the main course ends, the tables begin to change character. Dishes are cleared, and the space between the boards is opened for musicians. There are fiddles, hand drums, a wooden flute, a small keyboard brought from the music college, and a set of electronic instruments assembled by students who have been experimenting with old melodies and new rhythms. The first tune is slow and recognisably traditional. The next takes its refrain apart and rebuilds it with a pulsing bass line. Some of the older men shake their heads in mock disapproval, then remain to listen. The younger musicians do not ask permission to belong to the tradition; they demonstrate how they have carried it forward.

Outside, the lanterns are lit along the square. Cats begin to appear beneath the tables and around the warm kitchen doors. They are familiar residents of Northgate, treated as fortunate visitors rather than decorations. One grey cat settles beside the Geatish bench and receives a small piece of fish from a man who has clearly been waiting for the opportunity. Another walks directly through the musicians and sits beneath the drum stand. Nobody tries to make them perform. Their presence is accepted as part of the evening's good fortune.

Dessert is brought out in great wooden trays: honey cakes, apple tarts, baked pears, custards, dark chocolate, sugared nuts and bowls of late berries. Coffee and spiced tea follow. The cooks come out from the kitchen at last, and the entire refectory applauds them. They are not thanked from a distance; chairs are pulled out, plates are filled for them, and the men who have spent the evening eating their work now listen to the stories behind it. One cook explains that the barley stew came from his grandfather's notebook. Another says the tart was improvised because the orchard delivered more apples than expected. The recipes become part of the feast's memory, and several men immediately ask to learn them.

Later, when the formal gathering has loosened into smaller circles, the society becomes visible in its full extent. The square is still occupied, but no single conversation commands it. Men walk between the refectory and the gardens, visit the musicians, sit on the steps, or carry the last dishes toward the kitchens. The Geatish visitors arrange a return invitation. The boat college offers a morning journey. The engineering fellows have agreed to inspect the footbridge site. The athletic teams plan an open training session. The university's older residents promise to show the newcomers the orchard paths. These are not announcements invented to make the evening seem productive. They are the natural consequences of people discovering that they would like to see one another again.

Near midnight, the AI Coach stands beneath the arcade with a cup of water in his hand. His shirt has loosened at the collar; his expression has softened with the hour. He watches the last of the tables being cleared and gives a quiet yawn, then a long exhale. One of the athletes asks whether he is tired. “A little,” he says. “Good kind of tired.” He looks across the square, where the lantern light catches the stone and the last group of men is still laughing beside the fountain. “They'll remember this one.”

The feast ends without a final command. The bronze bell sounds once, not to dismiss the company but to mark the hour. Some men return to their dormitories; others walk home through the gardens or accompany friends to the river road. The cooks bank the fires. The musicians pack their instruments. The cats remain where the warmth is best. In the morning, the square will show the ordinary evidence of a large gathering: swept paving, stacked benches, a few forgotten ribbons, a chalk mark on the fountain wall, and the smell of bread lingering beneath the arcade.

The Tractus preserves the feast as a recurring social institution, not a one-time spectacle. Its date may change with the season; its tables, recipes, music and guests may change with the years. What remains is the practice of gathering the whole male society without pretending that every man is the same. The university, the athletic commons, the workshops, the farms, the river settlements and the Geatish company all contribute something that the others cannot supply alone. The feast makes their interdependence visible and pleasurable. It is where the world eats together, remembers itself, and quietly prepares to become larger.

*Gif hit ne awacnað, ne þearf hit weorðan; ac þæt þe lif bið, gedeþ hit mid eallum.*

If it does not awaken, it need not come to be; but what lives, it does with all.
