# Reader Care, Medication, and Emotional Pace
**Status:** active editorial principle across GEEHUB
**Scope:** fiction, lore, character writing, dialogue, interface copy, and reading experiences

GEEHUB should be welcoming to readers who take antidepressants, readers who are having a difficult day, and readers who simply want a humane place to spend time. This is an editorial and accessibility commitment, not medical advice and not an assumption that every reader has the same experience.

## The writing

Keep warmth, humour, appetite, attraction, physical confidence, discovery, friendship, romance, and the pleasure of inhabiting a well-made place. Do not flatten the world into bland reassurance. Let characters be interesting, funny, ambitious, occasionally awkward, and capable of change.

Prefer emotional steadiness over relentless escalation. Give scenes room to settle. Let meals be eaten, journeys take time, rooms become familiar, and conversations end without every moment needing a revelation. Avoid using humiliation, abandonment, panic, despair, or threat as automatic engines of momentum. Difficult experiences may appear when they belong to the story, but they should be handled with context, agency, and room for recovery.

Use clear, natural prose. Avoid confusing temporal jumps, excessive sensory overload, or long runs of hostile dialogue. Keep chapter paragraphs substantial and continuous, while making the reading experience easy to follow. Humour should invite the reader in rather than make a vulnerable character the target.

## Characters and antidepressants

Some adult characters may take antidepressants. Treat medication as an ordinary part of their lives, not a defining identity, a moral failing, a joke, a magical cure, or a plot device that can be casually started or stopped. Do not imply that medication erases personality, attraction, ambition, masculinity, creativity, or emotional depth. Characters can have good days and difficult days while taking medication. Their experiences differ; no single character stands for everyone.

Do not prescribe, recommend doses, advise discontinuation, or invent medical certainty. If a plot touches medication decisions, side effects, withdrawal, or crisis care, keep the scene human and encourage appropriate professional support within the fiction rather than presenting the narrative as a treatment guide.

## Reading comfort

- Offer a sense of welcome and continuity; let readers return without needing to catch up on every detail.
- Balance energetic scenes with quieter chapters and satisfying ordinary routines.
- Keep conflict proportionate and give characters practical ways to respond.
- Preserve pleasure: good food, comfortable rooms, companionship, cats, craft, exercise, music, affection, and small accomplishments.
- Avoid implying that a reader must feel better, be productive, or respond positively to deserve care.
- Do not assume a reader is medicated, unwell, or seeking therapy.

## Continuity rule

Care does not mean removing complexity. It means writing complexity without cruelty and allowing people to remain more than the hardest thing that has happened to them.
