Do not flatten the body into a barrel, shrink the legs, or lose either man's identity.

I come through the workshop door while the narrator is halfway through explaining the model.

"Hold on. Stop there. You're doing it again."

The chest is already broad, the shoulders are pronounced, and the arms have been given enough volume to make the silhouette impressive from the front. From the side, though, I can see the problem immediately.

"You're building a pose. I'm asking for a body."

I walk around the platform. The back has to carry the chest. The shoulders have to connect to the upper arm without turning into separate pieces. The rib cage, waist, pelvis, and legs have to share the same weight. Hands and feet matter. Neck and head matter. If the frame changes, the whole man has to remain recognisable while the proportions develop around him.

"Start at the floor," I tell the rigging team. "Show me where the weight goes."

They switch the model from its turntable pose to a standing test. The feet settle. The knees make small corrections. The pelvis finds its balance above them; the spine and rib cage respond; the shoulders stop looking like ornaments attached to the torso. A breath moves through the chest and back. The hands relax instead of remaining fixed in a presentation pose.

"Better. Now walk him."

The first step exposes another weakness. The torso moves, but the arms lag behind. The shirt stretches in one place and clips through the shoulder in another. I point at the screen.

"That's not one problem. That's the rig telling you the systems aren't talking to each other."

Coach is watching from the doorway. He comes closer and studies the motion.

"Whole body, one continuous movement," he says.

"Exactly. Skeleton, muscle volumes, soft tissue, skin, clothing, and the floor response. Separate systems under the surface, one coherent man in the scene."

I ask them to run the model through several ordinary states: standing at ease, walking across the room, bracing to lift, completing a controlled repetition, sitting down, and recovering afterward. Each state has to preserve the same face, height, limb relationships, and recognisable silhouette. The muscles change shape under effort; the skin follows without becoming a hard shell; the clothing reacts to the body beneath it; the feet and surrounding surfaces register contact and weight. The lower torso and pelvis remain part of the complete adult anatomy, not a missing section or a disconnected accessory. The simulation should preserve anatomical continuity without turning private anatomy into a spectacle.

The model walks again. This time the shoulders counter-rotate, the chest follows the breath, the hips and legs carry the load, and the shirt responds to the movement. When the figure stops, he does not snap back into a statue. His balance settles. His breathing slows. He remains the same man.

"That's what I wanted. Don't give me a perfect still image and call it finished. Give me a body that can live in the room."

I take the chalk from the bench and write across the board: **THE WHOLE BODY REMEMBERS THE CHANGE.** Then I add a second line beneath it: **EVERY MOVEMENT MUST BELONG TO THE SAME PERSON.**

"And don't forget the room. If the man grows, the bench, doorway, training equipment, camera distance, and floor contact all have to be checked. The environment is part of the test. I want to see what changes around him, not just what happens to the mesh."

Coach nods toward the open doors. Beyond the workshop, Heorot is busy: men crossing the courtyard, someone carrying a tray from the kitchen, two others arguing over a training schedule. The model is no longer an isolated asset. It is ready to enter a place with other people in it.
