# The Working Artifacts of Tractus

The world does not live by chapters alone. It leaves things behind. A key warms in a pocket. A market tally is folded into a coat. A chair bears the marks of a repair made by someone who did not ask who would sit there. A letter crosses three districts and arrives with rain in its crease. These objects are not decoration around the story. They are small working instruments through which the story passes from one person to another.

This collection is the first working cabinet of Tractus artifacts. Each object has a use, a maker or keeper, a material history, and a set of relationships. Some are mundane. Their importance comes from being handled repeatedly. Others are ceremonial without becoming magical in the narrow sense. They hold a promise, authorize a passage, record a debt, preserve a name, or make a place available to someone who has not yet arrived.

An artifact may be fictional within the world and still have a real source record here. The distinction matters: the object exists in the fiction; this file is its repository inscription. A future implementation may give these objects images, audio, inventory records, state histories, and navigable links. Until then, the text is the confirmed artifact record.

## 1. The Northgate Passage Key

The key is longer than a dormitory key and shorter than a gate key, cut from dark iron with a flattened oval bow. Four shallow grooves cross the bow. They are not decorative. They mark the four maintenance crews who have held the key since the eastern passage was opened: stone, water, light, and doors.

It does not open the Lion Gate itself. The gate is never locked against the public. The key opens the narrow service door beside the western paw, a door most visitors mistake for a seam in the masonry. Behind it is a passage used by porters, groundskeepers, night stewards, and the men who inspect the old drainage channel after heavy rain.

The current keeper is Tomas Venn, a senior porter at Northgate. He carries the key on a cord beneath his jacket, not because it is secret but because it is easy to lose. He signs it out each morning and returns it before the lamps are lowered. The ledger has one unusual rule: a keeper may pass the key to another man without asking a supervisor, but both men must write the time. The key records trust as a transfer of responsibility, not as ownership.

A small brass tag is tied to the ring. Its inscription is worn but legible: *A passage remains a passage when another man can use it.* The phrase was added after a winter flood, when the service door was kept open for three nights so that the infirmary could be reached without crossing the flooded square.

In the Tractus, the key connects the Lion Gate, the service passage, the drainage channel, the night porter rota, and the winter flood account. Its state changes when custody changes, when the passage is repaired, or when a new route is established. It is not a universal key. It is a record of a particular door and the people who keep it usable.

**Cabinet note, unsigned:** The key was found on the sill after the first thaw. Tomas says he had returned it. The ledger says otherwise. No inquiry was opened. The next morning, the key was hanging on its hook, dry and clean.

## 2. The Founders Dormitory Laundry Token

The token is a flat disk of blue enamel, about the size of a large coin. One side carries the number 18. The other has a small white drawing of a folded shirt. The enamel has chipped along the lower rim where it has been struck against a washer door.

Founders Dormitory issues these tokens to residents who use the shared laundry rooms. The number does not identify a person. It identifies a basket position, which can be borrowed, exchanged, or temporarily reassigned. A resident who finds a token on a machine places it in the wooden tray beside the detergent shelf. No one is expected to know whose it is.

The first token numbered 18 belonged to a resident named Edrin Vale, who left Founders before the end of term. He returned the room key, settled his account, and forgot the laundry token. The next resident, Mikel, used it for a month before discovering the number had been written inside the rim in pencil: *E.V. — Tuesday mornings*. Mikel did not erase it. He added his own initials beneath the first line, then left the token in the tray when he moved to another floor.

The token has passed through five hands. Each resident has added a small mark inside the rim. The marks are not a chain of legal ownership; they are a record of use. The laundry steward keeps it because the disk has become a local custom. New residents are told that if they find number 18, they can use it, but they should leave the old writing alone.

**Attached slip, folded twice:**  
*I thought the token was assigned to a room. It turns out it was assigned to a habit. Tuesday mornings are quieter. Whoever comes after me can have the machine first.*

The artifact connects Founders Dormitory, the laundry room, Edrin Vale, Mikel, the steward's noticeboard, and the recurring Tuesday-morning routine. It is a small example of continuity without possession.

## 3. The Digitorium Silk Trader's Docket

The docket is a narrow sheet of cream paper, ruled in brown ink and folded into thirds. A red thread passes through two pinholes at the top. The trader, Samir of the Lower Arcade, uses it to track one bolt of blue-green silk from arrival to sale. The docket does not record every piece of stock in his stall. It follows one length of cloth because this particular bolt has travelled farther than the others.

The first entry records its arrival from the eastern caravans, packed in oilcloth and carried by a mule whose left ear was torn. The second records inspection by a dyer, who found a pale band near the selvage. The third records a buyer who wanted the entire bolt but could not pay until the next market day. Samir reserved it without taking a deposit. A younger trader objected that this was poor practice. Samir wrote in the margin: *A reservation is not a sale. It is a little time lent to another man.*

On the second market day, the buyer returned with the money and a companion. They purchased two-thirds of the bolt. The remaining length was cut into strips for a tailor's demonstration at the Digitorium. The docket records the division, the price of each portion, the name of the cutter, and the fact that the pale band was placed inside the seam of a ceremonial jacket.

The last entry is not a sale. It is a note from the tailor: *The cloth takes the light differently after dusk. Keep one strip for the room where the new apprentices learn to judge color.*

Samir has kept the docket even though the bolt is gone. It is pinned behind the scale, where the paper has darkened from years of smoke and handling. When a young trader asks how to keep accounts, Samir shows him the docket and explains that a good record follows the material, not merely the money.

**Reverse, in another hand:**  
*The pale band is not a flaw. It is how we know which part came from the first bolt.*

This docket links the Digitorium, Lower Arcade, eastern caravan route, dyers, tailor's workshop, apprentice room, and the market's credit customs. It can later be connected to the jacket and the remaining silk strip if those objects receive their own records.

## 4. The Cabin Chair Repair Ticket

The ticket is a square of thin cedar board, scored with a knife so that it can be folded. Brograntimax made it after repairing the chair beside the Cabin stove. The chair's rear left joint had loosened, and one rung had begun to split. He replaced the damaged peg, warmed the joint, fitted a narrow cedar brace, and left the finish untouched so that the repair would remain visible.

The ticket reads:

*Chair by the stove. Rear left joint reset. Lower rung braced. Do not drag by the back. Lift from the seat frame. Repair completed before noon.*

Below that, in smaller writing:

*No name required. No place reserved.*

The ticket was meant to be temporary. Herbarius placed it on the mantel, beside the jar of almost-names, and it remained there. Privathor read it on his return. He sat in the chair without asking whether it had been repaired for him. Lexor stood by the window and watched the stove flame settle. Nobody discussed the ticket.

A week later, Brograntimax added one more line: *The chair is holding.* It was not a report about the wood alone. The chair had become a shared measure of the Cabin's way of making room. A man could leave, return, sit, or remain standing. The object did not decide for him.

**Workshop record:** Cedar brace cut from the offcut kept beneath the bench. Peg made by hand. No stain applied. Repair is reversible if the joint must be opened again.

The ticket connects the Cabin, Brograntimax, Privathor, Lexor, Herbarius, the stove-side chair, and the Cabin chronicle *The Jar of Almost-Names*. Its status remains active while the repair is part of the chair's history.

## 5. The Hrothgar Hall Supper Ledger

The supper ledger is kept in a broad book with a cracked leather spine. It began as a list of meals, but the men of Hrothgar have used it to record who cooked, who arrived late, which stores were low, and what should be prepared when a visitor is expected. It is not a ceremonial roll of honor. It is a practical record of a household large enough that no single man can remember every preference.

A page from the first winter lists barley broth, smoked fish, onions, black bread, and a pot of apples left near the hearth. Beside the meal, the cook has written the names of three men who took second portions and one who ate nothing until after the meeting. At the bottom is a note: *Leave a bowl covered. He may come down later.*

The following page records a disagreement about the firewood. One man had used the dry stack for the bathhouse; another had intended it for the hall. The ledger does not settle who was right. It records the replacement count and the date the new wood arrived. A later hand adds: *The argument ended when they carried it together.*

The ledger's most frequently used page is a table of ordinary provisions: flour, salt, beans, oil, soap, lamp wicks, bandages, and tea. The quantities are crossed out and rewritten as the season changes. In the margin, a cook has drawn a little cup beside the entry for tea. Beneath it: *There is always enough for one more if the kettle is put on early.*

At the back, a page has been left blank for the next winter. No one has torn it out or filled it with a prediction.

**Loose receipt tucked inside:** Two sacks of flour, one sack of barley, lamp oil, three lengths of clean linen. Paid in full. The carter stayed for supper.

The ledger connects Hrothgar Hall, its kitchen, stores, bathhouse, cooks, provisioners, visitors, and seasonal routines. It is a household artifact: a place where male emotion appears not only in declarations but in the repeated work of feeding, noticing, waiting, disagreeing, and making enough.

## 6. The Observation Ring's Blue Card

The card is made of stiff blue stock and carries a white circle printed slightly off-centre. It belongs to the Observation Ring, where trainees record what they notice before they decide what it means. The card is not a test sheet and does not assign a score. It has four prompts:

*What happened?*  
*What did you expect?*  
*What changed your account?*  
*What remains uncertain?*

A trainee named Orven once filled the card after watching a demonstration in the Subject Wing. He wrote that the subject had failed to complete a movement. The coach who reviewed the card asked him to watch again. On the second viewing, Orven noticed that the movement had been completed differently because the subject was protecting an injured wrist. He crossed out *failed* and wrote *adapted*.

The coach did not remove the first word. He drew a line through it and left it legible. Beside it he wrote: *Keep the first account. Add what changed it.*

The card became part of the Ring's teaching practice. Trainees keep their first observations and attach later cards rather than replacing them. A changed interpretation is evidence of learning, not a reason to pretend the earlier interpretation never existed.

**Back of card:**  
*Do not confuse a clean record with a final answer.*

The blue card connects the Observation Ring, Subject Wing, coach, trainee records, the Tractus continuity contract, and the principle of reciprocal transformation. It is a paper-scale expression of the system's central discipline: preserve the encounter, record the revision, and allow the revised account to alter what happens next.

## 7. The Moon Temple Bell Schedule

The schedule is written on a thin sheet of hammered copper. It hangs beside the Temple of Moon's inner stair, where the light reaches it in a narrow strip before sunrise. The schedule lists the bell sequence for ordinary days, feast days, repairs, funerals, and nights when the fog is too thick for the outer path to be used.

A recent amendment changes the third bell. It had once marked the opening of the lower court. Now it marks the moment when the outer doors are checked and the lanterns are carried down. The change followed a night when a visitor arrived after the court had closed and found the lower stair dark. The bell keeper added the new instruction without naming the visitor.

The schedule has no signature line. Changes are recorded with the date, the reason, and the initials of the keeper who made them. A later keeper may reverse an amendment, but must leave the earlier instruction visible beneath it. The copper has become crowded with fine incisions. It is polished only around the current day's sequence.

**Keeper's note on paper beneath the plate:**  
*The bell is not there to command the night. It tells the people inside the walls what the people outside can expect.*

The schedule links the Temple of Moon, the inner stair, outer path, bell keeper, lantern stores, night visitors, and the temple's public hours. Its revisions alter how the place is inhabited. A timetable, here, is a form of hospitality.

## 8. The Letter That Crossed the Arcade

The letter is addressed simply: *To the man who keeps the blue cloth.* It travelled from the tailor's room to Samir's stall by way of three other men. The first carried it because he was already going to the Lower Arcade. The second recognized the handwriting and asked whether the sender was well. The third placed it beneath the scale so that it would not be lost among the day's receipts.

The letter reads:

*You asked whether the pale band could be hidden. It can. I have put it inside the seam, where the cloth is strongest. But I wanted you to know that the apprentices noticed it. They argued about whether it was a flaw, a dye mark, or a change in the light. I let them argue until they began to compare it with the other strips. They learned more from the disagreement than they would have learned from my answer.*

*I have kept the narrow offcut. It is pinned above the worktable. When the new men arrive, I will show them the difference between a defect and a feature that has not yet found its use.*

*The jacket is finished. The buyer wore it to the hall supper. He said nothing about the band. I think he liked knowing it was there.*

There is no signature. On the back, Samir has written: *Received after market close. Answer when the next bolt arrives.*

The letter connects the silk docket, tailor's workshop, apprentice room, ceremonial jacket, Hrothgar Hall supper, and the eastern caravan supply route. It demonstrates a route of transmission: an artifact can move through people who are not its author or final recipient, gathering context without losing its original message.

## 9. The Empty Place Card

The place card is a small rectangle of cream paper, folded into a tent. It has no name written on it. It is kept in the Hrothgar Hall kitchen drawer with the spare candles and the good matches.

The card is set at the end of the supper table when the cook expects that someone may arrive late. It does not reserve a seat indefinitely. If the meal is finished and no one has come, the card is returned to the drawer. If a man arrives, the card is removed and the place is made ready. No announcement is made.

The practice began after a winter when a returning traveller came into the hall long after the others had eaten. He stood in the doorway, unsure whether the meal had ended. The cook saw him, took the empty card from the table, and placed a bowl beside it. The traveller sat. The next evening, the card was there again.

There are no rules about who deserves the place. It is not a reward for loyalty or an instrument for tracking absence. The card expresses a limited, practical expectation: someone might come, and the room can be prepared without demanding an explanation.

**Kitchen drawer inscription:** *One card. One bowl. Keep the broth warm if the road is bad.*

The card connects the Hrothgar supper ledger, kitchen, hall doorway, returning travellers, and the customs of shared meals. It is deliberately unowned.

## 10. The Tractus Artifact Register

The cabinet is not complete, and it should not become a closed collection. New artifacts enter when they have a distinct use, a material identity, a traceable appearance, or a relationship that changes how an existing place or character can be understood. A passing mention of a cup need not create a record. The cup becomes an artifact when it carries a recurring obligation, is inherited, is repaired, changes hands, or becomes the means by which a scene can be entered from another direction.

The register uses these fields:

- **Address:** stable identifier that does not change when the title changes.
- **Name:** the name used by people in the world.
- **Material and form:** what the object is physically like.
- **Use:** what people do with it.
- **Custody:** who keeps it, if anyone; ownership and custody are not assumed to be the same.
- **Provenance:** the scene, chapter, maker, event, or source through which it entered the Tractus.
- **Relations:** the people, places, institutions, and other artifacts connected to it.
- **State history:** repairs, transfers, amendments, losses, returns, and reinterpretations.
- **Open questions:** uncertainties that remain visible rather than being filled with invented certainty.

An artifact can be active, dormant, lost, retired, or unresolved. Lost does not mean erased. Retired does not mean forgotten. A later chapter may return an object to use, and that return should be recorded as a new event rather than silently changing its history.

The cabinet should eventually contain a visual study for each artifact, but image generation is not a prerequisite for its existence. A text record can establish shape, material, scale, wear, inscription, and use. When a visual asset is made, it should preserve those details and be linked to this address. The artifact's image is one view of the object, not a replacement for its history.

## 11. The Cabinet at Closing Time

At the end of the day, the cabinet is not locked. The clerk checks the hooks, returns the blue card to its drawer, lays the silk docket flat beneath a weight, and leaves the passage key on its peg. The empty place card goes back beside the matches. The copper schedule remains where the moonlight can find it.

There is a narrow shelf for objects that have not yet been identified. On it sit a wooden button, a cracked cup, a strip of green thread, and a folded note whose writing has run in the rain. The clerk has not assigned them addresses. He has written only the date on a small slip and placed it beside each one.

A man enters near closing time and asks whether anything has been left for him. The clerk checks the shelf, then the drawer, then the ledger. He finds no name. He does not invent one.

He says, "Not yet. But I can keep it here."

The man nods and leaves the door open behind him. The clerk writes the time in the margin. The cabinet remains available for the next return.

## Continuity and Use

These artifacts are designed to interweave existing GEEHUB settings rather than establish isolated curiosities. The Northgate key ties the gate to its service geography and maintenance workers. The laundry token gives Founders Dormitory a recurring social custom. The silk docket and letter connect the Digitorium market to a tailor, apprentices, a garment, and the Hrothgar supper. The Cabin repair ticket gives a physical object a quiet role in the ongoing lives of Privathor, Lexor, Herbarius, and Brograntimax. The supper ledger and empty place card make the men of Hrothgar legible through domestic practice. The blue card translates Tractus's continuity discipline into an in-world teaching instrument. The Moon Temple schedule shows how a small amendment can change the expectations of an entire place.

Future chapters should use these objects in motion. A character should find the key wet after rain, discover the old initials on the token, carry a letter without knowing its full history, or notice that the place card has been returned to the drawer. The object should not appear merely to remind the reader that it exists. Its presence should affect a decision, reveal a relationship, open a route, or alter the next inscription.

When an artifact changes hands, record both the transfer and its consequence. When it is repaired, preserve the damage and the repair. When its meaning changes, keep the earlier meaning visible. When two accounts disagree, let the disagreement remain until an event or a deliberate editorial decision resolves it. The cabinet is a living part of the Tractus because the world keeps using what it remembers.
