# The Men's Papers — Private Records and Unfinished Things

The public archive records what a place needs to remember. The men's papers preserve what a man notices when he is not addressing the public. They are not all confessions. Some are lists, measurements, reminders, letters never sent, sketches of a hinge, accounts of a disagreement, or a page on which a man has written the same sentence three times because none of the versions quite says what he means.

These papers belong to the fictional men and institutions of GEEHUB. They are literary artifacts, not claims about private records belonging to real people. Their purpose is to give characters continuity between major scenes. A man should have a life when he is not being watched by the chapter. He should remember what he meant to repair, whom he promised to visit, what he has not yet understood, and what he has chosen not to say.

The papers are arranged by form rather than by rank. A notebook is not automatically more truthful than a public ledger. A letter may conceal as much as it reveals. An inventory can become intimate through the objects it lists. An unfinished page is not a failure of the archive; it is evidence that a life continues beyond the available account.

## 1. Privathor's Pocket Notebook

The notebook has a black cloth cover, a softened spine, and a narrow elastic band that has begun to lose its tension. Privathor carries it in the inside pocket of his coat. The first pages contain practical reminders: replace the porch lantern wick, ask Herbarius about the north shelf, return the borrowed awl, check the Cabin roof after the next storm. Later pages become less orderly.

**Page 11 — before dawn**

The house is quietest before the stove catches. The chair is still holding. I checked the rear joint although Brograntimax already checked it yesterday. The brace is sound. I sat down for a moment and heard the wood settle. It did not complain.

Lexor was awake. He did not come into the kitchen. I saw the light beneath his door and left the kettle where he could find it. There is no need to ask every time.

**Page 16 — list**

- New lamp oil.
- Two lengths of clean cord.
- A packet of nails for the porch step.
- Ask whether the eastern path is passable.
- Return the small book to Herbarius.
- Do not forget the blue thread.

Beside the last item, in a different ink: *Found it in the drawer. It was never missing.*

**Page 22 — no date**

I used to think that leaving a place meant I had failed to belong to it. That is not true. A door can remain open without making a demand. I came back because I wanted to come back. Nobody asked me to explain the time away. That was more difficult to accept than an accusation would have been.

**Page 23 — crossed out**

I should tell Lexor—

The sentence ends there. A line has been drawn through it, but the pressure of the pen has left the words legible on the next page.

**Page 24**

Not everything becomes easier when it is said. Some things become easier when a man knows he may say them later.

The notebook has a folded corner at page 24. No one has added a title to the passage. Privathor keeps the book rather than submitting it to the Cabin cabinet. Its existence is known because he once used it to prop open the pantry door while carrying wood.

## 2. Lexor's Inventory of Things Not Taken

Lexor began the inventory after moving his belongings into the Cabin. It is written on a sheet of thick paper folded into quarters. The heading is precise: *Things I brought, things I left, things I may still need.* He has not completed the third category.

**Brought**

One coat, gray. One spare shirt. Two books, one with a broken corner. A small tin of buttons. A pocketknife. A wooden comb. A cup wrapped in cloth. A letter with no address on the outside. Three coins from a place I do not intend to visit again.

**Left**

A blue coat. One book I had not finished. A key that no longer opens anything. A bowl with a hairline crack. A name written on the inside of a drawer. The name is not entered here.

**May still need**

A second blanket. A better pair of gloves. A reason to return to the eastern road. A place to put the letter.

At the bottom of the page, Lexor has written: *The things left behind are not all losses. Some are only things I did not carry.*

A second sheet is attached with thread. It contains a small correction: the pocketknife was not brought from the old room; it was given to him by a man at the market after Lexor helped carry a broken cart axle. He has added the giver's name, then covered it with a narrow strip of paper. The name remains visible if the sheet is held to the light, but the archive does not reproduce it. The giver's identity is not required to understand the object's provenance, and Lexor has not authorized the name for wider circulation.

The inventory is not a complete account of Lexor's possessions. It is a map of the boundary between what he carries, what he leaves, and what he is not yet ready to classify.

## 3. Herbarius's Correspondence with the Garden Keeper

Herbarius writes in a narrow hand and uses the backs of old notices whenever he can. The following letter was sent to the garden keeper at Northgate after a shipment of medicinal herbs arrived damp.

*The upper leaves are sound, but the roots have taken too much water. I have separated the damaged bundles and laid the rest on the eastern rack. Do not discard the whole shipment. There is still enough for the winter preparations if we dry it slowly.*

*I would also ask that the next delivery be divided between the infirmary and the lower garden. The infirmary needs the prepared stock. The garden needs a living portion so that we are not dependent on a road that may close.*

*You once asked why I keep seeds from plants that do not thrive here. I keep them because a failed season is not the same as a useless plant. Some need another soil. Some need a different month. Some will never grow in this place, and that is information too.*

*Please send word when the next rain barrel is repaired. The young men have been carrying water from the lower cistern, and the path is becoming slick.*

The garden keeper's reply is written on a separate page:

*The barrel is repaired. We have set two stones along the slick part of the path. I will send a living portion with the next delivery. I have kept the plants that did not take. One of them has begun to recover in the shaded bed.*

Herbarius has underlined the last sentence. In the margin he has written: *Ask to see it.*

The correspondence connects the Cabin's Herbarius, Northgate's lower garden, the infirmary, seasonal supply routes, and the practical exchange of knowledge between men who do not share a residence. The letters create a route between the domestic and institutional worlds.

## 4. Brograntimax's Work Order No. 27

The work order is a broad sheet of brown paper, creased at the centre. It was issued after the Cabin porch step began to lift at one end. Brograntimax wrote the first inspection in pencil, then returned after the repair to complete the report in ink.

**Location:** Cabin, porch, lower step.  
**Observed condition:** Right end raised; two nails loose; timber dry but not split.  
**Immediate risk:** A man descending in low light may catch the edge of his boot.  
**Materials:** Two iron nails, one cedar shim, a small quantity of oil.  
**Work:** Lifted the step, cleared grit from the joint, inserted shim, reset nails, tested under weight.  
**Result:** Step level and secure.  
**Follow-up:** Check after heavy rain.

A second note appears below the official report:

*Privathor held the lantern. Lexor brought the nails without being asked. Herbarius came out with a cup of tea and stayed until the step was tested. Nobody needed to be assigned a task.*

The work order is signed by Brograntimax. A small drawing shows the porch step from above, with the shim indicated by a dark rectangle. The drawing is not to scale, but it is sufficient to show where the repair was made.

Three weeks later, the follow-up box contains one word: *Holding.*

The work order is connected to the Cabin repair ticket, the stove-side chair repair, the porch lantern, and the shared maintenance practice of the house. It also establishes Brograntimax's method: identify the actual weakness, repair it with the least disruptive intervention, test it under use, and return to inspect rather than assuming the work is complete.

## 5. Seth's Training Margin

Seth's training notebook is ruled in narrow columns. It is not a diary. Most pages contain exercise names, repetitions, recovery intervals, equipment settings, and short observations about movement. The margins are more revealing than the tables.

**Monday**

Warm-up completed. Shoulder rotation restricted on the left. Reduced load. Coach says the movement should remain clean before the weight increases.

Margin: *I wanted to finish the set. I finished the movement instead.*

**Wednesday**

Partner work. Nick noticed the bench had shifted before the second set. We reset the feet and checked the floor. No injury. The room needs a mark showing the proper bench position.

Margin: *A strong man should not have to pretend he did not notice a hazard.*

**Friday**

The coach asked me to describe the difference between effort and strain. I said effort is what I can direct; strain is what I have to listen to. He told me to keep that answer.

Margin: *I don't know if it is right. It is the best account I have today.*

A later page records the bench mark: two short strips of white tape on the floor. Nick added a third strip after noticing that the bench could still drift when the room was crowded. Seth drew the revised position and wrote: *Use the room we have, not the room we wish we had.*

The notebook connects Seth, Nick, the coach, the Heorot Flesh Workshop, the training floor, and the developing Flesh Dynamics material. It records fictional training continuity. It does not establish a real person's actual training history or private thoughts.

## 6. Nick's Letter to Seth, Not Sent

The letter is folded but not sealed. Nick wrote it after the bench incident and kept it in the back of his notebook.

*Seth,*

*You were right about the bench. I had seen it moving before you said anything, but I thought it would hold for another set. I was more concerned with stopping the exercise than with admitting that the floor was not ready.*

*I don't like being corrected in front of the others. I think you know that. I also don't want you to stop correcting me when it matters. I can take the difference between being embarrassed and being put at risk. I am still learning how to tell them apart before I react.*

*I added the third tape mark. It looks a little excessive, but the bench stays where it should. If someone complains, they can move it after they explain why.*

*There is something else I wanted to say about the new trainees. They watch us when they think we aren't paying attention. They copy the way we load the bar, how we set the room, how we speak after a bad set. We should be more deliberate about what they can learn from us.*

*I will tell you this in person if the moment comes. Until then, the letter can stay here.*

There is no date. On the outside of the fold, Nick has written: *Not a complaint. A correction to myself.*

The letter remains unsent. It should not be treated as a conversation that has already occurred. Its narrative function is to preserve the difference between what Nick has understood privately and what he has communicated to Seth.

## 7. Dan Roman's Pocket Map

Dan Roman keeps a small map folded into the back of his coat. It is drawn on a piece of cream paper, with the main streets of the district in black ink and the lesser passages in blue. He has marked the bakery, the public well, the repair yard, the western stair, and a narrow lane that is not named on the official city plan.

The lane is marked with a small square. Beside it he has written: *Ask before entering.*

A visitor once asked why the map showed a lane but did not name it.

"Because the people who use it have their own name for it," Dan said.

"Why not write that?"

"Because I don't know whether they want it written."

The visitor looked at the map again. "Then why mark it at all?"

"So I remember where the question begins."

The map has a second layer. Dan has drawn the walking time between the bakery and the repair yard at three different hours. Morning is fastest because the market carts have not arrived. Midday is slower because the lane is crowded. Evening is uncertain because the repair yard closes at different times depending on the work.

He has not added a north arrow. Instead, he has written the name of the hill that can be seen from the upper street. The map is intended for walking, not for surveying.

On the reverse, Dan has listed four errands: collect a repaired clasp, deliver a letter to the lower arcade, ask the baker about the flour order, and return a borrowed measuring cord. Three are crossed out. The last remains.

The map connects Dan Roman, the unnamed lane, the market, the repair yard, the bakery, and the lower arcade. It is a practical geography whose omissions are deliberate.

## 8. The Coach's Spare Whistle

The whistle is made of dark wood, polished smooth by handling. It is not the coach's primary whistle. That one hangs on a cord beside the training floor. The spare is kept in a drawer with chalk, tape, spare laces, and a small roll of bandage.

A trainee named Iven found it while looking for a pencil. He picked it up and turned it over.

"Does this one work?"

"Yes," the coach said.

"Why keep two?"

"One can be lost."

"Has this one been lost?"

"Once."

The coach took the whistle and examined the small crack near the mouthpiece. It had been repaired with a thin ring of metal. He showed Iven the repair.

"It stopped working during a winter session. I used it to call the floor to a halt. Nobody heard me. We stopped because one of the men saw my hand."

"Why keep it if it failed?"

"It did not fail completely. The sound changed. I learned not to depend on sound alone."

The coach returned it to the drawer. He added a small note to the equipment inventory: *Spare whistle — repaired — audible at close range — visual signal remains primary for emergency stop.*

Iven looked at the note. "So the whistle is still useful."

"Yes. But the room should never depend on one signal."

The whistle connects the coach, training floor, equipment drawer, emergency procedure, and the lesson that a system should retain more than one route for communicating an important change. The object is ordinary. Its repair has become part of the institution's knowledge.

## 9. The Letter from the Upper Room

A letter arrived at the Cabin in a plain envelope with no return address. Herbarius recognized the handwriting but did not identify the sender aloud. He placed the envelope on the table and went outside to check the herb rack.

Lexor found it first. He turned it over, then left it where it was. Privathor came in later and saw it beside the kettle.

"Is it for someone here?"

"I think so," Lexor said.

"Do you know who sent it?"

"No."

Privathor did not touch the envelope. He put a clean cup beside it and returned to the porch.

When Herbarius came back, the envelope was still there. He opened it with a small knife and read the page. His expression did not change, but he folded the letter more carefully than he had opened it.

The letter read:

*I have been meaning to write. The upper room is quiet, and I have learned that quiet can be useful without being comfortable. I have repaired the latch. The window still catches when the weather turns. I will ask someone to look at it before winter.*

*I am not ready to return. I am not asking anyone to wait. I wanted you to know that I remember the Cabin and the people in it. I remember the chair by the stove. I remember the cup that was left out without a question.*

*Please do not send a reply unless there is something you want to say. I am not keeping count.*

There was no signature. Herbarius folded the letter and put it inside a book on the shelf.

Lexor saw him do it.

"Will you tell us who wrote it?"

"Not unless he asks me to."

"Does he want us to know?"

"He wanted the letter to arrive."

Privathor came in from the porch. He looked at the book, then at Herbarius.

"Is he well?"

"Well enough to write."

That was all Herbarius said. They drank their tea. The letter remained in the book, not hidden, but not placed on the mantel for everyone to inspect.

Later, Lexor wrote one line in his inventory: *A letter arrived from the upper room. I did not open it. That was enough.*

The letter connects the Cabin, an unnamed resident of the upper room, Herbarius, Lexor, Privathor, and the stove-side chair. The sender remains unnamed until the story provides a grounded reason to identify him. The absence of a name is not an invitation to guess.

## 10. The Repair Yard's Measure

At the repair yard, the men keep a length of cord with knots tied at irregular intervals. It is not a measuring tape. It is a memory aid for a particular cart whose axle has been replaced twice. The first set of knots marks the old wheelbase. The second set marks the revised frame. A third knot, tied in red thread, indicates the point where the cart begins to pull left when heavily loaded.

Dan Roman brought the cord to the yard after finding it in the lower arcade. The foreman recognized it immediately.

"Where did you get that?"

"Under the bench near the public well."

"That's ours."

"It was left there."

The foreman took the cord and examined the knots. "Someone has added another."

The new knot was plain white. It marked a distance shorter than the red thread by the width of a man's hand.

"Who tied it?" Dan asked.

"I don't know."

The foreman laid the cord along the axle and checked the wheel alignment. The cart had been repaired the previous day. The new knot matched the position of the replacement brace.

"That is the right measurement," he said.

"Then someone checked it."

"Or someone remembered it."

The foreman tied a small tag to the cord and wrote the date. He did not remove the new knot. The cord returned to its hook, now carrying four measures: the original frame, the revised frame, the pulling point, and the new brace.

Dan watched him put it away.

"Wouldn't a proper measure be better?"

"It would be better for the next cart. This one has a history."

The cord connects the repair yard, Dan's walking map, the lower arcade, the cart, and the unnamed worker who added the final knot. Its measurements are not universal standards. They are accumulated knowledge about one machine.

## 11. The Private Inventory of a Room

A resident of Founders Dormitory made an inventory after the floor steward asked everyone to check the fire exits and keep the corridors clear. His list was ordinary:

One bed. One desk. One chair. Two lamps. Three books. Four shirts. One coat. One pair of boots. One small radio. One cup. One blue laundry token, borrowed for Tuesday.

The last entry made him stop. He had written *borrowed* because he did not know whether the token belonged to the room or the laundry tray. He went downstairs and asked the steward.

"Number eighteen?"

"Yes."

"Should I return it?"

"Put it in the tray when you're finished."

"Does anyone own it?"

"Not in the way you mean."

The resident thought about that answer. He returned to his room and crossed out *borrowed*. In the margin he wrote: *In use. Return to common tray.*

He added another line beneath the inventory:

*The chair is mine while I am sitting in it. The room is mine while I am responsible for it. The corridor belongs to everyone who must pass through it.*

He signed the inventory and placed it inside the door pocket. The steward later checked the fire exit and found the corridor clear. The paper remained in the pocket, where the resident could see it each morning.

This unnamed inventory may become a character record if the resident is later introduced. Until then, it remains an institutional occurrence, not a license to invent an identity.

## 12. The Object That Was Not Returned

The borrowed measuring cord did not come back to the repair yard on Friday. The foreman waited until the end of the shift, then checked the hook. The tag was still there, but the cord was gone.

He wrote an entry in the tool book: *Cord absent. Last recorded with Dan Roman. No return time entered.*

On Saturday morning, Dan arrived with a small wooden box. Inside was the cord, neatly coiled, and a note.

*I took this to compare the cart measurements with the lower arcade step. I should have entered the transfer. The step is higher on the north side by one finger. I did not alter it. The cord is returned.*

The foreman read the note and measured the step himself. Dan's observation was correct. The unevenness had been there for years, but it had not previously caused trouble. The foreman added the measurement to the yard's route notes and returned the cord to its hook.

He did not erase the absent entry. He wrote the return time beneath it.

The next week, a carpenter used the measurement to prepare a small wedge for the step. The wedge was fitted without closing the arcade. Men continued to pass while the work was done. The cord had not merely returned; it had carried information into another repair.

## 13. The Papers Kept by the Men

A private paper should enter the Tractus only at the level appropriate to its use and permission. A character's private notebook may be represented by a literary excerpt without implying that every page is public. A letter can be indexed by sender, recipient, route, and consequence while its contents remain restricted. A map may preserve an unnamed passage without publishing the name used by the people who live there. A training notebook may record a fictional exercise without being mistaken for a real person's biography.

The cabinet should therefore distinguish four degrees of access:

- **Public artifact:** intended to be read or handled by the wider community.
- **Shared artifact:** available to a defined group, household, crew, or institution.
- **Private artifact:** known to exist, but its contents are not open for general inspection.
- **Restricted artifact:** retained as a record of existence or provenance while sensitive contents remain withheld.

These are narrative and editorial categories for the fictional world. They do not replace real access controls or repository permissions. The distinction prevents the archive from treating every discovered paper as public property.

The next chronicles should continue from the consequences already established. Tomas and Neral must work together again after the key incident. Arlen should discover what happens when another resident uses token eighteen. Rovan's bridge report should affect a supply journey. Pel's amended bell schedule should be tested during a night of heavy fog. Orven's third card should lead to a new observation. The Cabin letter should remain unanswered until its sender chooses to write again. The silk repair should be worn in public and inspected after use. The repair-yard measurement should become part of the next cart's construction.

A continuity world grows when yesterday's objects alter today's decisions. It does not need every man to explain what he feels. Sometimes the change is a cord returned, a door held open, a bowl kept warm, or a line added to a ledger.
