# Tractus: The Fog Week

The fog came in from the eastern water on a Monday evening and stayed long enough for the city to begin making decisions around it. At first it was only a pale obstruction between the outer roofs. By midnight it had entered the lower streets, softened the lamps, and covered the stone bridge until the far parapet could no longer be distinguished from the river. The bell at the Moon Temple sounded as it always had, but its note seemed to stop halfway across the city. Men heard it from their windows and waited for the second stroke before deciding whether to go out.

No proclamation declared an emergency. The city had learned that a proclamation could make people look toward the tower when what they needed was to look at the ground. The repair yard put a lantern beside its gate. The market closed its eastern shutters. Hrothgar Hall kept the kitchen open one hour later than usual. At Northgate, the porter crew took the key register down from its hook and placed it on the table where both men on duty could reach it.

The archive did not yet call this a week. It began with separate marks: a late cart, a delayed bell, a wet boot print, a lamp trimmed twice, a bowl left covered. Only later did the marks acquire a common title.

## 1. The Key Between Two Men

Tomas arrived at Northgate before the first evening shift and found Neral testing the gate latch with the heel of his hand. The repair had held through the afternoon, but the iron tongue still caught if the gate was drawn too quickly. Neral had placed the old key on the table beside the register. It was not the key that had caused the earlier confusion; that one had been returned and entered under its proper custody. This was the spare, whose worn bow had begun to split around the ring.

Tomas looked at the register before he looked at Neral. The new procedure required both men to see the key change hands. Neral had written the time and left the receiving line empty.

“You can take it,” Neral said. “I want to see whether the ring holds.”

Tomas did not take it immediately. He examined the split, turned the key once, and asked for the small brass gauge from the drawer. The gauge was not listed among the gate tools. Neral had borrowed it from the repair yard and had not yet returned it. Tomas wrote that fact in the margin.

They measured the ring together. The metal had thinned at the point where it rested against the hook. It would not fail that evening, but it would fail if the key continued to be carried by its ring. Neral found a short leather thong in the drawer. Tomas threaded it through the bow and tied it to the key's body, leaving enough slack for the key to turn without straining the weakened metal.

The spare key was now safe to carry, but the register still showed an incomplete transfer. Neral entered Tomas's name. Tomas entered the condition of the ring. Neither man altered the earlier record of the first key incident. They added a new line beneath it, and the new line explained why the old procedure had changed.

When the fog thickened, they worked the gate together. One watched the street; the other watched the latch. A cart arrived with its driver unable to see the curb. Tomas held the lantern low while Neral guided the wheel past the drainage channel. The driver thanked them without asking their names. Later, when the register was closed, Tomas found a damp crescent on the page where the lantern had stood. He let it dry rather than copying the entry onto a clean sheet.

The next morning the key hung from its leather thong. The brass gauge was returned to the repair yard with a note. The note did not say that Northgate had solved a problem. It said that the yard should inspect the other spare keys before the next wet season.

## 2. The Token Is Not a Name

Arlen found token eighteen in the laundry room on Tuesday, lying beside the blue basket Mikel had repaired. The token had been used by another resident during the morning wash. The initials Arlen had added were still visible, but a second date had been written below them in a different hand. The communal basket had carried the token from one end of the room to the other, and someone had assumed that the initials marked ownership.

Arlen stood with the token between two fingers while the washing turned behind him. He was annoyed, though he could not have said whether he was annoyed with the other resident, with the assumption, or with the small fact that his own handwriting had made the misunderstanding possible.

Mikel came in carrying a length of blue thread. He saw the token and set the thread down.

“He thought it was yours,” Mikel said.

“I thought the date would be enough.”

“It was enough for you.”

They did not argue about the other resident. Mikel had already spoken with him and learned that he had used the token because the basket was full and the numbered pegs had been moved during cleaning. He had not taken anything. He had returned the token to the same shelf.

Arlen washed his hands and examined the basket repair. The thread had held, but one loop was beginning to loosen where the handle met the rim. He passed the token to Mikel and held the basket steady while Mikel stitched the loop down. They made a new mark for the basket, a small blue bar on the underside, and agreed that the token would identify a place in the laundry sequence, not a person.

In the room ledger, Arlen wrote: “Token eighteen used by another resident. No loss. Initials mistaken for ownership. Marking clarified.” He hesitated before adding his own name, then wrote it beneath the entry as the person who had made the first inscription.

The token returned to the shelf. It had not become anonymous. It had become more accurately communal. Arlen's initials remained part of its history, but they no longer governed its use.

That evening he found a folded note beside his clean shirts. The other resident had written that he was sorry for using the token and had thought it belonged to the basket. Arlen read it twice. He put the note in the shared ledger pocket rather than keeping it. The correction belonged where the misunderstanding had occurred.

## 3. The Bridge Report Becomes a Journey

Rovan's report had been written after supper, when the eastern crossing was still passable but the water had begun to rise against the lower stones. He had noted three things: the second marker was loose, the northern approach held more firmly than the southern, and carts should not cross in pairs until the marker was reset. At the time, the report had seemed like a small piece of practical information. On Wednesday morning it determined the route of a supply journey.

Samir had already packed the market goods: flour in sealed sacks, dried pears, lamp oil, two coils of rope, and a crate of small iron fittings for the repair yard. The original plan was to send the cart over the eastern bridge and return by Northgate. Rovan's report changed the order. The cart would go north first, unload the iron fittings at the yard, then continue to Hrothgar Hall by the upper road. The flour and oil would be delivered before the market's eastern lane was closed for the afternoon.

Rovan went with the driver. He did not present himself as an authority on the bridge. He carried the report in his coat and checked the stones against his own notes. At the northern approach, he found the marker leaning farther than it had the previous night. The driver stopped before the cart reached it. Together they moved the first coil of rope to the rear and used it to secure the marker to the iron post beside the road. The repair was temporary, but it made the warning visible.

At the yard, Brograntimax inspected the fittings and asked why the cart had arrived from the north. Rovan handed him the report. Brograntimax read it, then called for the measuring cord from the workbench. The cord had been used to establish the dimensions for the next cart. He measured the axle clearance and compared it with the width of the bridge approach.

“The cart is not the problem,” he said. “The turning space is.”

He marked the measurement on the work order and added a second line for a narrower wheel guard. The repair yard did not attempt to rebuild the bridge from one report. It changed the cart so that the next crossing would be less vulnerable to the same turn.

At Hrothgar Hall, the kitchen had kept the back entrance clear. The sacks were unloaded beneath the awning. Rovan carried the oil himself, one can at a time, and the cook made him sit long enough to eat a bowl of barley stew. He gave the bridge report to the hall steward, who copied the route change into the supply ledger.

The supper ledger now contained a journey that had not been planned when the day began. Beside the entry, the steward wrote the names of the men who had handled the goods. No one was praised for heroism. The flour had arrived dry, the oil had not leaked, and the iron fittings were in the yard. For that evening, those facts were sufficient.

## 4. The Bell in the Lower Street

Pel's amendment to the Moon Temple bell schedule had been written for a specific condition: if the lantern at the lower stair failed, the third bell would be delayed until the route had been inspected. The amendment had seemed almost excessive when the weather was clear. During the fog, the lower stair disappeared into a gray depth that made the first landing seem farther away than it was.

Pel arrived with Aven before the second bell. Aven carried the spare lantern and a covered tin of oil. Pel carried the schedule in a waxed sleeve. They checked the upper landing first. The wall lamp was burning, but its glass had clouded with moisture. Aven cleaned it with a strip of linen while Pel examined the stair rail.

At the lower turn, they found a man waiting with one hand on the stone wall. He had come up from the river road and could not see the next landing. He had heard the first bell but had lost the direction of the sound in the fog. Pel did not ask him why he had continued. He handed Aven the spare lantern and walked down two steps, keeping one hand on the rail.

The man followed the lantern rather than the bell. At the landing, Aven raised the light until the man could see the next section of stair. Pel waited until he reached the upper level, then returned to the schedule.

The third bell was delayed by eleven minutes. The time was entered beside the amendment. The man was not named; he had not given his name, and the event did not require it. The record described the route, the condition of the lamp, the delay, and the fact that the lantern had been used to guide one traveler.

After the bell, Pel and Aven replaced the cloudy glass. They also set a small reflective plate at the lower turn so that the lantern's light would be visible from above. It was a modest alteration, but the next person descending would have two signs instead of one.

In the temple office, Pel copied the event into the schedule's margin. He did not erase the earlier instruction or rewrite it as if the fog had been expected. The amendment had been tested, and the test had revealed another weakness. The schedule now contained the original rule, its first use, and the change that followed.

Aven kept the linen strip. It had dried stiff with oil and soot. He folded it into the lantern box rather than throwing it away. When Pel asked why, Aven said that the next time the glass clouded, they would know which cloth had worked.

## 5. The Second Blue Card

Orven's third observation card had led another trainee to begin recording his own assumptions. On Thursday, the trainee returned with a card that described a man who had stopped at the Observation Ring and looked toward the western screen for nearly four minutes. The trainee had written that the man appeared uncertain.

Orven read the card without correcting it immediately. The western screen had been displaying a sequence of changing light levels. The man had been there before, and his earlier card showed that he had learned to distinguish the screen's slow flicker from the brief interruption caused by a faulty connection. Orven asked the trainee what else he had observed.

“He did not leave when the image changed,” the trainee said. “He waited until the pattern returned.”

“What did he do while he waited?”

“He counted with his thumb.”

Orven looked again at the card. The word “uncertain” was plausible, but it was not supported by the observation. The man might have been uncertain. He might have been checking the sequence, remembering it, or waiting for a familiar interval. The trainee had recorded an interpretation as if it were an action.

Orven placed his own earlier card beside it. His first account had made the same mistake. It had described a pause as hesitation, then a correction had shown that the pause was part of the man's method. The trainee read the two cards and crossed out the word “uncertain.” He replaced it with: “Waited through one interruption. Counted with thumb. Left after pattern returned.”

The card did not become less useful when the interpretation was removed. It became more exact. Orven added a question beneath the observation: “What can be learned from a repeated wait?” The trainee took the card back and asked to observe the screen again the following day.

Later, Orven stood alone in the Ring. The screen was dark. He could see his reflection in it, but only as a faint outline interrupted by the seams of the glass. He thought of the man who counted with his thumb and of the trainee who had corrected his own record. He did not make a new card. Not every encounter required an entry.

He did, however, leave the blue pencil beside the card tray with its point sharpened. The next observer would not have to search for one.

## 6. The Jacket at Supper

The jacket with the pale-banded silk repair appeared at Hrothgar Hall on Friday evening. Its owner had worn it through a morning of damp work and an afternoon of carrying crates. The repair had been made from the sample recorded in Samir's silk docket. It was not invisible. The pale band showed when the jacket opened, a narrow line against the darker lining.

Rovan noticed it first. He recognized the cloth from the market docket, though he had not known where the sample had gone after it was cut. He asked whether the repair had held. The owner opened the jacket and showed him the seam. One stitch had loosened near the lower edge, but the lining remained in place.

The men at the table examined the work without turning the jacket into a spectacle. Brograntimax suggested a second line of stitching along the edge. Samir said the remaining silk would be enough if the repair was kept narrow. The owner said he would bring it to the yard after breakfast.

Supper continued. The stew was thick with beans and carrots. A dish of pickled onions passed from one end of the table to the other. Someone had brought a loaf with a hard, dark crust, and the cook complained that it had been left too close to the oven. The jacket was folded over the back of a chair, where the pale band could be seen whenever its owner moved.

After the meal, the steward checked the supply ledger. The silk docket had recorded the original sample, the cut, and its new use. The supper entry now recorded that the repaired garment had been worn in public and that one stitch required attention. The jacket's history had passed from market goods to tailoring, from tailoring to work, and from work into a shared meal.

The next morning, Brograntimax repaired the loose stitch. He did not replace the pale band or try to hide it. The owner asked him to leave the edge visible. It had become a recognizable repair, and the jacket was still sound.

## 7. The Letter That Stayed Folded

The letter from the upper room remained in the Cabin desk through the fog week. No answer arrived. No one was authorized to open it, and the person who had left it had not returned to ask whether it had been read. The private inventory recorded its presence and the fact that the fold remained unbroken.

On Saturday, Privathor noticed that the desk had begun to take in moisture along its rear edge. He moved the letter to a dry drawer and placed a small cedar strip beneath the drawer's back rail. The strip had come from the repair yard as an offcut. He entered it in the room inventory as a temporary support, not as a permanent fixture.

Lexor saw the entry later and asked whether the letter had been moved. Privathor said only that it had been kept dry. Lexor did not ask what it contained. He added the drawer location to the inventory and left the access category unchanged.

That afternoon, the person who had left the letter came to the Cabin. He stood near the desk for a while, then asked whether anything had been damaged by the damp. Privathor said the paper was dry. The man looked at the drawer but did not ask to see it. He took the cedar strip from the repair bench, examined its grain, and said that he had once used the same kind of wood to make a box for his brother.

He left without opening the letter. The desk remained in use. The drawer remained closed.

The archive kept no account of what the letter said. It recorded the conditions that allowed it to remain unread without being lost: a dry drawer, a repaired desk, an inventory entry, and a man who had been given time to decide whether he wished to speak.

## 8. The Cart Takes the Measure

On Sunday, Brograntimax brought the measuring cord to the yard's open floor and laid it beside the unfinished cart. The mark from Rovan's bridge report had been copied onto the work order. The wheel guard was being narrowed by a small amount, and the turning clearance had been adjusted to give the driver more room at the northern approach.

Dan Roman arrived with his pocket map folded inside his coat. He had marked the upper road in pencil and drawn a second route that avoided the eastern crossing altogether. The route passed the garden wall, then followed a firm strip of ground toward the old stone storehouse. It was longer, but it stayed above the river road.

Brograntimax compared the map with the measuring cord. He asked Dan to walk the route with him before they changed the cart again. They went out with the unfinished wheel guard wrapped in cloth and checked the narrowest turn. The ground was firm enough for a loaded cart, but the storehouse gate was too narrow for the new guard if the cart approached straight on. It would need to enter at an angle.

Dan marked the angle on his map. Brograntimax wrote it on the work order. The two records did not duplicate each other. The map showed where the turn was; the work order showed what the cart needed to do.

They returned to the yard as the afternoon light began to clear. The fog had lifted from the upper roofs, though it still held in the lower streets. The cart would be ready by the next market day. Its route had changed because a man had noticed a loose bridge marker, written down what he saw, and allowed the report to be used by someone who could alter a piece of equipment.

Brograntimax wound the cord around its wooden spool. Dan folded the map along its old creases. Neither document was complete. The cart had not yet made the journey, and the road would have to be checked again after the next rain. The work order remained open.

## 9. The Register of Things That Moved

At the end of the week, the Tractus clerk assembled the records on a long table: Northgate's key register, the laundry ledger, Rovan's bridge report, the Hrothgar supply entry, Pel's amended bell schedule, Orven's blue card, the silk docket, the Cabin inventory, Dan's pocket map, and the repair yard work order.

He did not arrange them by importance. He arranged them by the order in which one had changed the use of another.

The key register had altered how the spare key was carried. The laundry entry had separated a person's initials from a communal token. The bridge report had changed the supply route. The delayed bell had led to a second light at the lower stair. The blue card had changed how a trainee described observation. The silk docket had followed a piece of cloth into a garment and back into repair. The Cabin inventory had protected a letter without disclosing it. The map and work order had begun to change the shape of a cart.

The clerk wrote a short heading: “Fog Week: linked events, provisional closure.”

He paused at the word closure. The bridge had not been rebuilt. The cart had not made its next journey. The letter remained folded. The trainee had not completed his second observation. The key ring would need inspection again. The silk repair would be tested by another day of work. The fog had lifted, but the lower street still held water.

He crossed out “closure” and wrote “review.”

The register was not a conclusion. It was a place from which the next event could be recognized.

## 10. The Men Who Kept the Week

On the first clear evening, the men of Hrothgar Hall stayed at the table after the bowls had been collected. There was no formal meeting. Rovan was cleaning mud from his boot. Samir was counting the remaining oil cans. Pel had brought the temple schedule because he wanted to show Aven the new reflective plate. Orven sat with the blue card in his pocket, and the trainee had come to ask whether he could observe the western screen again.

The conversation moved between practical matters. The eastern bridge would remain restricted until the marker was replaced. The cart would be tested on the upper road. The laundry basket needed another loop of thread. The lower stair lantern should be checked every third evening. The kitchen wanted more dried pears before the next market day.

At one point, Rovan began to describe the moment the cart had stopped at the bridge. He had thought the driver would insist on crossing. Instead, the driver had waited for the marker to be secured. Rovan said he had been relieved, though he had not realized it until they reached the yard. He had spent the rest of the journey holding the report inside his coat, afraid the paper would become too wet to read.

Samir passed him a clean cloth. Rovan took it and folded it carefully before putting it away. No one made a joke of the admission. Aven asked whether the report had dried. Rovan said it had, though the corner still curled.

The trainee asked Orven whether the man at the western screen had been uncertain. Orven said that he did not know. The trainee looked disappointed for a moment, then nodded. He understood that the record was not supposed to provide an answer where the observation had not provided one.

The men began to leave in pairs. Pel and Aven went toward the temple. Samir returned to the market store. Rovan took the upper road to check the bridge marker. Orven walked with the trainee as far as the Observation Ring. The cook extinguished the last lamp after the room was empty.

In the hall ledger, the steward entered the evening as an ordinary supper with an extended stay. He listed the men who had remained, not the subjects they had discussed. The next morning, the ledger would be read for supplies and meal counts. It would also preserve, without announcing it, the fact that these men had chosen to remain together after their work was done.

## 11. Continuity After the Fog

The fog week leaves several active records. They are not resolved merely because the weather has changed.

The Northgate spare key must be inspected with the other keys. The leather thong is a temporary protection, and the brass gauge must be returned after the inspection. Tomas and Neral should continue sharing the gate shift long enough for their revised procedure to become ordinary practice.

Token eighteen remains communal. Its initials are historical marks, not a claim of ownership. The blue basket's repair should be checked after repeated use, and the laundry room should retain the distinction between a numbered place and the person using it.

Rovan's bridge report remains active until the marker is properly secured and the crossing is inspected. The upper route must be tested with a loaded cart. Dan's map and Brograntimax's work order should remain linked but distinct: one records terrain, the other records construction decisions.

Pel's bell amendment has been tested once. The reflective plate and lantern need inspection after rain. Aven's oil-stained linen remains in the lantern box as a practical reference, not a relic.

Orven's card and the trainee's correction should remain together. The next observation must be made independently before either man consults the earlier account. The point is not to imitate Orven's wording but to learn how to separate an observed action from an interpretation.

The silk repair has been worn, inspected, and reinforced. Its remaining material should stay linked to the original docket. The Cabin letter remains private and folded. The cedar strip is a temporary desk repair whose usefulness should be checked before it becomes permanent.

The cart work order remains open. The map's upper route should be walked again after rain, and the finished cart should be tested before any new supply journey is assigned.

These records are not a list of tasks imposed on the city from outside. They are the unfinished edges of things the men have already begun. Tractus preserves them so that the next encounter can take place with some knowledge of what came before.

## 12. A Note on the Nature of the Week

The fog did not make the men different people. It changed the conditions under which their ordinary habits became visible. A key was carried differently because two men examined its weakness. A communal token was clarified because a mark had been misunderstood. A supply journey took another road because a report was trusted. A bell waited because a lantern had failed. A trainee corrected a word because another man's earlier correction had remained available to him. A piece of silk moved from a market shelf into a jacket and acquired a new history through use.

The city was not transformed by a single declaration. It was altered by many small acts that could be repeated, inspected, corrected, and inherited.

This is the Tractus principle in its ordinary form: a record changes the next action, and the next action changes what the record means. The men are not merely entered into the archive. They alter its categories, its routes, its objects, and its expectations. The archive, in turn, gives their later decisions access to earlier experience without forcing them to repeat it.

The fog lifted. The city retained what it had learned.
