# FIRST BUILD DAY
## The covered lumber store

The first morning of construction did not look important. It looked like a delivery arriving too early.

A flatbed truck came up the entrance road just after eight, its tires pressing two dark tracks into the damp gravel. Kirk was already outside. He had a notebook tucked into the back pocket of his work trousers and a tape measure clipped to his belt. He waved the driver toward the open ground beside the workshop, then changed his mind and walked the truck farther around so it would not block the garage.

Luke came out of the house carrying coffee.

“That's the first thing?” he asked.

“That's the first delivery,” Kirk said. “The first thing is making sure we can put it somewhere.”

Tyler appeared in the workshop doorway. He had been sorting tools since seven and had already found three extension cords that nobody remembered owning. He looked at the load of posts, framing lumber, roof sheets, and hardware.

“Where's the drawing?”

Luke handed him a folded plan.

Tyler opened it against the side of the truck. The proposed store was simple: a raised, ventilated timber structure with a broad roof, open sides, a lockable tool cage at one end, and enough room to separate long stock from shorter pieces. It would sit near the workshop but outside the turning radius of the delivery vehicles. The ground needed checking before anyone started digging.

Tyler studied the dimensions.

“Who measured this?”

“Kirk and me,” Luke said.

“Then I'll measure it again.”

“That's why you're here.”

Joseph came around the side of the house with a camera and a small notebook. He took pictures of the site before the truck was unloaded: the workshop wall, the line of trees, the open ground, the old fence, and the path people had already worn between the garage and the house. He wanted the record to show what the place looked like before it changed.

Bill arrived last with a tray of coffee and sandwiches.

“You've been here twenty minutes,” he said, “and already nobody's eaten.”

“We're unloading,” Kirk told him.

“You can unload after breakfast.”

The driver laughed. Tyler took a sandwich. Luke set his coffee on a post and began marking the corners with temporary stakes.

They did not start by lifting anything heavy. They walked the footprint. Tyler checked the diagonals. Kirk confirmed the delivery route. Joseph moved a stake that would have placed the store too close to the tree roots. Luke revised the sketch. Bill watched the whole operation and occasionally asked whether anyone had considered where the rainwater would go.

That question stopped them.

The roof could not simply shed water toward the path. They walked the fall of the ground, found the natural drainage line, and shifted the footprint a little farther east. The change cost them half an hour and saved them from building a problem into the first structure.

By midmorning the site was ready. The posts were unloaded onto temporary bearers, the hardware was counted, and the crew set up a clean work area. Tyler laid out the tools in the order they would be needed. Kirk kept the delivery lane clear. Joseph recorded the revised footprint and the reason for the change. Luke updated the day's plan on a sheet of plywood fixed to the workshop wall.

The work became physical after that.

They dug the post locations, checked the depth, prepared the bases, and began setting the frame. Nobody rushed the alignment. Each post was braced before the next one went in. The first beam took more adjustment than Luke expected. Tyler stood back, looked along the line, and asked them to lower one end by a fraction.

“That's barely anything,” Luke said.

“It's enough to see.”

They adjusted it.

From the house, the frame looked small. Up close, it was already changing the yard. A place that had been empty ground that morning now had corners, height, and a direction. The workshop seemed closer to it. The path from the house seemed to lead toward it. Even the delivery truck had a more obvious place to stand.

At lunch they sat on the low stack of unused timber. Bill distributed sandwiches and complained that the crew had made too much dust for one morning. Joseph showed them the photographs. In the first image, the ground was empty. In the last, the posts stood in a clean rectangle beneath the trees.

Luke looked from the photograph to the frame.

“Tomorrow we get the roof on.”

“Tomorrow we check the bracing,” Tyler said.

“Then the roof.”

“After the bracing.”

Kirk wrote both tasks down.

In the afternoon they finished the cross-members and tightened the temporary supports. They swept the work area, covered the remaining timber, counted the tools, and left the site orderly. The structure was not finished. It was not even weather-tight. But it was real, and the next day's work had become visible.

At dusk, Joseph walked back from the orchard and stopped beside the frame. The evening light passed through the open sides and laid long rectangles across the ground. He took one more photograph.

Bill was waiting on the porch.

“Are you coming in?”

“In a minute.”

Joseph looked at the posts again. They were rough, still marked with pencil and chalk. There was no sign or name on the building yet.

“What do we call it?” he asked when he came inside.

“The lumber store,” Tyler said.

Bill shook his head. “That's what it is. Not what it is called.”

Luke opened his notebook.

“Covered store, phase one.”

Kirk wrote the name into the Registry.

The first building of the expansion had begun as a practical answer to a practical problem. By evening it had acquired a place in the history of the Convent. Tomorrow they would check the bracing, raise the roof, and make somewhere for the next delivery to go.

There was already more work waiting than they could finish in a day.
