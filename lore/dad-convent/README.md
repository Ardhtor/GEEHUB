# THE DAD CONVENT
## GEEHUB world record

**Status:** Active world / living settlement  
**Primary mode:** Construction, household life, practical skill, continuity  
**Setting:** A wooded estate expanding into a substantial residential and workshop complex  
**Continuity:** A new setting branch connected to the post-book domestic cycle of *The God Daddies*. This record does not overwrite existing character canon.

### Premise

The Dad Convent is a settlement built by adult men who intend to remain. Its defining activity is construction. The residents do not merely occupy a finished estate or maintain a picturesque retreat: they continually enlarge, repair, adapt, and connect the place. Every completed building creates new needs, new skills, new gathering places, and another generation of projects.

The word *Convent* is an old, slightly humorous name that has survived through use. The place is not a religious institution. Its customs arise from shared residence, work, inheritance, hospitality, and the accumulated memory of people who have built together.

### Existing character continuity

The first narrative cycle follows the established post-book household:

- **Luke** turns days into productions. He coordinates the next undertaking, keeps the work moving, and sees how separate jobs fit into a larger whole.
- **Tyler** gravitates toward practical repair, tools, measurement, and the stubborn realities of making things fit.
- **Kirk** handles logistics, materials, transport, and the small decisions that prevent a large project from stalling.
- **Joseph** notices how spaces are used by the people living in them. He contributes to planning, finishing, grounds, and the human record of each change.
- **Bill** preserves the household's ordinary customs, feeds people, remembers what has been done, and insists that daily life be recorded alongside major achievements.

These roles are tendencies, not rigid assignments. Residents learn from one another and change specialties as the settlement grows. Additional fictional adult residents and households may enter through their own stories. New characters must not be confused with real people.

### The grounds

The estate begins with a main house, an open yard, a garage, a workshop, an orchard, and a long approach road beneath mature trees. These are the original anchors, not the final boundary.

The settlement expands through connected building phases:

1. **The Great Hall** — a timber-framed communal room with a long table, kitchen connection, storage, and an upper gallery.
2. **Residential Wing** — private rooms and suites arranged around shared lounges, washrooms, balconies, and sheltered passages.
3. **Iron Room** — a durable training building with lifting platforms, equipment storage, high ceilings, and clear circulation.
4. **Foundry and Fabrication Shop** — metalworking, welding, machining, repair benches, ventilation, and protected material storage.
5. **Carpentry Hall and Lumber Yard** — timber racks, covered cutting areas, joinery benches, and a dry store.
6. **Bathhouse** — stone, tile, hot water, pools, steam, changing rooms, and a sheltered outdoor court.
7. **Service Court** — vehicle bays, mechanical work, utilities, deliveries, and waste handling kept separate from quiet residential areas.
8. **Outer Estate** — roads, footpaths, bridges, retaining walls, drainage, gardens, orchards, and future building plots.

### Construction culture

Building is a shared civic practice. Each project has a named lead, a purpose, a site, a material plan, dependencies, a visible state, and a record of who contributed. The work is substantial and cumulative: foundations precede walls; weatherproofing precedes interiors; utilities and access are planned rather than improvised after occupation.

The residents value sound workmanship, safe access, durable materials, repairability, and buildings that feel inhabited. Competition is expressed through craft, ingenuity, speed within safe limits, and the quality of the finished work—not reckless shortcuts.

The architecture has a consistent language: heavy oak and other exposed timber, dark stone, brick, steel fittings, generous windows, deep doorways, substantial stairs, broad porches, and rooms sized for communal use. Later buildings may develop their own character while remaining recognizably part of the same estate.

### The Registry

The Registry is the settlement's memory system. It records residents and households, buildings, plans, project stages, material decisions, repairs, changes of use, shared events, and the stories attached to particular rooms and objects.

It preserves superseded plans rather than erasing them. A building's history remains visible: the first footprint, the extension, the repaired roof, the room that changed purpose, and the people who made the change.

### Narrative rules

- Show work happening: measuring, carrying, cutting, fitting, lifting, cleaning, checking, repairing, and returning the site to order.
- Let construction change relationships through shared decisions and accumulated trust.
- Keep ordinary life present: meals, coffee, laundry, jokes, weather, tiredness, music, and the quiet satisfaction of a finished day's work.
- Give each resident agency, preferences, practical knowledge, and the ability to disagree.
- Keep the settlement open-ended. Completion of one phase reveals the next need.
- Treat plans, scenes, maps, inventories, and build logs as linked artifacts of one evolving place.
