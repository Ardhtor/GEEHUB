# The Dad Convent — Second Build Day
## The Covered Lumber Store

The morning began with the sound of a delivery truck braking on the gravel approach. Its tyres made a low crunch beneath the trees, and the driver waited until Kirk came out to guide him through the turn beside the garage. The estate was still damp from the previous night's rain. Water sat in the wheel ruts, the grass along the service court was dark and flattened, and the old workshop roof held a narrow line of runoff that fell steadily into a bucket Tyler had placed beneath the eaves. It was not the most impressive arrival the Dad Convent would ever receive, but it carried the first full load of lumber intended for the new covered store.

Luke had already walked the site twice. He had the plan folded in his back pocket and a pencil behind one ear, and he was looking from the proposed footprint toward the workshop, the delivery turn, and the line of mature trees along the western boundary. The store needed to be close enough for materials to reach the carpentry hall without crossing the residential yard, but far enough from the garage that vehicles could move between the two without reversing through the work area. He had marked a generous rectangle in lime, though one corner had been moved after Joseph noticed that the first line crossed the natural drainage route.

"That was the wrong place," Joseph said, standing with a clipboard near the shallow ditch.

Luke looked at the new line. "It was the convenient place."

"Convenient until the first storm."

Tyler, carrying a level and a bundle of stakes, gave a short laugh. "We have a drainage expert now."

"No," Joseph said. "We have eyes."

Bill came out carrying coffee in a large insulated pot and a paper bag of rolls. He had prepared a breakfast that could be eaten standing up: sausage biscuits, sliced apples, and a small container of mustard. The men took food before unloading the truck. Bill insisted that the driver eat too, and the driver accepted when he saw that nobody was going to ask him to hurry through it.

The lumber was stacked in several grades, with longer beams on the bottom and smaller framing stock above. Tyler checked the labels against the delivery note. Kirk counted bundles and looked for damage along the edges. Joseph entered the received quantities into the Registry, keeping the delivered amount separate from the material that would be used today. Luke asked the driver about the next delivery and made a note of the time window. Nobody began lifting until they had agreed where each stack would go.

The first work was not construction. It was clearing and marking. The crew moved brush from the edge of the footprint, protected the existing trees, and kept the drainage channel visible. Tyler and Kirk reset the stakes after measuring the diagonals, while Joseph checked the level against the natural fall of the ground. The store would sit on a raised base with a ventilated floor, so the timber could remain dry without being sealed into a damp enclosure. The design was modest: heavy posts, a broad roof, open sides, a rear brace wall, and a gate wide enough for the longer beams to be moved by cart.

The Grammaton arrived after the first hour, carrying a board with the first construction sequence written in thick black letters. He was the codewright, but he did not behave as though a written instruction could replace practical judgement. He placed the board on two trestles and reviewed the sequence with the crew: establish the base; confirm drainage and height; set the posts; brace the frame; lift and secure the roof beams; inspect the load paths; then add the rear wall and storage racks. Each step depended on the one before it. If a measurement changed, the sequence would be revised rather than forced to preserve the appearance of progress.

"DECLARE the footprint," he said. "BUILD the base. ALTER only when the ground tells us what the drawing missed. INHERIT the measurements so the next crew doesn't have to guess."

Tyler tapped the board. "You make it sound like code."

"It is code," said the Grammaton. "The sort other men can read."

The Architect studied the route from the service court. The Builder examined the post bases. The Keeper began a materials ledger. The Witness walked the proposed path with an empty cart to see whether the turning radius was sufficient. The Heir, who had spent his first months copying the older plans, brought a revised drawing that preserved the original footprint while shifting the north brace and widening the entrance. The revisions were small but important. The store would be easier to use and less likely to trap water or block the road.

By midday the ground was prepared, and the first base members were laid out on the compacted pad. The timber smelled fresh and resinous where the saw had cut it. The men worked in pairs, checking each length and setting the pieces in the order they would be assembled. Tyler called for a pause when one bearing surface showed a slight twist. Luke wanted to keep moving, but Tyler showed him how the defect would pull the frame out of square if they simply tightened the joint.

"That beam isn't bad," Tyler said. "It's just not straight enough for this position."

"Can it go somewhere else?"

"Probably. We mark it and decide after the frame is up."

The Keeper wrote the beam's identifier in the ledger. It was not discarded, and it was not quietly moved into the structure where nobody would notice. The material retained its history, including the reason it had not been used at the first location.

During lunch the men sat on the old workshop steps. Bill had brought a pot of beef and barley stew, thick with carrots, onions, celery, and parsley. There was bread, pickled cabbage, and a little jar of hot mustard that travelled from one end of the group to the other. The conversation moved between the morning's measurements, the condition of the garage roof, and whether the future Great Hall should have a second stair at the eastern end. Luke said it should. Kirk asked whether the circulation needed it. Joseph pointed out that a second stair would matter only if it reached a part of the building people actually used. The discussion was not settled, but it found a place in the design notes.

Bill watched them talk for a while. "You boys can make a staircase out of anything."

"That's the plan," Luke said.

"No, it isn't. The plan is to build a lumber store."

The men laughed. Bill poured more coffee.

The afternoon was spent setting the first posts and temporary braces. They did not lift the roof beams. The base still needed inspection, and the crew had agreed that the first build day would end once the frame could stand safely under its temporary supports. The posts rose above the ground in an orderly rectangle, their pale cut ends bright against the dark tree line. From the road, the structure looked incomplete, but it had acquired its first real shape. A man could stand inside it and understand where the material would be stored, how a cart would enter, and where the roof would throw rain.

The Witness walked the perimeter with the notebook. He observed that the southern approach was too soft for a loaded cart and proposed a temporary gravel strip. Kirk marked the route. Joseph took a photograph of the existing drainage. Tyler checked the braces. Luke stood at the entrance and looked through the open frame toward the workshop, imagining the long beams stacked in their future places. He did not call it finished.

The Grammaton asked him to name the state.

"Frame started," Luke said. "Base established. Posts set. Temporary bracing installed. Roof beams not yet lifted. Drainage check pending."

The codewright wrote the phrase on the board.

"Good. The record can now be inherited."

At the end of the day the crew cleaned the site. Tools returned to the rack, offcuts were sorted, and the lumber was covered against the next rain. The truck had gone. The service court was clear. The posts remained upright in the evening light, each supported by temporary bracing, and the unfinished frame cast long lines across the compacted ground.

Kirk stayed behind to check the delivery gate. Tyler inspected the temporary braces once more. Joseph walked the drainage edge and wrote down two places that needed observation after rain. Luke took the revised plan inside and placed it on the registry table beside the first site survey. Bill carried the remaining stew to the kitchen and left a portion covered for anyone who came back late.

Nobody described the day as a triumph. It had been careful, useful work. The lumber store had not been completed, but the estate now had a measured footprint, a prepared base, and the beginning of a frame. The next crew would know exactly where to start.

That evening the men sat on the porch. The workshop lights glowed through the trees, and the unfinished posts were visible beyond the service court. The conversation was quieter than it had been at lunch. Tyler leaned back with his boots on the lower rail, Kirk read through the material note, and Joseph listened while Luke described how the Great Hall might connect to the kitchen yard without making the residential path feel like a delivery lane.

Bill watched the posts for a while. "A place like this isn't built in one day."

"No," Luke said. "But it has to start somewhere."

The Registry received the first construction record before the men went inside. It did not call the store complete. It recorded a beginning, the decisions that shaped it, the material set aside for another use, and the questions the next day would have to answer.

The Dad Convent had begun building its first common structure. The estate was not yet larger, but its future had acquired a place on the ground.
