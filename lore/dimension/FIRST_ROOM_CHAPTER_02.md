# The Passage Between the Buildings
*The Beginning of the Dimension — Chapter Two* The next morning, the passage was still there.

Joseph saw it first from the window. The street had returned to its usual proportions in daylight: the opposite roofs, the parked vehicles, the shopfronts and the narrow strip of pavement. The passage stood between two buildings as plainly as it had the evening before. Its first few feet were visible, then a turn concealed whatever lay beyond. A lamp burned somewhere inside, although the morning was bright.

Kirk came to the window with two cups. He looked at the opening, then at the notes they had made. The page was still in its folder. The circle was still open at the bottom. Nothing had been added overnight. They compared the view with the previous evening's description and found one difference: the light inside the passage was now paler, closer to the colour of morning than amber. The passage had not simply preserved the previous scene. It was present in the new day.

They went downstairs. The street smelled of damp stone and coffee from the shop on the corner. A delivery van was reversing into a loading space. Two people passed the opening without looking at it. Joseph stood opposite the passage and checked the line of the walls. The buildings met above the entrance, leaving a narrow strip of sky. There was a drain at the threshold and a shallow groove in the stone where carts or hand-trucks might once have passed.

Kirk looked back toward the room. From the pavement, the window was visible above them. The passage was not a reflection in the glass. It occupied a position in the street and cast a narrow shadow across the drain. Joseph placed a small marker beside the entrance, not to claim the passage but to establish whether it remained in the same place. They took a photograph and wrote down the time. Then they waited.

A woman came out of the shop carrying a paper bag. She walked past the marker, paused, and stepped into the passage to avoid a delivery trolley. She disappeared around the first turn. A few seconds later she returned from the same opening, adjusted the bag beneath her arm, and continued along the pavement. She had used the passage as an ordinary shortcut. Joseph and Kirk did not stop her. They had learned something important: whatever the passage was, it was not visible only to them.

They waited until the trolley had gone. Then Joseph entered first, slowly enough to inspect the walls. The stone was old, but the mortar was sound. A copper pipe ran along the left side and disappeared behind a repair panel. There were marks at shoulder height where something had repeatedly brushed the wall. The floor sloped gently downward. At the turn, the passage widened into a small court with a drain in the centre and three doors. One was painted green, one was made of dark timber, and one was only a recessed arch with no door attached.

Kirk joined him. The street noise softened behind the turn, though it did not vanish. From the court they could still hear the delivery trolley, but the sound arrived as if it had travelled through a longer lane. The green door had a brass number plate with no number on it. The timber door had a handle polished by use. The open arch led to a second, narrower passage where the pale light continued.

They did not open the doors. They examined the court, noted the drain, the pipe, the wear marks, and the direction of the light. Joseph checked the wall for a street number. Kirk looked at the floor and found a small brass disc near the drain. Its surface carried the same open circle as the mark on the page. The disc was not warm. It was not moving. It had a small notch along one edge, as if it belonged to a larger fitting.

The discovery did not produce an answer. It produced a better question. The mark on the paper and the mark on the disc might share a source. They might also be a common decorative form. The brass could have been dropped by a maintenance worker. The court might have been an old service space recently uncovered by building work. Every ordinary explanation remained available, and the extraordinary explanation had not yet been established.

Joseph photographed the disc where it lay. Kirk recorded its position and the direction of the notch. They left it in place. On the way back, they passed the green door again. A faint sound came from behind it: not a voice, but a low mechanical hum that stopped when they paused. Neither touched the handle.

At the street entrance, the delivery trolley had gone. The marker remained beside the drain. They checked the time and returned to the room. From the window, the passage looked shorter than it had from inside. The court could not be seen. The pale light was still present at the first turn.

That afternoon, they added a second sheet to the folder. The first page remained untouched. The new sheet described the passage, the court, the three possible exits, the brass disc, and the ordinary passer-by who had used the lane without comment. They wrote down what they had not done: they had not opened the green door, removed the disc, or entered the second passage. A record of restraint was still a record of action.

The room had gained a route, but the route had not yet become a destination. Joseph and Kirk could return to it. They could ask the municipal office about the old service passage. They could examine the brass disc later if its removal became appropriate. They could also leave the matter alone. The dimension had not taken away their ordinary choices. It had added a few more.

In the evening, the bell sounded again. This time both heard it from the window. It came from the passage, but neither could say whether it came from the court, the green door, or somewhere beyond the second turn. The note lasted less than a second. The brass disc in the court did not move.

Kirk closed the folder. Joseph left the lamp on. They would return in the morning, not because the passage demanded it, but because they wanted to know what was there. The first crossing had begun as a decision to look again.
