# Pilling, or Forbidden
*The Beginning of the Dimension — Chapter Three* The next morning Joseph found a fine grey residue on the sleeve of his shirt.

It was not dust from the street. It gathered in little soft knots along the seam, the sort of pilling that comes from repeated friction: cloth rubbing against a chair, a bag, another surface, the ordinary wear of being used. He picked at one knot and it came away between his fingers. Beneath it the fabric was slightly thinner. He showed Kirk.

Kirk examined the sleeve, then looked at the folder on the table. The previous evening they had placed the brass disc's photograph beside the first page. The open circle appeared in both records, though the proportions were not identical. On the paper it was drawn with a narrow gap at the bottom. On the disc the gap had become a notch, cut cleanly into the metal. Neither object looked new. Both looked as though they had been handled for a long time, but neither carried the kind of wear that would establish where it had come from.

"Could just be the shirt," Kirk said.

"That's what I thought."

They did not make the sleeve into evidence merely because it had appeared beside the other evidence. Joseph changed shirts. Kirk folded the affected one and placed it in a clear envelope, writing the date and the circumstances on a label. It was a small act, almost comically formal. They had begun to understand that the dimension did not announce which details mattered. A mark could be a clue, a coincidence, or simply a mark. The only protection against making the wrong thing important was to keep the record honest.

They returned to the courtyard before noon. The passage was brighter than it had been the day before. The green door stood at the far side, its paint worn around the lock and along the lower edge. The dark timber door had a shallow scratch near the handle. The open arch still admitted the pale light. The brass disc remained by the drain, but the notch now pointed toward the green door.

Joseph stopped. Kirk checked the photograph on his phone. In the earlier image the notch pointed almost directly toward the arch.

They compared the angle against the drain and the line of the paving. The disc had moved. Not far: perhaps the width of a finger. It could have been nudged by a shoe, a cleaning brush, or the movement of water through the shallow channel. There was no reason yet to suppose that it had moved by itself. They took another photograph and left it where it was.

The green door gave a low hum. This time the sound did not stop when they approached. It continued at an even pitch, barely audible beneath the distant traffic. Joseph leaned toward the brass plate. Its blank surface reflected a distorted strip of the courtyard. Kirk noticed a line of small holes around the edge, as if a number had once been fixed there and removed. He ran a finger near them without touching the plate.

"Forbidden," he said, reading a word scratched into the lower corner of the frame.

The word was so shallow that it might have been missed in different light. It was not painted. Someone had cut it into the wood, then rubbed the surface until the letters almost disappeared. Joseph photographed it. The word did not explain whether the door was forbidden to them, whether it marked a room that had been closed, or whether it belonged to an earlier system of signs. It might even have been a warning left by someone who had no knowledge of the page or the bell. They did not open it.

Instead they examined the timber door. Its handle turned a fraction under Kirk's hand, then met resistance. The wood around the lock was polished by repeated contact. A narrow line of warm air escaped beneath it, carrying the smell of cedar, old paper, and something faintly metallic. Joseph put his palm near the gap. The air was moving outward. Whatever lay behind the door was ventilated, occupied, or connected to a space with a different pressure. That was all they could say.

The arch offered no handle and no lock. Beyond it, the second passage seemed narrower than before, though the walls had not visibly moved. A small square of sunlight lay on the floor some distance inside. It should have been impossible to see the sun from that angle. Kirk walked to the edge of the arch and watched the square. It shifted slowly, as sunlight would shift across an ordinary floor, but the light came from a direction that did not correspond to the opening.

They stood there for several minutes. A cart rattled along the street behind them. The square of light moved another few inches. The passage was not behaving like a picture or a reflection. It had duration. It contained a day.

On the wall beside the arch, Joseph found a strip of pale material caught beneath a loose stone. It looked like the edge of a paper label, softened by damp. He eased it free. Only a few letters remained: PILL—. The rest had torn away. There was a line beneath the letters, and below that a small printed circle with a gap at its base.

The word might have been "pilling." It might have begun another word entirely. The fragment was not enough to establish a connection to the residue on his shirt. Yet the two details now sat beside one another in the record: fibres gathering through friction, and a torn label whose surviving letters suggested the same process. Joseph placed it in a separate sleeve. Kirk wrote down the exact location and the fact that the fragment had been removed with care.

They began to see how the courtyard accumulated things. The brass disc had been left near the drain. The word had nearly vanished from the green door. The label had become trapped in the wall. Each object had a different history, and the courtyard was not necessarily their origin. It might be a place where lost things collected because several routes met there. It might be a maintenance court. It might be a threshold whose ordinary function had been forgotten. Their notes could hold these possibilities without choosing one.

On the way back to the street they heard the bell again. This time the note seemed to come from the timber door, but the echo arrived from the open arch. Kirk marked the two impressions separately. Joseph listened for a second note. None came.

At the room, they spread the records across the table. The first page, the photographs, the new fragment, the sleeve envelope, and the notes from the courtyard formed a small arrangement. The objects did not make a map. They made a set of relations. The open circle recurred, but each occurrence differed: ink, brass, print. The word "forbidden" appeared only on the green door. The fragment began with PILL, but its full word was unknown. The residue on the shirt could be ordinary wear. The bell had no confirmed source.

Kirk suggested that "pilling" might describe what happened when one layer rubbed against another until small pieces gathered at the surface. Joseph thought of the passage itself: a new route appearing where two buildings met, not replacing either building but forming at their seam. He thought of the page, whose words seemed to have arrived from somewhere else while remaining physically ordinary. The dimension might be accumulating at points of contact. Or they might be using a metaphor because they had not yet found a better explanation. They wrote both interpretations down.

That afternoon Joseph returned to the green door alone only as far as the street entrance, where he could see the courtyard without entering. A man in work clothes came out of the passage carrying a short ladder. He paused to speak to someone outside the frame of Joseph's view, then went back inside. The ladder was old, with a strip of blue tape around one rung. The man did not look toward Joseph. The passage was part of somebody's working day.

Joseph waited until the man had gone. He did not follow. He wrote down the ladder, the tape, and the time. The extraordinary place had acquired a maintenance worker, or at least a person carrying equipment. This was more important than any sudden revelation. A dimension that could be inhabited would have routines, repairs, deliveries, arguments, forgotten tools, and people who did not regard its existence as a mystery.

That evening Kirk returned to the question of the word on the green door. "Forbidden" could be an instruction, but an instruction required an authority and an intended reader. Neither was known. They agreed to use the word only as a transcription of the mark, not as a rule they had accepted. A sign did not become binding merely because it had been found. They could respect the possibility of danger without surrendering their judgement to an unknown writer.

They added a new heading to the folder: Pilling, or Forbidden. Beneath it they placed the sleeve record, the torn label, the door inscription, and the photographs of the disc. The title was provisional. It described the two strongest impressions of the day: accumulation through contact, and a boundary whose authority remained uncertain.

The room was quiet. The first page lay where it had always been. Joseph noticed that the paper had begun to soften along its folded edge, not enough to tear, but enough to show that it had been handled many times. He unfolded it carefully. The phrase remained unchanged. Destiny, her O Dreadmaster.

For the first time, the words seemed less like a message addressed to a person and more like a label waiting for its object. They still did not know who or what the Dreadmaster might be. They did not know whether the title was a joke, a threat, a fragment of some larger text, or a phrase that had been separated from its original context. They left it alone.

Before bed, Kirk placed a blank sheet beside the first page. He drew the open circle, leaving the gap at the bottom. Then, beneath it, he drew three small marks: one for the green door, one for the timber door, and one for the arch. He did not connect them. The map would begin with what they knew, not with what they wanted to be true.

In the morning they would ask the municipal office about the old service court. They would also look for the man with the ladder, if he appeared again. The green door could remain closed. The timber door could remain unopened. The arch could keep its impossible square of sunlight.

Nothing had been solved. But the courtyard was no longer empty, and the forbidden word had not prevented them from returning. The first layer had begun to gather at the seam.
