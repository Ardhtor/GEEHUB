# The Service Ledger
*The Beginning of the Dimension — Chapter Four*

The municipal office occupied the ground floor of a building that had been altered so many times that its entrance no longer seemed to belong to the original design. A broad stone stair ended at a glass door, the old brass lettering remained above it, and a newer sign had been fixed over the space where another department had once been named. Rainwater darkened the pavement outside. Inside, the air carried the mixed smell of paper, wet coats, floor polish, and the warm dust of radiators. Joseph and Kirk arrived with the folder between them, not because they expected the office to explain the passage, but because they had agreed that the ordinary records should be consulted before they built a theory around an unusual place.

SETH: Look at the building before they even get to the desk. You can see the layers of use in it: old stone, new glass, the sign covering another sign. This is what we're interested in. The place has had a life before these two walked in, and it will have one after they leave.

NICK: And from across the room, you can see how carefully they're carrying that folder. They aren't bringing in a solved mystery. They're bringing in a few photographs, a map they haven't finished, and details that still don't agree. That's a much better way to begin.

The clerk who came to meet them was a man in his late forties with a neat shirt, a narrow tie, and a way of listening that suggested he had spent much of his working life translating incomplete questions into something an office could answer. Joseph described the passage between the buildings, the small court beyond the first turn, and the three possible exits. Kirk showed him the photograph of the brass disc, but kept the close-up of the scratched word on the green door in the folder until they knew whether the municipal plan would offer a useful comparison. The clerk listened without smiling at the strange parts and without pretending that the details were familiar. He asked for the street name, the approximate position, and the side of the block. Joseph supplied what he could. Kirk corrected one measurement. The clerk wrote both versions in the margin.

He returned with a rolled plan and laid it across the counter. The paper was a pale cream colour, reinforced along one edge with linen tape. Its corners had been softened by handling, and a line of pencil marks crossed the printed drains and service routes. The two men leaned over it. At first the plan seemed to support the ordinary account: the buildings met along a shared wall, with a narrow service strip running behind the ground-floor units. Then Kirk found the small break in the wall line. It was not an entrance as such, only a gap in the drawing where a later amendment had been made and then crossed out. Joseph followed the pencil line with one finger. The court should have occupied the space beyond it, but the plan showed only a drainage channel and a rectangular service recess.

SETH: There. Don't rush past that little gap. You can see where the plan stops agreeing with the photograph. It doesn't prove a magical passage. It tells us the official drawing has a history of its own, and somebody changed it.

NICK: Exactly. Look at the pencil over the printed line, and then the erasure. A building can be altered on paper before anyone alters it in stone. It can also be altered in stone and never properly recorded. We have to keep both possibilities in view.

The clerk examined the photographs for longer this time. He turned the image of the courtyard sideways, compared the drain with the plan, and asked whether the open arch was at the north or east end. Neither Joseph nor Kirk could answer with confidence. The passage had made direction difficult to preserve: the street entrance, the courtyard turn, and the second narrow passage did not seem to agree with the orientation of the surrounding block. They had taken photographs, but they had not established a fixed north point. Kirk wrote that down rather than trying to rescue the answer. The clerk tapped the plan with the blunt end of his pencil and said that the service recess had been closed on one later drawing, though he could not tell whether it had been filled, enclosed, or simply removed from the record. There were older files in storage, but they would need a date or a department reference to find the correct bundle.

Joseph asked whether anyone maintained the rear service strip. The clerk said that the building management had a contractor for drains and exterior fittings, but the municipal office did not keep that contractor's daily work sheets. He pointed them toward a separate maintenance desk. It was a modest answer, though it changed the shape of their next step. The worker with the ladder might not be a municipal employee. The blue tape around the rung, the repair panel, and the marks on the pipe belonged to a working system that had records somewhere else, even if the first office could not retrieve them.

They thanked the clerk and left the plan on the counter while he made a copy of the relevant section. Outside, the afternoon had brightened without becoming warm. Rainwater still collected along the curb, and the windows across the street reflected a grey strip of sky. The two men stood beneath the awning to check the copy. It showed the same break in the wall line, now surrounded by a faint rectangular notation that might have been an old fitting or a reference to a removed structure. The copy was not enough to identify the courtyard. It was enough to give them a reason to return with a more exact orientation.

NICK: You can see the difference in their pace now. They haven't found the answer, but they've found a better question. The plan gives them something to compare when they go back.

SETH: And that's the useful part. Nobody has to announce a revelation. They have a paper record, a place to revisit, and one more person who might know how the service route is used.

They returned to the passage before the light began to fade. The street was busier than it had been in the morning. A delivery driver was backing a van toward the shop entrance, two men were carrying timber through the opposite doorway, and a bicycle had been left against the wall beside the narrow opening. The passage received all of this without ceremony. Its stone entrance remained dark beneath the buildings, while the courtyard beyond held a warmer, almost amber light. From the pavement the opening looked too narrow to contain the court they remembered. When Joseph stepped through, the space opened again around the turn. Kirk remained at the threshold for a moment, watching the delivery van in the street, then followed him.

The man with the ladder was there. He had set it against the wall beneath the copper pipe, the blue tape still wrapped around the same rung. A canvas tool bag sat on the paving beside the drain. He was checking a fastening near the repair panel and did not look up until Joseph greeted him. His face showed recognition, though not surprise. He asked whether they were looking for someone. Joseph said they had seen him the day before and wanted to ask about the passage. The man glanced toward the three doors, then back at them. He said he worked on the service fittings and came through when the drain needed attention. He had been using the route for several years. It was easier than bringing equipment around the whole block.

Kirk asked whether the green door had always been there. The man looked at it as if the question were an odd one, then said that it had been there for as long as he could remember. He had never been asked to open it. It was not on his maintenance list. The timber door, he said, was a different matter: someone used it from time to time, but he did not have a key and had never needed one. The arch was simply part of the route through to the rear service walk. He called the place the back court, not because it was an official name, but because that was what people who worked in the block called it.

SETH: Now we're getting somewhere. Listen to the ordinary language. He doesn't call it a portal or a hidden chamber. It's the back court. It's where he brings the ladder, where the drain blocks, where he has a job to do.

NICK: And that doesn't make Joseph and Kirk wrong about what they saw. It gives the place another history. You can stand where they are and see the same doors, but the man with the tools knows which fittings matter on a wet afternoon.

The worker showed them the repair panel. It was fixed with four screws, two of which had been replaced with newer heads. He explained that the pipe sometimes vibrated when the upper floors emptied their water at once. The hum they had heard behind the green door might have been the pipe carrying sound through the wall, although he had never heard it come from the door itself. Joseph asked whether the brass disc near the drain belonged to the old fitting. The man had not noticed it before. He crouched to look, but did not pick it up. The disc sat a short distance from the grate, its open circle darkened by damp, the notch still turned toward the green door.

The worker said he would leave it where it was until he knew whether it had fallen from something. He took a small torch from the canvas bag and checked the lower edge of the panel. The beam caught the worn brass for a moment, then moved on to the drain. His attention was practical and limited by the job in front of him. He had not come to solve their mystery. He had come because the pipe had been making a noise and the drain was slow.

Joseph and Kirk stepped aside while he climbed the ladder. The aluminium rails were scuffed, and one foot had a rubber cap that looked newer than the other three. As he reached the pipe, the courtyard's light moved across the wall. The open arch admitted a narrow band of pale gold, though the street outside was already losing its direct sunlight. Joseph watched the band travel over the stone. Kirk looked toward the second passage. The square of light they had seen the previous day was visible again, farther inside, resting on the floor at an angle that did not correspond to the opening.

The worker tightened the fastening. The hum stopped.

For several seconds nobody spoke. Then the sound of traffic reached them from the street, followed by the rattle of a trolley and a brief exchange between two people passing the entrance. The courtyard had not become silent; one sound had simply been removed from the mixture. Joseph wrote down the time. Kirk photographed the pipe, the panel, and the worker's ladder, asking permission before including the man's hands in the frame. The worker agreed, provided the photographs were for their notes and not a public complaint. They agreed.

NICK: That's an important distinction. The room changes when the pipe stops, but nobody has to pretend the worker did something supernatural. You heard the hum. He adjusted the fitting. The sound disappeared. That's a real sequence.

SETH: Keep that. And keep the square of light as a separate observation. One thing has an explanation that fits the evidence; the other is still sitting there on the floor, waiting for a better account.

When the worker came down, he wiped his hands on a cloth and packed the torch away. He asked whether they had found what they needed. Joseph said they had found a name for the court and a possible explanation for the hum. The man nodded, satisfied enough with the answer, and said the service walk beyond the arch sometimes flooded after heavy rain. If they intended to explore it, they should wear shoes they did not mind getting dirty. He did not make the remark mysterious. It was practical advice from someone who knew the route in wet weather.

They thanked him. He carried the ladder through the arch and disappeared down the narrow passage. The canvas bag followed, brushing the stone at the corner. A few moments later the sound of the ladder feet reached them from somewhere beyond the visible turn. Joseph and Kirk did not follow. They looked again at the green door, the timber door, and the opening beneath the fading square of light. The word scratched into the green frame remained shallow and difficult to read. The brass disc remained beside the drain. The worker had supplied a local name, a repair history, and a practical warning about water. He had not supplied a key.

At the street entrance, Joseph unfolded the plan copy. The back court was not named on it. The service recess was, though the notation was incomplete. Kirk added the worker's phrase in quotation marks and marked it as an oral description, not an official name. They now had three distinct accounts of the same small area: the municipal drawing, the maintenance worker's knowledge, and their own direct observations. Each was useful. None contained the whole place.

That evening they laid the new copy beside the first page. The paper's folded edge had softened further. The open circle remained visible beneath the sentence, while the plan showed a break in a wall line and a small unlabelled recess. Joseph placed the courtyard photographs in chronological order. Kirk wrote the worker's account on a separate sheet, leaving a blank space beneath it for a name if they learned one later. They did not try to connect every circle or every line. The archive was becoming more substantial, but it was not becoming more certain at the same rate.

SETH: From here, that's what I want you to notice. The folder is getting thicker, but they aren't forcing the pieces together. The municipal plan, the worker's description, the brass disc, the first page—they can all stay on the table without becoming one answer.

NICK: And you can see the dimension getting more ordinary as it gets stranger. Somebody has to fix the pipe. Somebody has to carry the ladder. Somebody has to remember which passage floods. The mystery is not outside daily life. It's sitting right in the middle of it.

Before bed, Joseph returned to the sentence on the first page. “Destiny, her O Dreadmaster.” It had not changed. The phrase still sounded as though it belonged to a larger declaration, and the title still had no known owner. Kirk placed the municipal copy underneath the photographs, then set the blank sheet with the open circle on top. Their records now held the room, the passage, the court, the maintenance route, and a man whose work had taken him through the same opening for years. The dimension had not introduced itself. It had acquired another witness, and the witness had been more interested in the drain than in the bell.

They left the green door closed. They left the brass disc beside the grate. In the folder, the word “forbidden” remained a transcription, the name “back court” remained a worker's phrase, and the service recess remained an incomplete mark on an old plan. In the morning they could return to the municipal office and ask for the earlier drawing. They could also ask the maintenance desk for the contractor's work sheet, provided the worker had been willing to identify the right department. The next step was practical, and the passage remained where it was.

The light in the second passage had gone when they looked again. The square was not present, but the floor still held the pale outline of where it had been. Kirk closed the folder. Joseph turned off the lamp.

Across the street, the ordinary windows reflected the last grey of the sky. The first room remained behind them, the courtyard remained beyond the turn, and the route between them had become a little easier to describe. Nothing had been solved. More of the place had become inhabited.

The back court had entered the record.
