# Under the Roots
*The Beginning of the Dimension — Chapter Eight*

They did not descend beneath the Crownheart that afternoon. Orlan had been clear about the water in the lower passage, and the repair to the bell had given them enough to do without adding an unsafe inspection to the day. Instead, they returned to the residential ridge with the service ledger copied into their notes and an appointment to meet the drainage crew the following morning. The forest received them differently on the way back. The route was no longer a sequence of unfamiliar bridges and trunks. Joseph recognized the cedar shelter with its basket of kindling; Kirk noticed the narrow channel that ran beside the fern court and the place where the path had been lifted by a root. The same landscape had become more detailed because they now had reasons to remember it.

Rain came during the night. It began as a soft tapping on the deep eaves of the dormitory, then gathered into a steady wash that moved through the canopy and down the sloped roofs. Joseph woke once to hear water running through the stone channels outside. The room smelled of cedar boards warmed by the stove and of damp wool drying on the rail. Beyond the window, the nearest trunks disappeared into mist. The forest did not become picturesque in the rain; it became practical. Roof gutters filled, paths darkened, and the lower hollows began to collect water. Somewhere across the ridge, a worker called to another beneath the sound of the weather.

At breakfast, Orlan joined them with two members of the drainage crew, Mara and Pell. Mara carried a rolled plan protected by a waxed cloth. Pell had a tool bag and a pair of heavy gloves tucked into his belt. They explained that the lower route had been closed after water began entering the root hall from a side channel. The closure had been intended as temporary, but the crew had discovered that the old drainage map did not match the ground. Several of the channels had shifted as the roots grew, and one stone culvert had settled. The gallery was safe because its supports had been inspected and repaired; the lower stair was another matter. No one had wanted to reopen it without understanding where the water went.

Joseph asked whether the lower hall was part of the original forest settlement. Mara said that the earliest records described it as a gathering room and store, but later entries suggested it had served several purposes. It might have held tools, maps, seed collections, or records from the first construction of the gallery. The names changed from one ledger to another. What remained consistent was the instruction to keep the lower route dry. Pell added that the hall was built into the root shelf rather than cut out of the tree. Its roof and walls were timber and stone, with a drainage channel running around the outside. The tree itself had not been hollowed to make a room.

Seth and Nick followed the preparations from across the scene, their viewpoint extending over the table, the rolled plan, and the rain-streaked windows.

SETH: This is what makes the discovery matter. There is a real reason the lower route was closed. The forest isn't hiding a secret behind a theatrical lock; its people have been dealing with water, shifting ground, and an old map that no longer describes the place accurately.

NICK: And the records are beginning to overlap. The bell, the gallery, the cabinet, the lower hall. Each one has a practical history, but the dates suggest that the same sequence of events has been recorded in different parts of the settlement.

After breakfast, the group took the lower path as far as the first inspection landing. The rain had eased, leaving drops suspended along the fern edges. The timber stair beneath the Crownheart was dark with moisture, but its central boards remained firm. Mara tested the handrail, Pell checked the first three treads, and Orlan compared the visible stonework with the old plan. Joseph and Kirk waited until they were invited to help. The work was slow, and the slowness was necessary. They cleared a shallow channel beside the landing, lifted a loose grate, and found that water was backing up behind a mat of needles and small branches. The obstruction was ordinary. Its consequences had been accumulating for months.

They removed it in stages. Joseph held the grate clear while Kirk lifted the compacted debris with a hooked tool. Mara directed the water into a temporary channel cut through the gravel, and Pell checked the flow at the next bend. The stream moved again, first in a thin line and then with a more consistent current. It carried dark soil and fragments of leaf mould toward the outer drain. The smell beneath the roots changed as the water cleared: less stagnant, more like wet stone and fresh earth. No hidden door opened. No voice came from the darkness. The route simply became safer by degrees.

At the second landing, the stair passed between two massive roots. Their surfaces were ridged and damp, with small patches of moss growing where the light reached them. The roots did not form a natural arch so perfect that it seemed designed for people. One rose sharply, the other travelled along the ground before turning upward, and the builders had placed a timber lintel across the gap where the passage narrowed. The joinery was careful. A narrow strip of cedar had been fitted over the top of the stone wall to prevent water from running directly into the joint. Kirk noticed the repair and pointed it out to Pell, who said the strip had been replaced three times in the records.

The lower hall appeared only after the stair turned. Its entrance was set into a slope beneath the oldest tree, with a broad roof of overlapping cedar shakes and a stone apron that carried rainwater away from the threshold. The doors were heavy, made from vertical boards joined by two crosspieces and a dark iron latch. There was no ornament beyond the small open-circle mark cut into the lintel. The mark had weathered until its edges were soft. Beside the entrance, a narrow window admitted a greenish light from the fern bed outside.

Mara checked the threshold and found it dry. Pell listened at the door, then tested the latch. The hall had no lock, only a wooden bar set into brackets on the inside. They lifted the bar and opened the doors together. The first air that came out was cool and dry, carrying the smell of cedar, old paper, dust, and a little resin. It was not the smell of a sealed tomb. It was the smell of a room that had been closed carefully and had remained protected from the worst of the weather.

The hall was larger than the entrance suggested. Heavy posts supported a low timber ceiling, and the floor was made from broad boards laid over a stone foundation. Along one wall stood cabinets with labelled drawers. Another wall held shelves for rolled plans and flat storage boxes. At the far end, a long table occupied the space beneath a high window. The window looked out at the base of the old tree, where the roots divided around a deep bed of moss. A stove stood in one corner, its flue passing through a stone collar in the roof. There were no grand symbols or ceremonial objects. The room had been made for people to work, read, meet, and keep things safe.

The first cabinet contained drainage plans. The second held records of the gallery and bridge repairs. The third had been left empty except for a single wooden case. Mara checked the floor around it before anyone moved it. The case was built from cedar, with a lid that fitted closely and two bronze clasps. The open circle appeared on its top, but this version had a fine line running through the centre. Joseph took out the page from the first room and placed it beside the case. The marks were related, though again not identical.

Orlan opened the case with a small key attached to the cabinet. Inside were several folded sheets of thick paper, a narrow strip of bark with writing on its inner surface, and a bundle of small wooden markers. The papers were not maps of the forest as it currently stood. They showed the settlement at different stages: a clearing with no gallery, a later bridge crossing the pool, a row of work buildings along the ridge, and a network of paths extending farther into the trees. In every version, the Crownheart occupied the centre, but the lower hall was marked differently. Sometimes it was a store. Sometimes it was a meeting room. On the oldest sheet, the route beneath the roots continued beyond the edge of the drawing.

Kirk examined the wooden markers. Each bore a small number and a notch cut into one side. They were not keys. They looked like pieces used to identify positions on a plan. Joseph unfolded the last sheet. It showed the open circle at the Crownheart, then a line descending beneath the tree and passing through a sequence of marks. At the bottom was a phrase written in a hand similar to the one on the first page: RECORD THE WAY AS IT IS, NOT AS IT WAS EXPECTED TO BE.

The sentence changed the meaning of the old instruction without explaining its origin. Joseph read it aloud. Mara looked at the drainage plan, then at the lower route on the sheet. Pell said that if the drawing was accurate, the passage might continue beyond the hall toward a lower terrace. The existing drainage map showed no such terrace. Orlan said that the old route could have been abandoned, or the ground could have shifted, or the plan might describe a different arrangement of the forest. They would not know until the water was traced and the route inspected.

Joseph and Kirk stood at the long table, each holding a different part of the record. The forest above them was audible through the roof: rain dripping from the eaves, a branch moving against another branch, the distant footfall of someone crossing the gallery. The lower hall did not feel separate from the woodland. Its timber posts carried the weight of the slope; its window framed the living roots; its floor remained dry because people had built and maintained channels around it. Whatever the old maps described, the place had survived through ordinary acts of care.

They made an inventory before leaving. Mara recorded the condition of the cabinets. Pell measured the threshold and the first drainage channel. Orlan listed the papers and markers in the service ledger. Joseph copied the route drawing, while Kirk sketched the hall's layout and noted the position of the stove, window, and outer drain. The work took most of the afternoon. When they finally closed the doors, the rain had stopped. A broad shaft of late light fell through the canopy above the entrance, illuminating the cedar roof and the wet stone apron.

On the return to the upper gallery, Joseph looked back once. The entrance was modest, almost easy to miss beneath the roots. It did not look like the mouth of a great revelation. It looked like a room that had waited for someone to inspect it properly. That was more interesting. The mystery was not being protected by spectacle; it had been preserved by construction, records, and the patience of people who knew that a place could outlast the purpose for which it was first made.

The lower hall had answered one question. The route was real, the room had been used, and the old maps described more geography than the current plans acknowledged. It had also produced a new problem. Somewhere beyond the hall, under the roots and below the known drainage channels, a path continued toward a terrace that the present settlement no longer remembered. Before they could follow it, they would have to understand where the water went and whether the old route still had a stable floor.

That evening, the forest settled into a damp, fragrant quiet. The dormitory windows reflected the warm interior lights, and the trunks beyond them stood dark red against the mist. Joseph placed his copied drawing beside the first page. Kirk set his sketch alongside it. The open circle appeared on both sheets, but the lines beneath it pointed in different directions. Neither man tried to correct the other's drawing. They had learned that the difference might be the most useful part of the record.
