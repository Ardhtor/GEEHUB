# The Lower Terrace
*The Beginning of the Dimension — Chapter Nine*

The drainage crew began before the breakfast hall was fully awake. From the dormitory ridge, Joseph could see lanterns moving between the trees and hear the occasional knock of a tool against stone. The rain had left the forest heavy with water. Ferns bowed beneath the drops, the bark of the sequoias had darkened, and thin streams crossed the paths wherever the ground sloped toward the lower basin. The air was cool enough that the first breath outside carried the clean smell of wet cedar and soil. Somewhere beneath the gallery, water moved through a channel that had not been heard for years.

Kirk arrived with two cups of coffee and found Joseph studying the copied map. The lower line on the drawing passed beyond the hall, then bent toward a shape that might have been a terrace or a retaining wall. The mark at its end was not the open circle. It was a small square divided by a diagonal line. Joseph had seen the same shape once before, on the reverse of a service tag in the waste district. Kirk remembered a timber frame with a matching mark among the stored materials. Neither connection was certain, but they were enough to include in the day's notes.

Orlan met them at the lower hall with Mara and Pell. The crew had brought a measuring rod, a coil of line, a level, two lanterns, and a small hand pump. They had also brought a new drainage plan, drawn from the previous day's inspection. The first obstruction had been cleared, but the water still gathered at a point beyond the hall. Mara wanted to trace the flow before opening any further part of the route. The old plan suggested a channel beneath the floor, but the crew could not assume that it remained intact.

They began outside, following the channel around the root shelf. The work was slow because the ground held water in its upper layers. Pell placed small stakes at measured intervals, and Mara marked the direction of the slope on her plan. Joseph carried the lanterns and cleared loose branches from the edge of the drain. Kirk helped move a stone cover that had settled across the channel. Beneath it, the water ran into a narrow culvert lined with old brick. The brickwork was intact, but roots had grown across its upper edge. They were not forcing their way through the masonry; they had followed the moisture and thickened along the outside, gradually changing the shape of the channel.

The route turned beneath a low retaining wall. There, the crew found a second inspection point concealed by moss and a layer of fallen needles. Its cover was made from a single slab of timber, swollen by the rain and held in place by two iron loops. Kirk tested the weight before lifting. Joseph braced the slab with a length of timber, and Pell guided it aside. The opening revealed a shallow chamber in which water moved over a bed of smooth stones. At the far end, a narrow channel disappeared beneath the slope.

Mara lowered the measuring rod. The depth changed gradually, then dropped sharply near the far wall. She said the channel might have been designed to carry water beneath the terrace, but the old drawing did not show the fall clearly enough. They would need to inspect the other side. Orlan looked at the line on the map and said the terrace should be just beyond the retaining wall, though the current path ended several yards before it. The ground between them was covered in fern and young cedar growth. No visible stair or doorway marked the route.

Seth and Nick watched the crew from the upper gallery, looking down through the trees.

SETH: The route is becoming physical now. They have a slope, a culvert, a retaining wall, and a measured fall in the channel. The old drawing is useful, but it doesn't get to overrule what the ground is actually doing.

NICK: And the men are learning the place through different kinds of attention. Joseph is tracking the marks and the sequence of records. Kirk is reading the weight and construction of the things they have to move. Neither approach is enough by itself.

The crew cleared the fern in a narrow strip, taking care not to pull at the young cedars. Beneath the growth, they found a line of flat stones set into the soil. The stones were not a path in the ordinary sense; they formed a shallow lip that directed surface water toward the concealed inspection point. Once the stones were exposed, the direction of the old drainage became easier to read. The terrace lay below them, but its upper edge had been covered by soil and vegetation. The forest had not erased it. It had grown around it.

They followed the stone lip to a flight of steps. The first two were visible; the rest disappeared beneath a thick layer of needles and dark earth. Pell cleared the upper steps with a brush. Kirk tested each tread before putting his weight on it, while Joseph marked their positions on the plan. The steps descended beside a retaining wall built from rough stone and timber. The timber had weathered almost black, but the joints remained tight. At the bottom, a low landing opened onto a broad level space.

The lower terrace was larger than the Crownheart gallery but more enclosed. It sat beneath the main root shelf, protected on three sides by the slope and on the fourth by a long timber wall. The ground was paved in irregular stone slabs, with narrow drainage channels between them. A row of posts supported a roof that had partly collapsed at the far end. Beyond the roof stood a set of doors, wide enough for equipment and supplies to pass through. The square-and-diagonal mark was carved into the lintel.

The terrace had not been abandoned in the same way as the waste district. It had been closed and then gradually covered. A bench remained beneath the roof, its surface worn smooth. Several hooks were fixed to the posts. A narrow shelf held two clay cups and a small brush, both protected from the worst of the weather. The cups were dry. The brush was brittle, but it had not been left there by accident. Someone had once used this place regularly and had expected to return.

Mara inspected the drainage channels before approaching the doors. Water was moving along the outer edge and disappearing through a stone grate. The crew cleared the grate and watched the flow. It remained steady. Pell checked the roof supports and found that two posts would need bracing before the structure could be used. Orlan examined the doors. Their hinges were corroded but sound, and the latch had been tied back with a strip of old leather. He did not open them until the crew had finished checking the floor.

Inside was a long store room with a high ceiling and a row of timber racks. The first shelves held rolled canvas, spare lantern housings, and bundles of rope. Farther along were boxes of ceramic fittings, iron brackets, and wooden components shaped for a system none of them recognized. The floor was dry. A narrow window at the back admitted a green line of light from a gap in the roots. At the end of the room, a second door stood open by a few inches.

Kirk found the first square mark on a wooden frame leaning against the wall. It was made from thick oak, with a broad central opening and two crossbars set at different heights. Joseph recognized the proportions from the service tag in the waste district. The frame was not a door and did not appear to support a roof. It might have been a measuring device, a movable partition, or part of a larger assembly. The crew left it in place and recorded its dimensions.

Behind the second door was a small room containing a long workbench, a cabinet of hand tools, and a wall covered with diagrams. The drawings showed the Crownheart gallery, the lower hall, the drainage channels, and the terrace. One diagram extended farther beneath the forest than any of the maps they had seen. It showed a sequence of rooms following the slope below the roots, linked by covered passages and water channels. At the bottom of the sheet was a larger version of the divided square. Beneath it, a line of writing read: THE LOWER WORKS ARE A WAY THROUGH, NOT A PLACE TO REMAIN.

Joseph read the sentence twice. Kirk studied the diagram. The lower terrace was not the end of the route. It was a service point along a passage that continued beneath the forest. The phrase did not tell them where the way led, but it changed the scale of the discovery. The Crownheart was the visible centre; the lower works were an older network beneath it, built to move people, materials, water, and records through the woodland without disturbing the upper paths.

Mara said the drawings would need to be checked against the actual structure. The lower works might have shifted, flooded, or been partly blocked. The fact that one room remained dry did not prove the rest was safe. Pell agreed and began marking the roof supports that needed repair. Orlan made a copy of the diagram for the service ledger. The crew would return with additional equipment and a structural survey before opening the next passage.

Joseph and Kirk stayed behind long enough to document the room. The workbench had a shallow groove running along its rear edge, perhaps to keep small pieces from falling. The tool cabinet contained chisels, measuring rules, a small mallet, and several fittings wrapped in cloth. On the wall, beneath the diagrams, someone had pinned a sheet of paper with a list of names. Some were crossed out; others had a small circle beside them. The last entry was not a name but a phrase: KEEP THE UPPER FOREST OPEN.

The words seemed to describe the reason for the lower works. The forest above was not meant to become a maze of service routes and storage buildings. The paths and clearings were to remain open, allowing people to walk beneath the trees, gather in the halls, and move between settlements without having to pass through industrial yards. The lower network carried the infrastructure out of sight. Its purpose was not secrecy for its own sake. It was to protect the character of the woodland while allowing the settlement to function.

The discovery made the sequoia forest feel larger, not smaller. There were upper paths for ordinary life, the Crownheart for gathering and shared records, the lower hall for preservation, and a network beneath the roots for the work that kept the place running. Each layer had a different purpose, and each depended on the others. The forest was not an untouched wilderness with a few buildings placed inside it. It was a carefully inhabited environment whose architecture had learned to remain subordinate to the living trees.

When they returned to the upper gallery, the rain had stopped completely. Sunlight moved through the canopy in broad green-gold bands, and the wet trunks shone with a deep red-brown colour. A worker was replacing a handrail board near the outer bridge. Another was carrying a basket of split wood toward the kitchen. The ordinary life of the settlement continued above the newly discovered terrace. Joseph looked down through the trees and understood that the lower works had been designed precisely so that this upper life could continue without interruption.

The day's record ended with three practical decisions: the drainage channel would remain open; the terrace roof would be braced before further access; and the lower diagram would be compared with the waste district's stored components. The divided square appeared in both places, and the old oak frame might be part of the same system. They had found a connection between the forest and the district of discarded materials, but they did not yet know whether the objects had been moved there after the lower works closed or whether they had always belonged to a larger network.

That evening, Joseph and Kirk returned to their room with the copied diagrams spread across the table. The first page, the Crownheart drawing, the lower hall plan, and the terrace map formed a sequence that neither could have assembled alone. Beyond the window, the sequoias rose through the dusk. The forest had a centre above ground and a passage beneath it. The next stage would require them to leave the familiar paths again, this time with a clearer idea of what they were looking for.
