# The Open Table
*The Beginning of the Dimension — Chapter Twelve*

The Crownheart gallery had been built for gatherings long before Joseph and Kirk arrived. Its broad curve followed the edge of the basin, with the oldest sequoias standing across the water and the outer path rising toward the residential ridge. The beams were dark cedar, joined by heavy brackets that had been replaced in stages. The floor carried the marks of generations of use: polished boards near the benches, shallow dents where equipment had been set down, and pale repairs where the timber had split under weather. The place was not ceremonial in the manner of a palace. It had the confident scale of a room designed for people who expected to return, and its furniture could be moved when a meeting became a meal or when a repair crew needed space to lay out a plan.

By midmorning, the gallery had filled with people from the upper paths and lower works. Mara brought the revised survey; Pell carried a drawing of the reinforced gate; Orlan had the service ledger open to the record of the old closure. Several residents had come from the cedar settlement beyond the fern courts. Two carpenters arrived from the lower workshop, and a woman who kept the kitchen at the Crownheart brought a large pot of beans, onions, and smoked vegetables, together with brown bread and bowls that could be carried outside. The meeting was expected to last, and no one had confused a discussion about water with a reason to keep people hungry.

Joseph and Kirk placed their records on the table. The first page was kept inside its folder. The brass disc was shown only in a photograph, because neither of them wanted to remove it from the courtyard before its condition had been established. The blue strip of wood from the lower gate was placed beside the copied drawing. Its worn edge and faded pigment made it look like a piece of an old instrument rather than a message. The open circle appeared on all three records in different forms. The new mark, with a curved line and three short strokes, remained unexplained.

SETH: Look at the table. This is a different kind of scene from the lower chamber. The records are here, the people who maintain the routes are here, and the people who live above them have come to hear what the survey found. You can see the whole question in one place now.

NICK: And nobody has made the table into a throne. The kitchen is still working, the carpenters have their drawings, and the residents can ask questions. The decision is going to happen in the same room where people eat and repair things. That's the right scale for it.

Mara began by explaining what the crew had measured. She described the drainage channels, the lower junction, the uneven waterline, the inspection gallery, and the circular gate. She showed the group where the old plan disagreed with the actual passage and where Neptune's reflection had suggested a third route. She was careful with the verbs. The crew had measured the first branch and found a gate. They had not entered the basin beyond it. They had installed a support so that the mechanism could be inspected safely. The water's reflection had shown a further route, but no one had followed it. The drawing contained measured facts, observed correspondences, and unresolved possibilities, each marked in a separate way.

Pell explained the bracket repair. He set a small timber model on the table and showed how the new support transferred the load to the sound part of the frame. He described the crack near the upper bolt and the risk of turning the wheel before the counterweight housing had been inspected. One of the carpenters asked whether the timber was old enough to have weakened throughout. Pell said that some sections were sound and some required replacement. He would not recommend replacing the entire gate until they had checked each joint. It was a repairable structure, not a relic that needed to be preserved untouched.

Orlan described the closure. The old records had named a stone fall, then flooding, then a temporary barrier. The dates did not line up. He remembered a crew being sent down with lanterns and a pump, and he remembered the lower route being closed after the return of a man with a damaged leg. He could not remember whether the injury had happened in the lower works or on the upper path. The record had no name beside the incident. The people listening did not ask him to guess. One of the older carpenters remembered that the lower basin had once been used to store timber before it was cut for the galleries. Another remembered a period when water had risen beneath the residential ridge after a storm. Their accounts did not fully agree, but they showed that the closure had affected more than the gate.

A resident from the upper settlement asked whether opening the route would change the water supply. Mara said that it might, depending on the direction and rate of flow. The upper channels were currently stable, but the hidden branch could carry water away from the lower basin or bring it toward the settlement during heavy rain. She wanted a monitored trial with a temporary barrier and a clear overflow route. The residents would need to know where the water was going and how quickly the level could change. The trial should be short enough to reverse and long enough to reveal how the channel behaved under ordinary conditions.

Another resident asked whether the passage would become a public route. The carpenters wanted access to the lower store, but the kitchen staff used the upper gallery and did not want tools carried through the dining area. A worker from the cedar settlement said that the forest paths had become crowded during the last repair season. She did not want a new route opened unless there was a plan for carrying materials without blocking the ordinary walkways. The discussion shifted from whether the gate was mysterious to what the opening would actually mean for the people who used the forest.

ZEUS arrived while the questions were still being considered. He did not enter with a thunderclap. The air simply grew bright above the gallery, and a distant bank of clouds became visible between the trunks. He stood at the outer railing and looked over the people gathered around the table. His presence changed the scale of the room, but it did not interrupt the meeting. He waited until Mara had finished answering a question about the overflow route.

ZEUS: You have gathered the evidence in the open. Now let the decision be made in the open as well. I will not call the storm to force an answer. But if the route is opened, the settlement must know what will be protected, what may be lost, and who is responsible when the weather changes.

A carpenter asked whether Zeus intended to hold the storm over the forest until the vote was complete. Zeus said that the clouds were moving east and that he had no wish to turn the meeting into an endurance test. The answer drew a quiet laugh from the table. He did not appear offended. His authority was not diminished by the ordinary humour of the people listening to him. In fact, he seemed to regard it as evidence that they had not surrendered their judgement.

NEPTUNE came to the opposite side of the gallery. The pool below the rail darkened, and a deep blue reflection moved beneath the surface. He stood beside the water and looked down into it. The residents grew quiet, but he did not ask for silence. He spoke toward the pool first, as if checking the current before addressing the people.

NEPTUNE: The lower water is older than the present routes. It has carried timber, soil, leaves, and the marks of every channel that has been opened or closed. You can keep it covered, but you cannot make its history disappear. The gate is not the beginning of the water. It is one decision made along its course.

Mara asked whether the route could be opened without disturbing the upper channels. Neptune answered that the water could be directed, but the settlement would have to observe it over time. He could reveal the old current. He could not make a changing river behave like a fixed pipe. The lower basin remembered seasons, and its level would rise and fall with conditions that could not be inferred from one morning's reading.

ZEUS: Then the trial needs an end point and a limit.

NEPTUNE: A limit is useful when it can be seen. It is dangerous when people mistake it for the whole river.

The two figures looked at each other. Zeus wanted a public standard, and Neptune wanted the standard to remain responsive to the current. Their disagreement had become more precise. Neither wanted reckless opening. They differed over whether a safe route could be governed by a fixed rule or whether the water required continual attention. Mara said that the engineering answer was a monitored range: a level at which the gate could be held, a level at which it should be closed, and a level at which the crew must leave the lower chamber. The thresholds could be reviewed as more measurements became available. Zeus accepted that. Neptune said that a threshold should be read as a warning, not as a promise that conditions would never change.

God Daddy arrived with the kitchen crew. He carried a tray of bowls and set it at the end of the table. He helped move two benches so that an older resident could sit near the warm wall, then brought another cup of water to Pell, who had been speaking for some time without taking a break. He did not interrupt the technical discussion. When he had finished serving the food, he sat at the edge of the gathering and listened.

One of the residents asked him whether he supported opening the lower route. God Daddy said that he supported giving people the information and the freedom to decide. Some would want to use it. Some would prefer the upper paths. Some might be uneasy about a passage whose history was still incomplete. None of those responses should be treated as a failure of courage. Protection was not the same as making everyone accept the same risk.

GOD DADDY: A safe place isn't one where nobody is allowed to choose. It's one where people can understand the choice, ask for help, and leave without being made ashamed of leaving. Keep the exit clear. Keep the food warm. Let the people who live here have a say in what happens to their home.

The kitchen keeper placed another pot on the table. She said that if the route became a regular work passage, the lower crew would need a separate rest station. Their clothes would get wet, and they would need somewhere to dry boots and store tools without bringing mud into the dining hall. God Daddy agreed and asked her what space she would need. She pointed to a disused room beside the upper stair. It had a stove flue, a drain, and a door that opened onto the service path. It could be cleaned and fitted with hooks, benches, and a shelf for dry clothing. The suggestion was added to the plan.

SETH: That's a good example of the way he's working. He's not taking over the meeting. He's hearing what the people need if the passage becomes part of their lives.

NICK: And it changes the plan. A rest station, dry boots, somewhere for the tools. That's what protection looks like when it becomes architecture instead of a speech.

Joseph and Kirk were asked to describe what they had seen at the first room and in the courtyard. They did not claim that the open circle, the bell, the brass disc, and the lower gate were all parts of one mechanism. Joseph explained the separate observations and the order in which they had occurred. Kirk described the sound of the bell and the way the vibration had travelled through the timber supports. He said that the gate's low note resembled the earlier bell, but he could not establish a direct connection. The first page remained a separate artifact. Its phrase, “Destiny, her O Dreadmaster,” had not been explained by the lower works. The group listened without trying to turn the sentence into a command.

A younger resident asked whether the Dreadmaster would be the person who controlled the gate. Joseph said they did not know who the title referred to. Kirk added that nobody had the right to assign the title to a person simply because the room contained an old mechanism. The answer was accepted. The phrase remained strange, and it remained in the archive. The lower route had not solved the first page.

The meeting reached its decision late in the afternoon. The gate would not be fully opened. A limited trial would be carried out after the next dry-weather inspection, with a small crew, an accessible return route, a temporary overflow barrier, and water-level markers visible from both sides of the chamber. The trial would last only as long as the measurements remained within the agreed range. Anyone could decline to enter the lower works. The upper forest paths would remain open, and the work crew would use the service route rather than the dining gallery. A rest station would be prepared beside the upper stair. The people responsible for maintenance would review the results in a second meeting before any wider access was considered.

Zeus agreed to recognize the decision publicly and to make the storm conditions visible if the weather changed during the trial. Neptune agreed to reveal the current's direction at the measured points, while allowing the crew's instruments to provide the practical record. God Daddy agreed to help prepare the rest station and make sure the crew had a clear exit and adequate supplies. None of them claimed that the arrangement would remove every risk. It made the risk visible and gave the people of the forest a way to respond.

At dusk the gallery emptied gradually. The carpenters carried their drawings back to the lower workshop. Mara rolled the plan into its case. Pell collected the brass level and wrapped it in cloth. Orlan closed the service ledger and returned the old key to its hook. The kitchen crew packed away the remaining food, but left a covered pot for the night workers. Joseph and Kirk remained at the table with the first page and the photograph of the brass disc. The day had turned an unknown passage into a public question with a limited answer. It had not explained the dimension. It had given the inhabitants a way to live with the next step.

Seth and Nick watched the last lanterns being lit along the gallery. Across the pool, the oldest sequoia carried its healed scar in the deepening evening light. The lower route lay beneath the roots, the water continued through its channels, and the rest station had already been marked on the building plan.

SETH: So there's the decision. Not a grand opening, not a dramatic command. A measured trial, with the people who work here deciding what they can safely do.

NICK: And everybody has left something in the plan. Neptune gives them the current, Zeus gives the public standard, God Daddy makes sure the people have a place to recover and a way out. The next step is going to show whether the arrangement works.

Joseph looked across the water. For a moment, the surface reflected the gallery lamps and the trunks beyond them. Then another line appeared beneath the reflection: a deep blue curve, followed by three short strokes. The mark was the same as the one on the strip of wood recovered from the lower gate. It travelled slowly across the pool and disappeared beneath the far bank.

Kirk saw it too. He did not say what it meant. He took the photograph of the blue marker from the folder and placed it beside the first page. The two objects did not match. The open circle remained open; the blue curve remained unclosed. Between them was a blank space large enough for another account.

The trial had been agreed. The lower route remained closed for the night. Beyond the Crownheart, the sequoia forest continued through the cedar paths and the fern courts, with kitchens, workshops, bridges, and residences lit for the evening. The gods had not taken the forest away from its inhabitants. They had become part of the argument by which the inhabitants would decide what the forest could become.
