# The Trial of the Lower Gate
*The Beginning of the Dimension — Chapter Thirteen*

The trial began under a sky that had not yet decided whether it would rain. The upper canopy held a thin mist, and the Crownheart's outer gallery shone with the damp light of early morning. Workers had arrived before the kitchen opened. They checked the rest station beside the stair, laid dry clothing over the rail, filled the water jars, and placed the first-aid case where it could be reached without crossing the working area. Mara's plan had been copied onto a larger board and fixed beside the entrance. The agreed water limits were marked in dark pencil. A second sheet listed the people entering the lower works, the people remaining above, the signals for stopping the trial, and the route by which anyone could leave. The arrangement was not elaborate, but nothing important had been left to memory alone.

Pell arrived carrying the final bracket inspection. The new support had held overnight. He had tested the bolts, checked the timber for fresh splitting, and compared the alignment with the previous day's measurements. The gate remained under pressure, but the load was distributed more evenly than before. Orlan brought the service ledger and read the old instruction aloud: hold the gate until the basin is ready. He then added the current date and the name of the trial. The words did not tell anyone that the basin was ready. They showed that the people responsible for it had begun a new test.

Seth and Nick were positioned on the upper gallery, looking down toward the entrance to the lower works. From their side of the scene, the viewer could see the board of limits, the small group of workers, the wet roofs between the trees, and the dark opening beneath the Crownheart. The shot held long enough for the practical details to become part of the event. The trial was not a dramatic opening detached from its surroundings. It was a working day with a record, a team, a meal waiting above, and a route that had to remain safe.

SETH: Take a look at the entrance. The first thing they've done is put the limits where everyone can see them. The gate has been the centre of the mystery, but the board is what makes this a public trial. Everybody knows what the crew intends to do, and everybody knows when they're supposed to stop.

NICK: And the people staying above have a job too. They're watching the upper waterline, keeping the rest station ready, and making sure the route back is clear. You can see the whole system here, not just the people going below.

Mara checked each person's equipment. Pell had the level and the inspection tools. Orlan carried the ledger and the old key. Joseph and Kirk had lanterns, a coil of line, and the copied diagrams. Two additional workers, Sera and Halven, were assigned to the upper water markers. They had volunteered because they knew the channels nearest their homes. Neither wanted to enter the gate chamber, but both were willing to monitor the water from the gallery. Their decision was accepted without argument. A trial did not require every person to take the same part.

God Daddy arrived last, carrying a pair of dry blankets and a small covered basket from the kitchen. He checked that the rest station had enough seating and that the passage to the upper path was clear of tools. Then he asked Sera whether the water markers were visible from the gallery. She pointed toward the rail. God Daddy moved a bench a few inches so that the marker could be seen without obstructing the walkway. He put the blankets on the shelf and left the basket closed until the group returned. He did not insist on accompanying the crew. Mara asked him to remain near the rest station, where he could help anyone who needed to leave. He agreed.

ZEUS appeared above the far side of the basin. The cloud bank had gathered beyond the ridge, but the sky over the Crownheart remained bright enough to show the detail of the gallery beams. He looked toward the markers, then toward the lower entrance. He raised one hand. The wind eased along the upper path, and the mist stopped moving for a moment. The change was not a command to begin. It gave the crew a stable interval in which to take their first readings.

NEPTUNE was already present in the water beneath the gallery. His reflection travelled along the pool, following the same blue curve that Joseph and Kirk had seen on the strip of wood. The current was visible as a fine movement beneath the surface. He did not tell Mara what to do. He watched the waterline and waited for the instruments to establish their baseline.

The crew descended. The lower works were cool and quiet, with the water moving beneath the stone floor and the old oak frame standing beside the inspection chamber. The lantern light made the grain of the timber visible. At the gate, Pell checked the bracket one final time and placed a chalk mark across the new support. If it shifted, the line would break. Mara set the measuring rod in the channel. Joseph and Kirk took their positions at the wheel and the counterweight housing. Orlan opened the ledger to the old instructions. The group repeated the agreed signal: three short knocks against the rail would mean stop; one continuous note from the hand bell would mean leave the chamber by the return passage.

SETH: This is the part where the room has to do what the plan says it can do. Watch the chalk mark on the support and the water marker beside Mara. Those two things give the crew a way to see whether the mechanism is staying within the limits.

NICK: And the hand bell is not the old mysterious bell. It is an agreed signal for this crew. They're using a familiar sound for a practical reason, and everybody knows what it means.

Mara gave the signal to begin. Kirk took the wheel while Joseph steadied the counterweight housing. Pell watched the bracket. Orlan read the sequence from the service ledger. The wheel resisted at first. The old grease had hardened, and the bronze rim was cold beneath Kirk's hands. He applied pressure gradually. The counterweight shifted by less than the width of a finger. Water moved under the gate, then stopped. Mara read the level. It had changed by a small amount, still within the agreed range.

The second movement was smoother. A pale line appeared along the lower edge of the gate. Water entered the channel on the far side, not as a surge but as a slow, clear sheet that followed the stone gutter. The floor gave a short vibration. Pell examined the chalk mark. It remained intact. Joseph felt the pressure through the wheel and changed his grip. Kirk held the position until Mara had taken another reading. The water advanced another few inches.

The chamber beyond the gate began to brighten.

At first it seemed to be a reflection cast by the lanterns. Then the light widened across the far wall, showing a low arch and a passage beyond it. The stone was pale and smooth, unlike the rough walls of the lower works. A row of small square recesses had been built into the arch, each one holding a piece of dark wood. The arrangement was deliberate. It resembled the divided square mark found on the timber frame, but there were more recesses than the drawing had shown. The light did not flicker. It appeared to come from the passage itself.

NEPTUNE: The water has found the old line.

Mara checked the measuring rod. The flow had become steady. Its direction matched the blue curve in the lower diagram. She asked whether the water was entering the basin or only reaching the first chamber. Neptune said that it had reached the first chamber. The basin lay farther along the route, beyond a bend that had been sealed by the old gate. The crew could proceed to the threshold and inspect the passage, but they should not attempt to reach the basin on this trial.

ZEUS: The boundary is clear. You may inspect the first chamber. The next passage remains closed until the measurements are made.

His voice carried through the stone without echoing. The water held its line. The gate did not move further. Zeus had made the limit visible; Neptune had revealed the route; the crew still had to decide whether to cross the first threshold.

Mara looked at Pell. He checked the support, then the waterline. Both remained within range. Orlan read the next line of the old ledger. It described a landing, a shelf, and a room used for receiving materials. The entry did not mention any danger beyond the gate. Joseph and Kirk waited. Nobody hurried them.

Mara gave permission for the inspection team to cross. Joseph went first with the lantern. Kirk followed with the line. Pell remained at the gate with Mara, monitoring the support and water. Orlan stood at the threshold with the ledger open. The new passage was dry. Its floor consisted of large slabs of pale stone, carefully fitted and worn smooth along the centre. The air smelled of cedar, old paper, and mineral water. The walls were built with a precision that suggested a different period of construction from the gallery above.

The first chamber was small. It contained a bench, a table, a narrow shelf, and a window set into the far wall. The window did not look onto the forest outside. It looked onto a room.

Joseph stopped with the lantern held at chest height. The room beyond the glass was familiar in its proportions: a table, two unmatched chairs, a lamp whose shade leaned slightly to one side, and a window facing an ordinary street. The light was late afternoon. A vehicle passed outside. Somewhere in the building, a pipe knocked twice, paused, and knocked once more.

The first room.

Kirk came beside him. He recognized the table before he recognized the lamp. The room was not a picture that happened to resemble the place where the page had appeared. It had the same visual depth, the same window position, the same small flaw in the lamp shade. Yet there was no page on the table. There were no people in the chairs. The room existed beyond the glass with the quiet completeness of a place being lived in elsewhere.

Joseph did not touch the window. He took one photograph, then another from a slightly different angle. Kirk recorded the sound of the pipe. The two men compared what they heard. Joseph heard the vehicle first; Kirk heard the pipe. They did not try to make the observations identical. The scene had returned them to the beginning, but the room beyond the glass was not necessarily the same room at the same time.

SETH: There it is. Look through the window. The first room is back in front of them, with the same table, the chairs, and that lamp. But don't call it a time machine yet. They have a view of a room that matches their first record. That's what they can say.

NICK: And notice the absence. The page isn't on the table. Nobody is sitting there. They have found a correspondence, not the missing explanation. The room is present, but the question is still open.

Joseph leaned closer to the glass. The street beyond the room seemed to continue in both directions, but the angle was wrong for the building they remembered. A thin strip of amber light appeared along the lower edge of the window. Then a second shape entered the room, moving across the far wall. It was not a person. It was the shadow of a tree, though there should not have been a tree outside that city window. The shadow moved slowly, as if branches were shifting in a wind. Kirk looked back toward the passage. The air carried a faint smell of wet bark. Somewhere beyond the chamber, water moved through the lower channel.

God Daddy appeared at the threshold. He had come down after receiving the agreed signal from the upper station. He did not cross into the chamber until Joseph and Kirk had stepped back from the window. He asked whether they were steady and whether the route remained clear. They said yes. He looked through the glass, then at the two men. He did not tell them what the room meant. He asked whether they wanted to continue looking or return to the gate. Their answer mattered more than his curiosity.

Kirk said they should return. The trial had been limited to the first chamber, and the view had given them enough to record. Joseph agreed. They had not come down to force the first room to explain itself. They had come to inspect the passage safely. God Daddy nodded and stood beside them as they left. He did not hurry them. He kept the space clear and waited until both men had crossed back into the lower works before turning away from the window.

The gate remained stable. Mara had recorded a rise in water level, then a steady interval. Pell's chalk mark remained unbroken. The support had carried the load. The crew began the closing sequence. Kirk turned the wheel back by the agreed amount while Joseph watched the counterweight. Water withdrew from the far channel in a slow line. The pale light beyond the gate dimmed, but it did not vanish immediately. The last thing visible through the opening was the window of the first room, with the tree shadow moving across it.

When the gate returned to its resting position, Mara recorded the final waterline. The level had changed by less than the agreed maximum. The old passage had opened and closed without a surge. The trial had not reached the basin, but it had established that the first chamber could be accessed under controlled conditions. The crew packed the instruments and returned to the upper gallery.

God Daddy was waiting at the rest station. He had warmed the food, brought fresh water, and placed dry towels beside the bench. Pell sat down first, rubbing his hands together to restore their warmth. Mara reviewed the readings while eating. Orlan entered the trial in the service ledger. Joseph and Kirk added their observations of the room beyond the glass. The photograph did not reproduce the full visual effect, but it preserved the window, the table, and the empty chairs.

Zeus arrived after the crew had finished the first meal. He asked for the report and listened as Mara explained the waterline and the support's condition. She recommended a second trial before any longer crossing. The gate had behaved within the agreed limits, but the passage beyond the first chamber remained unknown. Zeus accepted the recommendation. He did not call the successful opening a victory or declare the route safe. The storm had held back for the trial; it could not guarantee what would happen under another season's rain.

Neptune appeared in the upper pool as the first afternoon light reached the gallery. The blue line moved beneath the surface again, but this time it stopped at the edge of the reflection rather than continuing toward the far bank. He looked toward Joseph and Kirk.

NEPTUNE: The room is not the basin. The water has carried you to the first chamber. The deeper route remains.

Joseph asked whether the room had been there before the forest. Neptune said that the water remembered the route, but memory did not always preserve the order in which places had been built. The first room might be older than the chamber, or the chamber might have been built to observe it. He could not tell them which account came first. Joseph wrote the answer down without forcing it into a diagram.

The three powers met at the upper edge of the gallery as evening approached. Zeus stood near the railing, watching the cloud bank move beyond the ridge. Neptune remained beside the pool, his reflection extending beneath the surface. God Daddy stood near the rest station, checking that the crew had everything it needed before the walk back. They did not hold a ceremony. Their presence was part of the day's record because each had taken a different responsibility: Neptune had revealed a remembered route, Zeus had made the limit and the public conditions visible, and God Daddy had protected the people and their ability to choose when to stop.

Seth and Nick watched from across the pool, where the dark water reflected the great trunks and the warm lamps along the gallery. The viewer's position held the three figures, the crew, and the open forest in one field of attention.

SETH: That's the result. They opened the gate, crossed the first chamber, and found the first room on the other side of a window. It's a real discovery, but it isn't the whole answer.

NICK: And they brought the important things back with them: measurements, photographs, a closed gate, and a decision to test again before going farther. The mystery has become a route with conditions. That's progress.

The gallery grew quiet after the crew departed. The kitchen keeper covered the remaining food and set aside a bowl for the night watch. A worker walked the upper path, checking the lanterns one by one. The Crownheart pool reflected the window of the gallery and, for a moment, another window beyond it. The first room was not visible in the water now. Only the tree shadow remained, moving slowly across the surface as the wind passed through the highest branches.

The trial had reached its limit and stopped there. The old gate was closed. The lower chamber had been entered and left without damage. The first room had appeared beyond the water route, but no one had learned who placed the page on the table or why the sentence addressed the Dreadmaster. The next trial would have to begin with the same care, carrying forward what had been seen without pretending the unknown had become familiar.
