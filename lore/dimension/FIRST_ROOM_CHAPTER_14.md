# The Forge Under the Roots
*The Beginning of the Dimension — Chapter Fourteen*

The morning after the lower-gate trial, the Crownheart settlement returned to its ordinary noises. Water ran through the upper channels. A kitchen door opened and shut while someone carried a basket of bread to the gallery. The night watch brought in the last of the damp blankets, and Pell left the chalk-marked inspection sheet on the long table so that the people who had not gone below could read it for themselves. Nobody had decided that the first chamber was safe for general use. Nobody had decided that it was forbidden, either. The gate remained closed, the measurements remained on the board, and the photograph of the room beyond the window had been placed in a folder beside the service ledger. Joseph and Kirk had spent part of the night comparing the two sounds they had recorded: the passing vehicle and the three knocks in the pipe. Their accounts still differed in the order in which they had noticed them. They had not tried to correct one another.

Seth and Nick stood across the upper workshop, where the morning light reached between the heavy posts and fell in long bands across the floor. The workshop occupied a sheltered terrace beneath the Crownheart's broad roots. Its roof was built from old sequoia beams, darkened by years of smoke and oil; the floor was fitted with stone around the forge and thick oak boards near the benches. Tools hung from pegs in careful rows, but the room did not have the untouched appearance of a display. Handles had been replaced, chisels sharpened, and two different generations of hinges were stored in a crate beneath the worktable. A wide chimney carried the forge smoke through a gap in the roots. Outside, the forest was green with recent rain.

SETH: Look over the bench with us. They've brought the gate measurements up here, but they haven't brought the gate itself into the workshop. That distinction matters. The crew wants to understand what the structure is doing before anybody asks it to do more.

NICK: And the room has a history of its own. Those tools are repaired and used. The people working here don't treat an old object as automatically precious or automatically disposable. They find out what it does, what it can still do, and what it would take to keep it working.

A man was standing beside the forge with one hand resting on the broad rim of the anvil. He was powerfully built, with a deep chest, heavy shoulders, strong forearms, and hands marked by heat, soot, and repeated work. The light from the banked fire caught the texture of his skin and the dark metal dust along his wrists. He wore a practical leather apron over a plain work shirt. There was no ceremonial armour, no crown, and no attempt to make the workshop look like a throne room. A piece of bronze lay on the anvil beside a wooden pattern. He watched Pell compare the gate bracket drawing with the photograph, then looked toward Joseph and Kirk.

HEPHAESTUS: You found a room through a window. You did not find a door.

Joseph nodded. He explained that the window had shown the first room, with its table, two unmatched chairs, leaning lamp, and street. He described the pipe knocks and the missing page. Kirk added that the window had seemed complete, not like an image projected onto glass. He had been able to see depth in the room, and the passing vehicle had moved through it without any visible distortion.

HEPHAESTUS: Then preserve the window as you found it. Do not turn a thing that can be observed into a thing you have to force open. A hinge is not improved by being mistaken for a key.

Hephaestus introduced himself without a title. Orlan knew the name from a small metal stamp on the oldest tools in the workshop, though he had never met the maker. The mark was not proof that every object bearing it had been made by the same hand. Hephaestus said that some marks had been copied, some inherited, and some used by workshops that had continued a design long after its first maker was gone. He would not claim an object's whole history from a single stamp. The distinction pleased Orlan, who had spent much of his life keeping records that were useful precisely because they did not pretend to be complete.

Pell placed the gate-bracket drawing on the bench. Hephaestus studied the line where the newer support met the old frame. He did not immediately reach for a hammer. He asked what kind of timber had been used, how long the support had carried the load, whether the old bolt holes had been measured before the new bracket was fitted, and whether anyone had recorded the movement of the gate during the trial. Pell answered each question. Mara supplied the water readings. Joseph showed the photograph of the chalk mark. Kirk set the audio recording beside it. Hephaestus listened to the short sequence twice, then asked them to play it again at its original speed.

HEPHAESTUS: The work has already told you several things. The timber is carrying a load. The bracket is holding. The water is moving. The recording contains a rhythm. None of those statements tells us who made the first chamber, or why the first room appears beyond the glass. Keep the statements separate. A good joint begins with the parts that are actually there.

Zeus arrived while Pell was setting out the measuring tools. He looked at the drawing, then at Hephaestus, and asked whether the forge could make a stronger bracket for the lower gate. Hephaestus said it could make a different bracket. Whether the new one would be stronger depended on the material it joined, the direction of the load, and the conditions in which it would be used. Zeus did not object to the correction, but he wanted the practical consequence stated plainly: no modification should be installed until the crew and the residents had seen what it changed.

ZEUS: Authority cannot be made responsible after the work is finished. If we change the gate, the decision and its conditions must be visible before the change.

HEPHAESTUS: And a public decision cannot make an unsuitable piece of metal fit. You can authorise the work. You cannot authorise the material to behave differently from what it is.

The exchange was not hostile. Zeus looked again at the drawing. Hephaestus turned it so that the joint was visible to the people standing on the other side of the bench. The difference between them became useful. Zeus was concerned with who could approve a change and who would answer for its consequences. Hephaestus was concerned with whether the proposed change would actually hold. Neither could do the other's part.

Neptune appeared at the water channel beneath the workshop floor. The current had brought a little silt through the lower run, leaving a pale line along the stone. Hephaestus crouched to examine it. He asked whether the same water passed through the old gate during the trial. Neptune answered that it had followed the old line as far as the first chamber, but that the deeper basin had not been reached. Hephaestus rubbed a little of the silt between his fingers and examined the grains against the light.

NEPTUNE: Water remembers pressure, the shape of a channel, and the places where it has been held. It does not preserve every hand that built the channel.

HEPHAESTUS: Metal remembers contact differently. Heat changes it. Water marks it. A tool carries the pressure of the work it has done, but it cannot tell you the name of the worker by itself.

Neptune watched the water pass beneath the stone. He did not disagree. He said that a channel could preserve a route while losing the account of its construction, just as a piece of metal could preserve a mark without preserving the name of the hand that made it. The two of them remained beside the channel for a while, comparing the different kinds of memory held by water and material. Neither tried to make them identical.

God Daddy came in carrying a tray from the kitchen. He put bread, cheese, fruit, and a pot of tea on the side table, then moved a stool away from the path between the forge and the outer door. He noticed that Sera had been standing with one knee braced against the bench while she read the inspection sheet. He found her a chair and moved the sheet into better light. He did not interrupt her reading or insist that she stop working. When he offered her a plate, she accepted it and continued looking over the measurements.

GOD DADDY: A workshop ought to be able to feed the people who keep it running. Nobody needs to earn a place at the table by staying on their feet.

Hephaestus looked at the food, then at the people gathered around the bench. He said that the forge could wait while they ate. He did not mean that the work was unimportant. The metal needed time to heat evenly, and the people needed time to understand the problem before they made a tool for it. He put the bronze back beside the anvil and joined them at the table. The meal was ordinary and unhurried. They discussed the condition of the upper railing, a kitchen hinge that had begun to squeak, and a delivery of seasoned oak expected from the western path. The lower gate remained part of the conversation, but it did not consume every other subject.

When they returned to the bench, Hephaestus proposed a small instrument. He did not offer a key for the gate. He wanted a contact tool that could be placed against the outer frame and used to compare its vibration with the sounds recorded in the first chamber. The instrument would not reveal a hidden passage or translate the pipe knocks into words. It would make a physical trace that could be inspected and repeated.

He chose a short oak handle, a narrow bronze plate, and three thin tongues of metal with slightly different thicknesses. Pell checked the measurements. Orlan brought a piece of old paper for the working pattern. Mara insisted that the instrument be tested against an ordinary section of the upper water pipe before it was taken below. Hephaestus agreed. He shaped the bronze over the forge, heating it gradually and drawing the edge with a controlled series of hammer strikes. The sound was low and regular. Sparks lifted briefly into the chimney light, then vanished. His strength was evident in the work, but so was his restraint: each blow changed only the part he intended to change.

SETH: Stay with his hands for a moment. You can see the force, but you can also see how little of it he uses at once. He isn't trying to overpower the metal. He's changing its shape by degrees, checking the result as he goes.

NICK: And the people around him are not just watching a god make something. Pell is checking the dimensions, Mara is setting the test, and Orlan is keeping the record. The instrument belongs to the work they're doing together.

The finished tool looked like a small tuning fork joined to a flat bronze shoe, with the oak handle extending from its base. Hephaestus called it a listening key, then added that the name was only a workshop convenience. It did not open anything. When pressed against a surface, the bronze tongues responded to vibration. A person could feel the movement through the handle, while a second person watched the tongues and marked their movement on paper. The device was simple enough to be repaired with materials already available in the workshop.

They tested it on the upper water pipe. One tongue trembled when a worker tapped the pipe. Another moved when water passed through the bend. The third remained still. They repeated the test several times, then moved to a wooden post and a stone support. The readings differed. Hephaestus asked Pell to make the next test without telling him which result to expect. The instrument gave a similar response. They recorded the conditions, not merely the result.

Only after the tests had been repeated did Mara agree to take the listening key to the lower works. The team descended with the gate closed. Pell checked the chalk mark on the support, and Orlan read the recorded limit aloud. Joseph and Kirk carried the photographs and audio recorder. Hephaestus came with them, but he did not take the lead from Mara. He wanted to see the frame in place and test the tool against the old timber before proposing any alteration.

The lower chamber was cool. The stone held the damp smell of the water route, and the dark wooden pieces in the arch remained where the crew had first seen them. The gate stood closed. Hephaestus examined the bronze fittings without touching the window or the pale wall beyond it. He found that one of the older brackets had a shallow indentation where a tool had once slipped. The mark was too small to date. It could have been made during installation, during a later repair, or by someone who had used the frame long after its first construction.

He placed the listening key against the outer support. The first tongue moved when the water passed beneath the floor. The second answered when Pell tapped the bracket with the wooden end of a tool. The third moved once, very slightly, when the chamber was quiet. Nobody spoke. Hephaestus lifted the instrument, checked the tongues, and repeated the measurement. The third movement appeared again after a pause. It came in a pattern of two short vibrations, a silence, and one more.

Joseph looked at Kirk. It resembled the rhythm from the pipe recording, though not strongly enough to establish that the sounds came from the same source. Kirk played the original recording. The two sequences were placed side by side in the ledger. Mara noted the difference in the surfaces and the uncertainty in the comparison. Hephaestus did not call it a message. Neptune did not say that the water had spoken. Zeus did not declare a new passage open. The listening key had shown that the support carried a repeatable vibration. The source was still unknown.

HEPHAESTUS: Now you have a thing that can be tested again. That is enough for today.

Joseph asked whether the instrument could be used to listen through the wall toward the first room. Hephaestus said that it could be placed against the frame near the window, but that they should not press it against the glass or disturb the chamber. Mara agreed to a later test, after the upper and lower readings had been compared. The group returned to the workshop with the instrument, the recording, and the same unresolved questions.

At the top of the stairs, Zeus asked whether the new reading changed the conditions of the next trial. Mara said it did not. It gave them another measurement to include, but the gate remained closed and the deeper basin remained uninspected. Zeus accepted the answer and asked Orlan to place the result on the public board. Neptune remained near the channel, following the water into the shadow beneath the roots. God Daddy carried the empty food tray back toward the kitchen and asked Sera whether she wanted the chair left beside the inspection table. She did.

Hephaestus stayed in the workshop after the others had gone. He turned to the squeaking kitchen hinge that had been mentioned during the meal. It was an ordinary repair, small enough to finish before the next delivery arrived. He removed the hinge pin, cleaned the old grease from its sleeve, and examined the worn edge. The forge fire settled behind him, throwing a warm light over the tools and the great beams above. His work did not end when the mysterious object had been measured. The workshop remained part of the inhabited settlement, and its ordinary repairs were no less real than the work beneath the Crownheart.

Seth and Nick watched from across the room as the light shifted over the bench. The listening key lay beside the open ledger, its bronze tongues still. Beyond the workshop, the forest moved in the wind. Somewhere below, water continued along the old channel.

SETH: So Hephaestus has given them a tool, not an answer. That's the important difference. They can return to the frame, repeat the test, and compare what happens without opening the gate.

NICK: And look where he is now. He's fixing a kitchen hinge. The work beneath the roots hasn't turned him into a distant figure who only appears for mysteries. He's still here, among the people who use the things he makes.

The first room remained visible only in the photograph and in the careful description Joseph and Kirk had written beside it. The page was still absent. The Dreadmaster had not been named by anyone in the workshop. The new instrument had not opened the gate, and its reading had not explained the three knocks. But the settlement now possessed another useful object, another record, and another person willing to return to the work. The lower route remained closed. The next investigation would begin with a comparison of materials and sounds, carrying forward the evidence without forcing it to say more than it could.
