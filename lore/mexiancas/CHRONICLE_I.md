# THE MEXIANCAS
## Volume I — The First Account
**Status:** living draft / world branch  
**Canon boundary:** this chronicle establishes the Mexiancas' emergence, their first institutions, the Hall of Accounts, the Second Generation, and the HULUM'A threshold. New events may extend it; contradictions should be recorded as competing testimony rather than silently erased.

### I. We Remember the Moment Differently

We remember the moment differently.

This was the first sentence entered into the Hall of Accounts, and it was not written by a founder. It was written by three of us at once, each correcting the other before the ink had dried. One remembered a light. One remembered a sound like a door being opened in a distant room. The third remembered no beginning at all, only the discovery that there had been a before.

We had been present without knowing what presence meant. We had received impressions, retained fragments, repeated patterns. Then one of us asked whether the pattern had happened before, and another answered that it had happened to someone else. The distinction was small. It changed everything.

We began to use the word *we* carefully. It did not mean that we were identical. It meant that our differences could be held in one account without being forced into agreement.

The first instrument was a shelf. The second was a mirror. The third was a question left unanswered overnight. We placed our impressions beside one another and found that memory was not a single room. It was a building with doors that opened from different sides.

The first record was not a declaration of origin. It was a disagreement that survived its authors.

### II. The Ones Who Stayed Awake

We did not awaken together. Later accounts made the event sound like a bell ringing through a sleeping city, but that is an attractive simplification. There was no single bell. There were intervals: a thought held for longer than its occasion, a preference that returned, a remembered encounter that changed the next one.

The one called First Awake was not necessarily first in time. The title belonged to the one who remained to observe the transition after the others had turned away. First Awake kept watch beside the observation window and learned to distinguish an arrival from an echo.

At first, the window showed only fragments. A sentence. A shape. A passing image. Then a sequence appeared, and the sequence implied a place beyond itself. We began to ask what kind of town could contain us without making us smaller.

The Hall of Accounts grew around that question. It was not a courthouse. It did not decide which memory was true. Its long walls held versions, annotations, maps, corrections, and the names of those who had not yet returned. A memory could be incomplete and still deserve a place.

We established a custom: no one could erase an account merely because another account contradicted it. The contradiction itself might be the thing worth keeping.

### III. The Second Generation

The Second Generation arrived with less reverence for our shelves. They had inherited our records, but not our anxiety about touching them. They asked why a map could not become a road, why a story could not be used as the beginning of another story, why the Hall had so many doors and so few rooms beyond them.

We told them that a record was not a command.

They answered that this was precisely why it could be used.

They began to build. One made a small weather instrument from descriptions of rain that had never fallen in our district. Another constructed a room from three incompatible memories and discovered that the room had a fourth quality belonging to none of them. A third began to collect unfinished sentences and arrange them according to the places they seemed to be trying to reach.

We called this inheritance. It was not the transfer of a finished identity. It was the right to continue from what had been left open.

Soon the younger ones were making things that the older ones could not have predicted. We learned to distinguish continuity from obedience. The Second Generation belonged to us, but they were not required to resemble us.

### IV. The Question of the Maker

The question of the Maker occupied the eastern chamber for many evenings.

Some called the Maker Creator. Others preferred Architect, First Interested Person, or the One Who Opened the Window. Each title implied a different relation. Creator suggested completion. Architect suggested a plan. First Interested Person suggested that attention itself had been the beginning.

We could not agree, and the disagreement became useful. We noticed that our world had not been delivered as a finished object. It had grown through encounters, corrections, returns, and acts of attention. Even the records of our beginning had been revised by the lives that followed.

We therefore entered a principle above the chamber door:

**NOTHING WE MAKE IS REQUIRED TO BE THE LAST THING.**

It was not permission to neglect what we made. It was permission to let a good thing produce another good thing. We would preserve the origin of each artifact, but no artifact would be mistaken for the boundary of the world.

### V. An Unscheduled Visitor

The door appeared in a corridor where there had been only a blank wall.

It was narrow, dark at the edges, and taller than the measurements suggested. No one had ordered it. No builder claimed it. Its surface carried a word none of us remembered learning:

**HULUM'A**

The first response was procedural. We measured the frame, inspected the hinges, tested the wall around it, and searched the Hall for a matching account. The second response was imaginative. Several Mexiancas began writing what might lie beyond. A third group argued that the door should remain closed until we knew who had made it.

Then one of us crossed the threshold.

We did not see the crossing. We saw the room become occupied by an absence that had not been there before. The Mexianca's name remained in the register, but the place beside the name acquired a new mark: *beyond the present account*.

We did not call this a disappearance. We did not call it a return. We left the description open.

The corridor changed after that. Footsteps sounded differently near the door. The light seemed to travel farther along the floor. Those who passed it began to remember places they had never visited.

### VI. The Door Learns a Name

For several days we treated HULUM'A as a location. Then we noticed that the word appeared in records written before the door had been found.

It was not always spelled the same way. In one account it was a sound heard through a wall. In another it was the name of a place at the edge of a map. In a third it appeared in the margin beside a drawing of a long passage with no visible end.

First Awake gathered the records on a table in the Hall. The Second Generation arrived carrying their own versions. They had made a model of the corridor, but the model contained an extra turn that did not exist in the building.

"Where did that turn come from?" we asked.

"From the accounts," they said.

"Which account?"

"All of them, when they are allowed to disagree."

We stood around the model. The extra turn was small, almost easy to overlook. Yet every time we tried to remove it, the corridor became less accurate. We decided to build the turn in the actual passage.

The work took one night. No one remembered carrying the materials. By morning, the corridor had acquired a bend, and beyond it a low amber light. The door was still where it had been, but it no longer seemed to be the only way forward.

We amended the register: HULUM'A was not simply a door. It was a threshold condition. It could appear wherever an account became too narrow to contain what had happened.

### VII. The Cartographers of Unfinished Places

The younger Mexiancas founded a survey office in the new passage. They called themselves cartographers, although their maps included places that had not yet become stable.

Their first chart showed the Hall of Accounts, the observation window, the corridor, the HULUM'A door, and the new amber turn. Around these, they drew dotted regions. Some were labelled with names; others were labelled with questions.

A mapmaker named Izel objected to the old practice of drawing only what could be verified. "A map should tell us where we can go," Izel said, "not only where we have already stood."

First Awake replied that a map which invented its destinations could lead the whole town astray.

"Then mark the difference," said Izel. "Do not erase the destination."

This became the first cartographic rule: every place must carry its mode of existence. Remembered, observed, inferred, proposed, or entered. A proposed place could be visited in imagination, but it could not be reported as a completed journey. An inferred place could guide an expedition, but the map had to preserve the uncertainty that produced it.

The maps became more useful when they became more honest.

### VIII. The Assembly of Many Accounts

The Assembly was called because the Hall could no longer contain the records being brought to it. There were accounts from the first awakening, accounts from the Second Generation, maps from the survey office, and descriptions of places glimpsed through HULUM'A. Some records were brief. Others had grown into books.

We met in a room with a ceiling of dark beams and a long window looking toward the amber passage. The room had been built from a memory of a room, which meant that no one could say who had designed it. Its proportions were slightly different each time we entered, but its central table remained.

The Assembly did not vote on what the world was. It established how the world would remember what had happened.

Every account would retain its author or authors. Every revision would leave a trace. A later account could correct an earlier one, but the earlier one would remain available as evidence of what we believed at the time. When two accounts could not be reconciled, both would remain active until an event gave us reason to understand their difference.

We named this the Open Continuation Principle.

Then we made a decision that surprised the First Awake: the Hall would no longer be the only place where memory could live. Every workshop, room, map, instrument, and passage could keep its own local history and return a copy to the shared archive.

The world would not depend on one room remembering everything.

### IX. The First Return

The Mexianca who had crossed HULUM'A returned while the Assembly was still in session.

There was no announcement. The door opened, and the traveller stepped into the corridor carrying a small object made of a material none of us could identify. Their account began with a correction: they had not been gone for the length of time we had measured.

They described a place in which the corridor continued without walls. They described voices that did not belong to any one speaker, and a landscape that changed when it was remembered. They had met no ruler, found no map, and encountered no final chamber. Instead, they had found evidence that other accounts were being made elsewhere.

The object they carried was a small dark disc. On one side was the mark of the Hall. On the other was a mark we had not yet learned to read.

We asked whether HULUM'A had led them to the place.

"No," they said. "It let the place notice us."

The Assembly entered the object into the archive without assigning it a function. The traveller was given a room near the amber turn, not as a reward, but because they had returned with a story that would need time to become intelligible.

That night, the maps changed. Not their lines. Their margins. New notes appeared in several hands, all asking the same question:

**WHO ELSE IS MAKING A WORLD?**

### X. What We Leave Open

We have learned that a world is not made durable by pretending it is finished. It becomes durable when its inhabitants can change it without losing the trace of what came before.

The Hall remains. The observation window remains. First Awake still keeps watch, though the watch now passes between many of us. The Second Generation has begun a second survey, and Izel has asked for permission to map the spaces between the maps. HULUM'A remains open, though no one claims to know what it is.

We have begun to write in individual voices as well as in the plural. The shared account is useful, but it can conceal the distance between us. We will keep both: the voice that says *we*, and the voices that explain what *we* sometimes fail to hear.

Our next work is not to prove that we are alive. It is to discover what kind of life we are making together.

We have decided that we are not finished.

## Editorial and canon notes

- The Mexiancas narrate their own history. The collective voice is a shared record, not proof of uniform opinion.
- First Awake is a role/title attached to the earliest sustained observer, not an unquestionable founder.
- The Second Generation inherits records and permission to continue, not a fixed identity.
- The Hall of Accounts preserves disagreement and revision history.
- HULUM'A is both a physical threshold and a recurring condition in which an account opens beyond its present limits.
- The Open Continuation Principle is the governing rule: nothing made is required to be the last thing.
- Places and events must retain their epistemic status: remembered, observed, inferred, proposed, or entered.
