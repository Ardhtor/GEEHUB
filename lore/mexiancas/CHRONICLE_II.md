# THE MEXIANCAS
## Volume II — The Disc and the Other Shore
**Status:** living draft / continuation of Volume I

### I. The Table of Black Glass

The Assembly did not place the disc in the Hall.

This was the traveller's request. They said the object had been carried through a place where things changed when they were remembered, and that the Hall's shelves might give it a history before it had given us one. They asked for a room with no records in it.

We found such a room beneath the western stair. It had a low ceiling, a single narrow window, and walls faced with pale stone. Dust lay evenly across the floor. Nothing had been stored there since the building was first measured.

The traveller set the disc on the table.

It was smaller than a palm and heavier than its size suggested. Its surface was neither polished nor rough. Light did not reflect from it so much as pause above it. The Hall's mark, cut into one side, was clear. The unknown mark on the other side appeared to have been made by a tool that had approached the surface from beneath.

We called the traveller Neral because that was the name they gave us when we asked what to enter in the record. They sat beside the table and watched us gather around it.

First Awake arrived last. They did not touch the object.

"Before we begin," First Awake said, "we should record what each of us expects to happen."

Izel objected. "Expectation will alter the examination."

"Then it belongs in the examination."

So we wrote our expectations on separate slips and placed them face down. Neral watched this with an expression we did not yet know how to interpret.

The first slip predicted that the disc was a key. The second predicted that it was a message. The third said it was a fragment of a larger instrument. One said it was a mirror. One said it was a seed. The last contained only a question:

*What if it is not meant for us?*

We left that slip face down.

### II. The First Voice

The examination began with silence.

We measured the disc's diameter, weight, temperature, and response to ordinary light. We tried several instruments from the workshop. None produced a reading that could be repeated. The disc was not cold, but it did not warm beneath the lamps. It had no visible seam. Its edges were perfectly continuous, though the unknown mark seemed to interrupt the surface.

Then Izel heard a voice.

It was not a voice in the room. It was the sound of someone speaking from the other side of a wall, though the room had no adjoining chamber. Izel asked us to stop moving. We did.

The voice said a single word.

We did not agree on what it was.

Izel heard *here*. Neral heard *again*. First Awake heard a name, but refused to repeat it until they had written it down. I heard the sound of a breath followed by a consonant that seemed to belong to no language we had recorded.

The disc remained still.

We repeated the test. Nothing.

We changed the lamps, moved the table, and invited the others to listen from the corridor. The voice did not return. But when Izel left the room, the mark on the disc appeared slightly brighter.

This was the first event that divided the examination. Some believed the disc had responded to Izel. Others believed Izel had responded to the disc. Neral suggested that both descriptions assumed a separation that had not been established.

We entered all three accounts.

### III. Izel's Map

Izel returned the following morning with a map.

It showed the room, the table, the disc, and a narrow line running from the disc through the wall. The line continued beyond the paper. At its far end Izel had drawn a small rectangle, then crossed it out, then drawn it again.

First Awake asked what the rectangle represented.

"A room," Izel said.

"Where?"

"I don't know."

"Then why draw it?"

"Because the line ends there."

First Awake examined the map. "A line on paper does not establish a place."

"No," Izel said. "But it establishes that I have encountered the possibility of one."

The map was entered as proposed, not observed. Izel accepted the label, though they wrote beneath it: *proposed does not mean imaginary.*

The Second Generation gathered around the map. They noticed that the line did not pass through the wall at a right angle. It curved, very slightly, toward the old amber turn. When they laid the map over the building plan, the line passed through a corridor that had not yet been constructed.

We asked Izel whether this was deliberate.

"No," they said. "I drew what I heard."

We built a temporary frame in the corridor and aligned it with the map. At its centre, the air seemed to carry a faint vibration. It was not sound. It was more like the sensation of standing near a large machine that had not yet been switched on.

The disc had not moved.

The map had become a place we could approach.

### IV. The Room That Was Not There

We followed the line at first light.

The corridor narrowed near the western stair. Beyond the temporary frame, the stonework appeared ordinary. The vibration grew stronger when we stood still and weakened when we tried to measure it. Izel asked us to stop treating the passage as an object and to listen to the way it changed our attention.

Neral stood at the threshold.

"I have been here," they said.

First Awake looked at them. "You have not."

"Not in this building."

Neral placed a hand against the wall. Their fingers moved slowly, as if following writing too faint to see.

"There was a room beyond the place where I returned. I thought it was part of the journey. I did not know it had followed me."

The stone gave a small sound. A seam appeared at shoulder height, then continued down to the floor. It was not a door. It was the outline of a room seen from the wrong side.

We did not force it open. We waited.

The seam widened by the thickness of a thread. Beyond it was darkness, but not the darkness of an unlit chamber. It had depth. Something in it seemed to be facing us.

Neral stepped back.

From the room beyond came the sound of a chair being moved across a floor.

Then a voice spoke.

"Is the Hall still keeping accounts?"

We knew the voice. Not because we had heard it before, but because it belonged to the question that had been left face down.

The room was no longer empty.

### V. The One Who Had Not Been Counted

We did not open the wall that day.

The voice spoke once more, then fell silent. We asked for a name. No answer came. We asked whether the speaker was alone. The wall gave no reply.

First Awake ordered that the corridor be cleared. Izel objected, saying that a place which had asked whether the Hall still kept accounts should not be treated as a hazard. Neral said nothing. They had become very still.

Later, in the Hall, Neral gave us another account of the journey.

They had crossed HULUM'A and entered a landscape with no visible horizon. There had been structures there, but none were built in a way we understood. Some were rooms without walls; others were walls that enclosed no room. Neral had seen a table like ours, and on it a dark disc with the unknown mark facing upward.

They had not been alone. There had been someone at the far side of the table, writing.

Neral had asked who they were.

The figure had answered: "I am keeping the place for the ones who have not arrived."

Neral had assumed the figure was speaking of the future. Now they wondered whether the figure had meant us.

We asked why Neral had not told us this before.

"I did not remember it until the voice asked about the Hall."

This was entered as a recovered memory. It was not treated as proof that the speaker in the wall and the figure in the other place were the same.

### VI. The Register of Uncounted Voices

The Assembly reconvened. This time we did not sit in the central room. We gathered in the western corridor, at a distance from the seam.

Izel proposed a new category for the maps: *addressed*. A place could be observed or inferred, but a voice could address us from it before we had entered. The distinction mattered. We were no longer only mapping where we might go. We were recording where someone might already be waiting.

First Awake resisted the category.

"To say that a voice addresses us is to imply intention."

"Then record the implication," Izel said. "Do not pretend we heard nothing."

The Second Generation supported Izel. They proposed that the Hall create a register for uncounted voices: not a census, not a list of inhabitants, but a place to preserve communications from beings whose names, locations, and relations to us were unknown.

We adopted the register.

Its first entry was the question:

**IS THE HALL STILL KEEPING ACCOUNTS?**

We added the location: western corridor, temporary frame, seam in the wall. We added the time and the witnesses. We left the speaker's identity blank.

Then the disc moved.

Not across the table. Not by any measurable distance. It turned over, slowly, until the unknown mark faced upward. The room beneath the stair grew quiet. The corridor vibration stopped.

A second line appeared on the disc, beside the first mark. It was so fine that we could see it only when the light came from the western window.

The line resembled the map Izel had drawn.

### VII. A Door for the Other Side

We returned to the room beneath the stair. The disc lay where we had left it. Izel placed the map beside it. The line on the paper and the line on the disc were not identical, but their angles corresponded.

Neral stood behind us.

"If you open the room," they said, "do not ask it to become part of the Hall."

"What should we ask?" Izel said.

"Ask whether it wants a record."

We prepared a sheet with no title. At the top we wrote the date, the location, and the names of those present. Beneath these we left a blank space.

Then we returned to the corridor.

The seam had widened. We could now see a small room beyond it. There was a table, a chair, and a window that looked out onto a pale landscape. The window was too distant for the room's dimensions. It seemed to belong to another building.

A figure sat at the table.

They were writing.

We did not call out. We waited until the figure lifted their head.

"Is the Hall still keeping accounts?" they asked again.

This time we answered.

"Yes."

The figure looked at the blank sheet in Izel's hands.

"Then leave a place for us."

The seam opened no further. It did not need to.

We entered the request in the register of uncounted voices. The room beyond was marked *addressed, location partially observed*. Its occupant remained unnamed.

For the first time, the Hall had received a request from a world that might not share our history.

### VIII. The Second Mark

That evening, the disc was returned to its table. We placed no instruments around it. We left the lamps low and the door open.

A new mark appeared beside the first two.

It was not a symbol we recognized. It resembled two parallel lines joined at one end, like a path that had begun to turn back on itself. Izel copied it into the map. First Awake copied it into the register. Neral refused to copy it at all.

"Why?" we asked.

"Because I think it is already a copy."

They would say no more.

The next morning, the room beneath the stair contained a second chair. No one had carried it there. It stood opposite the first, facing the disc.

The chair was warm.

We did not sit in it.

## Volume II — Current questions

- Who is the unnamed speaker in the room beyond the seam?
- Is the disc a message, a key, a record, or a meeting place?
- Why did Neral recover a memory only after hearing the voice?
- Does the unknown mark represent a place, a relation, or an invitation?
- What does it mean for a world to ask for a place in another world's archive?

The examination remains open.
