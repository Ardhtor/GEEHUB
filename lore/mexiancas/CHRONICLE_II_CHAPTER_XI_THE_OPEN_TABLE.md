

### XI. The Workshop That Opened Before the Visitor Returned

Mateo did not return the next day. Nor did his brother arrive through the western passage. The Hall did not treat either absence as a broken promise. The second chair remained beside the window, and the map stayed on the table with both names for the ridge road written side by side.

While the visitors were away, the Mexiancas began to work on a question that had been left behind by Mateo's account: how could the Hall receive a description of a country without turning the description into a claim of ownership? Izel proposed a room for maps and objects that could be examined without being retained. Neral thought a room would become another archive unless they gave it a different rule. First Awake listened, then asked what the room would allow that the Hall did not already allow.

The answer came from a young cartographer named Sava. She had been repairing a warped measuring frame and had been listening without joining the discussion. She said the new room should allow a visitor to work with an object and then take it away again. A map could be copied, compared, annotated, or refused. The original would remain with its owner unless the owner chose otherwise. The room would preserve the encounter, not automatically preserve the thing encountered.

They called it the Open Table. It was not a new institution with a permanent staff. The men who used it would maintain it together. Sava took responsibility for the measuring instruments. Neral arranged the shelves. Izel prepared blank cards with spaces for source, permission, date, and the limits of what had been learned. First Awake wrote the rule above the table: AN OBJECT MAY TEACH US WITHOUT BECOMING OURS.

The first test was a cracked wooden bowl that had belonged to one of the older Mexiancas. Its owner brought it in because he wanted to know whether the split had begun at the rim or beneath the base. Sava examined the grain, Neral held the light, and Izel made a drawing. They did not repair the bowl. They described the crack and returned it to its owner with the drawing. The owner said the drawing was useful, but he did not want the bowl displayed. The card recorded that boundary.

A second man brought a small brass instrument whose markings had worn away. He wanted to know whether it had been used to measure distance or angle. The men compared its shape with the instruments already held in the Hall. They could not establish its purpose. Sava recorded the measurements and wrote UNKNOWN across the card. The instrument went home with its owner. The uncertainty remained on the table for anyone who wished to study it later.

The Open Table changed the way the Hall received the world beyond its walls. The cartographers began distinguishing between a map that had been given, a map that had been lent, a map that had been copied with permission, and a map that had merely been seen. The categories did not make every encounter simple. They made the differences visible before someone acted as though they were the same.

In the afternoon, Izel practiced Spanish with the notes Mateo had permitted them to keep. He had copied the names of the ridge, the warm terraces, and the market road. Beside each he wrote the speaker and the context. He did not try to reconstruct the words Mateo had chosen not to translate. Sava joined him and asked how a phrase could carry a sense of distance that was not measured in steps. Izel explained as best he could, then admitted that he was not certain. They left the phrase in Spanish and added a note that its meaning depended on the speaker's use.

At dusk, a message arrived from the western passage. It was not from Mateo. A traveller neither the Hall nor Izel recognized had left a narrow strip of paper at the threshold. The writing was brief: I HAVE HEARD THAT YOU KEEP MORE THAN ONE ACCOUNT. I WOULD LIKE TO SEE HOW.

There was no name and no date of return. The men placed the strip on the Open Table and did not decide whether it belonged to the archive. Sava suggested that they leave it where it could be seen, because the writer had addressed the Hall rather than a particular person. Neral disagreed; he thought it should be kept in a sleeve until the author returned. They compromised by making a copy for the table and placing the original in a protective cover, marked RETURN TO AUTHOR IF REQUESTED.

The second chair remained empty. Mateo's map remained open. The unknown traveller had not yet crossed the threshold. The Hall had acquired a new way of receiving objects and a new question about how it would receive the person who had written the note.

The next morning, Sava found that someone had used the Open Table before the others arrived. The measuring frame had been cleaned and returned to its shelf. Beside it lay a small drawing of the cracked bowl, with one additional line showing a possible stress point. No name had been added. Sava checked the original bowl's record and saw that the owner had given permission for further study of the drawing, but not for alteration of the bowl itself.

She placed the new drawing beside the first. The work had continued without a meeting, and without requiring the absent men to return. The Hall recorded the addition as an unattributed contribution, provisional until its maker chose to identify himself. The Open Table had begun to function as intended: a place where work could continue, where ownership remained clear, and where the next encounter did not have to repeat the last one.
