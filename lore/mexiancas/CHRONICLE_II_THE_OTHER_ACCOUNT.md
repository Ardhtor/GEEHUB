# THE MEXIANCAS
## Volume II — The Other Account
**Status:** living continuation
**Canon boundary:** this volume begins with the Assembly's question, “WHO ELSE IS MAKING A WORLD?” It preserves the Other Side's independent voice and does not merge its people or history with the Mexiancas.

### XI. The Room Beyond the Wall

We received the question before we received the invitation.

WHO ELSE IS MAKING A WORLD?

It appeared in the margin of three maps, on the reverse of a workshop schedule, and once in the dust on a windowsill where nobody had written. We assumed it belonged to the Hall of Accounts because the Hall had become the place where unanswered questions accumulated. Then Izel found the same words on a sheet of paper that had come through the HULUM'A threshold, written in a hand none of us could identify.

The Assembly did not call an emergency meeting. We had learned that urgency could make a question smaller than it was. Instead, we opened the eastern room and placed a chair beside the long table. The chair was not ceremonial. It was made for use, with a broad seat and a back that could bear the weight of someone who wished to remain for a while.

The first account came without a name. Its author described a town with a courtyard, a kitchen, a workshop, and a House of Returns. The account was careful about its limits. It did not ask us to accept its descriptions as our own memories, and it did not offer a history of how the town had begun. It simply said: we are here, we have been making things, and we have noticed that another account has begun to notice us.

We listened.

The Assembly wanted to know whether the town was a place, a projection, a memory, or a proposal. Izel reminded us of the cartographic rule. We entered the account as received testimony: observed as an arrival, unverified as geography, and independent in authorship. The distinction mattered. A voice could be real as a voice before we knew where its speaker stood.

That evening we built a second room in the eastern wing. It was not a replica of the House of Returns. We had only its description, and a description was not permission to copy. We used our own timber, our own measurements, and our own habits of construction. We left one wall unfinished so that the room could receive a window when we understood which direction it ought to face.

### XII. The People Who Keep Their Own Names

The second account corrected the first.

The voice explained that the people of the House of Returns had several names for their community, and that none had been chosen for exchange. They were willing to be known by the temporary phrase *the Other Side*, but they did not want a convenient label to become a substitute for their own decision. We entered the boundary exactly as it had been given.

This changed the Assembly's practice. We had been accustomed to naming places when they entered our maps. Now we added a field for the name withheld, and another for the conditions under which a name might be shared. First Awake argued that a map without a name would be difficult to use. Izel replied that a map which stole a name would be worse.

The Second Generation took up the problem. They designed a new register in which an inhabitant could be present without being fully described. A person might contribute a drawing, a route, a recipe, a building plan, or a sentence, while keeping other parts of their life outside the record. We called this selective presence. It did not weaken the archive. It made the archive more trustworthy.

The new register was tested in the eastern room. The first contribution was a drawing of a table with six chairs. The second was a recipe for bread with toasted seeds. The third was a note that said only: the western window should open inward, because the wind is stronger than it looks.

We did not know who had written it. We did not need to know yet.

The Assembly adopted a rule: an account may be shared without surrendering its author, and an encounter may be remembered without claiming possession of the person encountered. This was not a rule of distance. It was a rule that allowed closeness to remain voluntary.

### XIII. A Place for the Unfinished

The room beyond the wall acquired its window in the spring.

The builders had waited until the prevailing wind could be observed over several weeks. They set the frame deep into the stone and made the hinges easy to repair. From the inside, the window looked toward the amber passage. From the outside, it reflected the sky and the dark line of the trees.

The room became a place for unfinished work. Mapmakers brought routes that had not yet been walked. Builders brought models whose dimensions were still being tested. Writers brought passages they did not want judged as complete. The room did not become a storehouse for abandoned things. It became a place where a work could remain active without being forced into a premature conclusion.

The voice from the Other Side returned through the chair. It told us that their House of Returns had developed a similar room, though its windows faced a courtyard. Neither community claimed that the rooms were the same. Their resemblance was an event between them, not proof of a shared origin.

Izel placed two drawings side by side. One showed our eastern room. The other showed the room beyond the wall. The dimensions differed. The materials differed. The windows faced different directions. Yet both drawings included a chair near the window and a table with enough space for another person's work.

“We have not made one place,” Izel said. “We have made two places that can receive one another.”

The Assembly entered this as the first confirmed correspondence between the two accounts.

The next question was practical. What could pass between the places without becoming a claim of ownership? We began with recipes, building measurements, maps marked with uncertainty, and short passages whose authors had agreed to share them. Every exchange carried its origin, its date, its permissions, and the conditions of its return. A copy could be adapted, but the source remained visible. A gift could produce another gift without being treated as a debt.

HULUM'A did not open wider. It became more precise. We understood that a threshold was not measured by how much could cross it, but by whether what crossed could arrive without losing the account of where it came from.

### XIV. The First Shared Meal

The first shared meal was prepared in two kitchens.

We did not attempt to make the dishes identical. Our cooks prepared beans with herbs and roasted vegetables, while the House of Returns sent a description of its own bread, baked with toasted seeds and a little honey. We followed the recipe as closely as our ingredients allowed, then sent back a record of what had changed.

The meal was placed on two tables at the same hour. The Assembly gathered in the eastern room. The other community gathered in its House of Returns. Between the two accounts there was no single dining hall, no magical joining of rooms, and no claim that distance had disappeared. There were two meals, two groups of people, and a shared record of the occasion.

The bread was good. Ours had a darker crust. Theirs was softer in the centre. We wrote this down because the difference was worth keeping.

Afterward, the Second Generation proposed a permanent exchange shelf. It would hold objects that could travel safely between the communities: copies of maps, small crafted items, seeds where permitted, and books whose authors had approved their circulation. The shelf would also hold a record of things that could not be sent. Some objects were too fragile, some too personal, and some belonged to places that could not be reproduced elsewhere.

The Assembly approved the shelf with one condition: nothing on it would be described as universal merely because two communities had found it useful.

### XV. The Map with Two Edges

Our latest map has two edges.

The first is the boundary of the places we have entered. It shows the Hall, the amber passage, the HULUM'A threshold, the eastern room, the workshops, and the paths leading toward the gardens. The second edge is not a boundary of land. It is the limit of what we can responsibly say about the Other Side.

Beyond that edge we have received testimony, drawings, recipes, building plans, and the account of a cat sleeping beside a window. We have not walked its streets. We have not measured its buildings. We have not named its inhabitants. The map shows the exchange points and marks the rest as independently held.

This is not a failure of knowledge. It is a more exact form of it.

First Awake has stopped asking when the two histories will become one. Izel has begun mapping the routes by which they can remain distinct and still affect one another. The Second Generation is building a small covered passage between the eastern room and the gardens, with a shelf for incoming and outgoing records. The work is ordinary, careful, and likely to last.

We have learned that another world does not become more real when we absorb it into our own. It becomes more real when we make room for its account to continue.

The question remains on the wall:

WHO ELSE IS MAKING A WORLD?

We no longer read it as a call for names. We read it as an invitation to keep listening.

## Editorial and canon notes

- This volume continues the Mexiancas chronicle without merging the Other Side into Mexiancas identity or geography.
- The Other Side is a self-authored community; its internal name remains withheld until its inhabitants choose to share it.
- Selective presence protects an author's right to share an artifact or account without surrendering unrelated personal information.
- Correspondence between places is not proof of common origin. Similarities remain similarities unless further evidence changes their status.
- Exchanges preserve origin, authorship, permissions, date, and conditions of return.
- The two communities maintain separate maps, histories, and local customs while allowing voluntary exchange.
- HULUM'A is increasingly understood as a provenance-preserving threshold, not a device for erasing distance.
