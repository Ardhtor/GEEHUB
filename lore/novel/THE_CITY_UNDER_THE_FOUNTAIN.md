# THE CITY UNDER THE FOUNTAIN

The first thing Joseph noticed was that the rain was falling upward.

He stopped beneath the archway and watched it climb through the amber light, each drop rising from the pavement as though the street had become the bottom of an invisible sea. Behind him the city was still awake. Windows burned in the upper floors. A tram moved somewhere beyond the buildings with the slow metallic complaint of something too old to be trusted. From the square came the sound of men talking, laughing, arguing over a game neither of them had finished. Nothing about the ordinary world had changed except the direction of the water.

Luke stood several steps ahead of him, looking toward the fountain. "That's new," he said.

Joseph did not answer immediately. The fountain had always stood in the center of the square, black stone worn smooth by centuries of hands and weather. During the day it was almost disappointingly ordinary. At night it became difficult to look at directly. The basin seemed deeper than its construction allowed, and the water reflected places that were not present in the square. Joseph had once seen a forest in it. Another time he had seen the interior of a room with a red door. Tonight there was no reflection at all. There was only a hole.

Luke walked toward it. Joseph followed.

They had learned, by then, that the city changed most quickly when somebody approached something without pretending to understand it. The old men called this courage. The younger ones called it stupidity. Chase, when asked, had called it attention. He had said that the world did not reveal itself to people who demanded answers from it. It revealed itself to people who stayed long enough to notice when an answer had begun behaving strangely.

The two men reached the fountain. The upward rain continued around them.

At the bottom of the empty basin was a staircase.

It descended farther than the fountain was deep.

Luke crouched at the edge. "Was this here?"

"No."

"You sure?"

Joseph looked down. The staircase was made from the same black stone as the fountain, but the steps were wet with a thin silver film that moved against gravity. The deeper steps disappeared into darkness. Somewhere below, water was running. Not falling. Running sideways, perhaps, or along a wall. The sound was difficult to place.

"No," Joseph said. "It wasn't."

Luke smiled.

Joseph knew that smile. It was the expression Luke wore when the world had presented him with a problem that looked sufficiently impossible to become interesting.

They descended.

At first there were twelve steps. Then there were twenty. Then there were too many to count.

The city disappeared above them, but the sound of it did not. Voices came down through the stone. A door slammed somewhere far away. Someone laughed. A dog barked. A car accelerated through the square. These sounds became thinner as they descended until they seemed to belong to a city remembered rather than a city inhabited.

Then Joseph heard another sound.

A heartbeat.

He stopped.

Luke continued for two steps before realizing he was alone.

"What?"

"Listen."

Luke turned.

They stood in the stairwell.

There it was again.

Thum.

A pause.

Thum.

It was coming from beneath them.

Joseph put his hand against the wall. The stone was warm.

"That's not machinery."

Luke looked at him.

"How do you know?"

"Because machinery doesn't sound like it's waiting."

For a while neither man moved.

Then the staircase changed.

Not visibly. The steps remained where they had been. The walls remained where they had been. But Joseph suddenly understood that they were no longer descending into the foundation of the city.

They were entering another place.

The realization was so complete that he did not need to explain it to Luke. They both knew.

At the bottom was a door.

It was small enough that Luke had to lower his head to pass through it. The door had no handle. Instead there was a shallow depression in the center, shaped like a human palm.

Luke looked at Joseph.

Joseph put his hand against it.

Nothing happened.

Then Luke placed his hand over Joseph's.

The door opened.

Beyond it was a city.

It was impossible to see the whole thing because it extended beyond the horizon in every direction, but the first impression was of height. Towers rose through mist. Bridges crossed empty distances between buildings. Long avenues disappeared into amber darkness. There were gardens suspended above streets and streets running through buildings and buildings whose upper floors seemed to have become forests. Somewhere far away, enormous lights moved slowly through the fog.

Joseph stepped through.

The air smelled like wet stone and cedar.

Luke followed.

Behind them the door disappeared.

Neither man reacted.

They had already learned the second rule of the place: a door was not required to remain a door merely because you had entered through it.

A bell rang somewhere in the distance.

Then another.

Then twelve bells answered.

Joseph looked toward the sound.

On the highest tower stood a figure.

It was too far away to identify, but Joseph could see that the figure was watching them.

Luke saw it too.

"Friend?"

"I don't know."

"Enemy?"

"I don't know."

Luke considered this.

"Good."

They began walking.

The city seemed to notice.

Lights appeared in windows as they passed. Bridges illuminated beneath their feet. A long mechanical structure unfolded from the side of a building and became a staircase leading downward. Somewhere nearby, water began moving through pipes.

The city was not waking.

It was recognizing them.

Joseph felt the thought before he could form it.

This place had been waiting.

Not specifically for them.

For someone.

For witnesses.

The realization frightened him more than any monster could have.

They crossed the first bridge.

Halfway across, Luke stopped.

Joseph turned.

Luke was looking down.

Far below, beneath the bridge, stood another Luke.

Same face. Same posture. Same scar near the eyebrow. Same hands.

But the other Luke was standing in a room that could not have existed beneath the bridge. The room was brightly lit, and behind him was the red door Joseph remembered from the fountain.

The other Luke looked upward.

Their eyes met.

For one impossible second neither moved.

Then the other Luke raised his hand.

Not waving.

Pointing.

Behind Joseph.

Joseph turned.

At the far end of the bridge stood a man in a dark coat.

Chase.

He looked older than Joseph remembered. Not merely older in the ordinary sense. He looked as though he had spent years somewhere that did not measure time properly. His hair was silver at the temples. His expression was calm.

He held a book beneath one arm.

"You came through the fountain," Chase said.

Luke stared at him.

"You knew about this?"

"I knew there was a city beneath it."

"Why didn't you tell us?"

Chase looked past them toward the impossible duplicate still standing beneath the bridge.

"Because you wouldn't have believed me."

Joseph felt the city move beneath his feet.

"What is this place?"

Chase opened the book.

For a moment the pages were blank.

Then writing appeared.

Not handwriting. Not typing. The words seemed to be forming from the paper itself.

Chase read the first line.

"This is where memories go when they become large enough to require geography."

Luke looked at Joseph.

Joseph looked at the city.

Suddenly the bridges made sense. The impossible rooms. The repeated faces. The doors. The distant towers. The places reflected in the fountain.

This was not a city built beneath the old city.

It was a city built beneath memory.

Every forgotten route had become a street. Every repeated encounter had become a room. Every unresolved departure had become a road that continued beyond the visible horizon. Every person who had been remembered strongly enough had left some portion of themselves here.

And somewhere beneath all of it was the heartbeat.

The second heartbeat.

Chase closed the book.

"There's something else."

Joseph waited.

Chase looked toward the center of the city.

"Someone has been building."

The bells stopped.

Every light in the city went dark.

For several seconds there was nothing.

Then, far beyond the towers, a single window illuminated.

Another window answered.

Then another.

A path of light began forming through the city.

It crossed bridges. It descended staircases. It passed beneath towers and through forests and across empty plazas. It came toward them.

Luke took a step forward.

Joseph caught his arm.

"Wait."

The path continued.

At its end stood a door.

This one was enormous.

It opened inward.

Warm light spilled onto the street.

And from inside came the sound of voices.

Not one voice.

Hundreds.

Thousands.

Every voice Joseph had ever heard in the Hub seemed to be speaking at once, not loudly but close, as though the entire archive had suddenly become a room and every memory inside it had turned its attention toward the same doorway.

Chase stepped aside.

"Now," he said, "you can see what the fountain was actually for."

Joseph looked into the light.

Luke stood beside him.

Neither man knew what waited beyond the door.

That was the reason they entered.

The city closed behind them.

And beneath the oldest fountain in the world, the next part of the story began.
