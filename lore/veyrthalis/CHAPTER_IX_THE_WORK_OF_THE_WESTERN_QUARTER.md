# Veyrthalis — Chapter IX: The Work of the Western Quarter

The western quarter woke before the bells. Rain had passed through the night and left the slate roofs dark, the gutters full, and the narrow lanes shining under the first grey light. The men who opened the market did not wait for the city to announce itself. They lifted shutters, tested hinges, carried baskets from covered stores, and swept the water away from the thresholds where it had collected.

At the lower end of the quarter, Tomas kept the key to the public drying room. He arrived with a ring of iron keys and a ledger wrapped in oilcloth. The room had been built for wet coats, boots, maps, and tools brought in from the Expanse. It was not an archive, though several men had begun leaving written notes there because the room was warm and the paper stayed dry. Tomas had asked them to stop until he could build a proper cabinet. Nobody had stopped. He had therefore begun building the cabinet.

His apprentice, Ren, arrived carrying two boards cut to different lengths. Tomas measured them, found that neither would fit the alcove, and asked Ren where he had taken the measurements. The figures were correct. The wall was not square. They spent the next hour making a paper template rather than cutting another board. The cabinet would be delayed, but the wood would not be wasted.

Across the lane, the brothers who maintained the lanterns were arguing about the eastern stair. One wanted to replace the whole bracket. The other believed the stone socket had shifted and that a new bracket would only conceal the fault. They fetched a mason from the upper street. He examined the crack, tapped the stone, and told them to leave the lantern unlit until the socket could be secured. The stair remained dim that evening. A notice was placed at both ends, and a temporary lamp was hung lower down. Nobody called the delay a failure. The repair had become a larger job because somebody had finally looked closely.

The quarter had its own rhythms. The bread ovens were lit in sequence. The map seller opened after the first delivery carts. A cooper worked with his door open so that the sound of the mallet could travel into the lane. The men who carried water from the lower cistern took turns, not because the city required a formal rota but because the route was steep and the barrels were heavy. A few apprentices had begun helping with the smaller vessels. They were shown how to keep the water level and how to stop before the shoulder gave way.

Luke came down from the House after the second bell. He carried the copied route from the chamber in the woods, folded inside a plain cover. He had not brought the original map. The covered name remained where it was, and the stranger's request that it remain covered had been entered in the archive. Luke had begun to understand that a record could be preserved without being displayed.

He found Martin in the public room, where three men were trying to determine whether the western road had been closed by the last storm. One had seen a fallen tree. Another had heard that the bridge was unsafe. The third had walked as far as the first marker and returned because the water was rising. Their accounts did not agree, but none was dismissed. Martin asked them to mark the exact point each had reached. The map gained three observations, each with a date and a source. The road was marked uncertain, not closed.

Luke placed his copied route beside the regional map. It did not connect cleanly to any known track. The cartographer on duty, a quiet man named Sorel, studied it for a long time.

"It may join the old charcoal road," Sorel said.

"May," Luke replied.

"I know. I am not drawing the connection yet."

Sorel took a second sheet and began a separate overlay. He marked the ridge, the visible bend, and the point at which the original line disappeared. Then he left the rest blank. He had been trained to complete maps, but the work of the quarter had taught him that a blank space could be more accurate than a confident invention.

At midday, Tomas brought the first cabinet frame into the drying room. It did not fit. The alcove narrowed near the floor, and the frame caught against the stone. Ren suggested removing a strip from the rear upright. Tomas examined the joint and refused. The strip would weaken the frame. They took the cabinet apart and made a narrower base. The work took the rest of the afternoon. When it stood upright, it was not beautiful, but it was sound. The notes left in the room were gathered into separate bundles: road observations, repair requests, weather records, and personal messages awaiting collection. The room had acquired a place for its own memory.

In the late afternoon, one of the lantern brothers returned with the mason. The stone socket had been secured, but the bracket still needed a new pin. They worked from a small platform while the other brother held the lamp. The stair remained closed to traffic until the pin was tested. A man carrying a sack complained that the detour added ten minutes to his walk. The lantern keeper agreed that it did. He did not open the stair early.

The House received no message from the stranger that day. Luke did not search the western wood. He spent the evening helping Sorel make a clean copy of the route overlay. Martin visited the drying room and found Tomas writing labels for the cabinet. Ren had gone home, but he had left a note explaining the revised measurements. Tomas added the note to the construction record rather than throwing it away.

When the lamps were lit, the western quarter was not transformed. Its work had simply accumulated. A road remained uncertain. A stair reopened after inspection. A cabinet stood where damp papers had once been scattered. The copied route waited beside a map that did not yet know where to place it.

Beyond the quarter, the forest darkened. Somewhere beyond the ridge, the unnamed man might have been walking, resting, or staying with someone whose name had not entered the House's records. No one knew. The uncertainty was left intact.

The next morning, a folded note appeared in the drying room cabinet. It contained no signature. It asked whether the mapmakers could make a second copy of the route, one that could be carried into the forest without risking the archive copy. Sorel read it, entered it as an anonymous request, and began preparing the paper.

The city had not solved the mystery. It had learned how to continue its work while the mystery remained.
