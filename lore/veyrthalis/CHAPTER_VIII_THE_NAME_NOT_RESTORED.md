# Chapter VIII — The Name Not Restored

The man in the dark coat did not repeat what he had said. He allowed the words to remain between them, where they could be heard without being turned into an accusation. The corridor was narrow enough that Luke could see the rain-dark grain of the coat and the faint dust along its shoulder. Behind the stranger, the carved panels continued toward the bend, each one holding a version of the three figures and the carefully polished absence between them. The lantern light travelled over the empty shape as if the wood had been touched more often there than anywhere else.

Luke held the photograph in one hand and the folded map beneath his shirt. The older Luke remained near his shoulder. Martin had moved half a step aside, no longer blocking the passage but not abandoning the space between the stranger and the others. Nobody had asked the man to prove that he belonged in the corridor. Nobody had asked him to leave.

"Why did you call yourself the guest?" Luke asked.

"I didn't."

"The House did."

"Then the House has chosen a word that makes it easier to receive me."

"Is it wrong?"

"It makes me temporary. It gives everyone a reason to decide that I am only passing through."

The older Luke looked at him. "And are you?"

The man glanced toward the carved panel. "I have been passing through for a long time. That does not mean I have never lived anywhere."

Martin studied the photograph. "You were there. These two men were there. Someone made a record, and someone else covered the name."

"Yes."

"Why?"

The stranger's gaze remained on the photograph. "There are several accounts."

"Which one is yours?" Luke asked.

A small change passed over the man's face. It was not relief. It was the expression of someone who had been offered a choice after years of having choices made on his behalf.

"My account is that I was present, and that the record made my presence useful to other people. They could point to me when they needed proof of a promise. They could remove me when the promise became inconvenient. They could describe my departure as betrayal, my silence as agreement, and my return as an attempt to reclaim something. Each description made the families' own decisions appear inevitable."

"Did you leave?" Martin asked.

"At one point."

"Were you forced out?"

"At another point."

"Did you come back?"

The man looked at Luke. "I am here."

The answer did not settle the history. It made the history larger. Luke found himself looking again at the covered name on the map, where the paper strip had been pasted carefully along all four edges. The older Luke had told him not to peel it because the name was attached to the paper beneath. He now understood that the warning had been literal, but not only literal. The covering had become part of the record. To remove it without consent would destroy the evidence of why it had been placed there.

"Can we ask you anything?" Luke said.

"You can ask. I can decline."

The simplicity of the rule unsettled him. He had expected a bargain, a challenge, or a clue. Instead the man offered a boundary that would remain in force even if the conversation became difficult.

"Then I'll ask this," Luke said. "What do you want us to understand about the map?"

The stranger stepped closer to the workbench, stopping before the edge. He did not reach for the paper. "It records routes that were travelled, routes that were forbidden, and routes that people remembered differently. The families treated it as a map of land. It was also a map of permission. One road was open to one man and closed to another, even when both could see the same trees."

The older Luke nodded slowly. "That explains the red line."

"It explains one line. Not the other."

"The unapproved route?"

"That line was drawn by someone who was trying to find a way home without passing through either family's gate."

Luke remembered the faint line that only appeared when the lantern was held low. It ran outward from the chamber into the forest, avoiding every marked path. He had thought it was a survey error, or a private shortcut. Now he saw that it might have been the most important line on the page, though its destination remained outside the map.

"Did you draw it?" he asked.

The man did not answer at once. "I helped preserve it."

"That's not the same thing."

"No. It isn't."

Martin folded his arms. "You keep telling us what things aren't. Are you going to tell us what they are?"

The stranger looked at him, and for the first time something like amusement appeared. "You sound like the family that opened the baseline."

Martin did not smile. "I don't know whether that's an insult."

"It isn't."

The four men moved out of the corridor and into the wider chamber, where the stone floor gave way to oak boards. A narrow window admitted a view of the redwood trunks beyond the building. Their bark was dark from rain, and the upper branches disappeared into low cloud. The chamber contained a long bench, a small stove, a basin, and a cupboard whose hinges had been repaired with newer brass. It was clearly used, though no one had prepared it for a guest.

Luke set the photograph on the bench. The stranger looked at it but did not pick it up.

"Do you want us to restore your name?" Joseph's voice seemed to come from the memory of another conversation, although Joseph was not there. Luke wondered how much of the House's interior he was carrying into this room.

The stranger answered the absent question all the same. "No."

"Why not?"

"Because restoration assumes that the proper ending is to put everything back where it was. I do not know whether the old place was ever mine in the way the map claims. I do not know whether the name was mine before it was covered. I know that people used it, and that they used its absence. I will not ask you to make a clean line through a history that wasn't clean."

Luke looked down at the photograph. "Then what should we call you?"

"Ask me when you need to address me. I may answer with a name. I may answer with nothing. You don't have to turn the absence into a title."

The older Luke leaned against the bench. "That will make the record awkward."

"Good," the man said. "It should be awkward where the history is awkward."

Martin examined the room. "Where have you been staying?"

"Beyond the western wood."

"Alone?"

"Not always."

"Do you need anything?"

The stranger looked at him with a degree of surprise. "Dry clothes. A meal. A place where I can sleep without being asked to account for the years."

Martin nodded. "Those are ordinary things."

"They are difficult to obtain when everybody believes you are a historical question."

The room changed then, but not by opening another passage. The men understood that the next action was not to investigate him. They could provide a meal, dry clothing, and a room whose door he could close. Luke went to find towels. Martin checked the stove. The older Luke remained near the map, not guarding it, but keeping it in view so that nobody would move it without permission.

Later, they ate soup at the small table. It was thick with potatoes and leeks, with black pepper, bread, and a bowl of apples set within reach. The stranger ate slowly. He seemed unused to being asked whether he wanted more without being asked what he intended to give in return. Luke told him about the chamber under the western hall, the unfinished road, and the vessel left in the chest. He did not tell the story as though the stranger must know it. He described only what he and Martin had seen.

When the meal was finished, the man carried his plate to the basin. Luke started to take it from him, but stopped when the man made no movement to surrender it.

"I can wash a plate," he said.

"I know."

"Then let me."

Luke stepped back. The stranger washed the plate, dried it, and set it on the shelf. It was a small act, but it changed the room's arrangement. He was not being displayed as a visitor or protected as an artifact. He was participating in the ordinary work of a household.

Before he went to the room prepared for him, he returned to the map. He stood over the covered name, studying the strip of paper.

"That should remain covered," he said.

Luke nodded.

"But the other route should be copied."

"Where does it go?"

"I don't know."

"Then why copy it?"

"Because somebody took the trouble to draw it, and because it avoids the gates."

The older Luke found a second sheet. Martin held the lantern at a low angle, and Luke traced the faint line without touching the original map. He copied the visible route, including the portion that disappeared beyond the edge of the paper. At the end he wrote: DESTINATION UNKNOWN. SOURCE: ORIGINAL MAP. STATUS: OBSERVED, NOT TRAVELLED.

The stranger watched him finish. "That is better."

Luke placed the copy beside the photograph. The covered name remained untouched. The map now had a second life, but it had not been completed.

When the stranger went upstairs, the others did not follow. They heard his door close, then the ordinary sounds of someone setting down a bag and moving a chair. The House did not announce that a lost man had returned. It did not alter the corridor or illuminate the carvings. It allowed the room to remain a room.

In the morning, Luke found the stranger's coat hanging beside the stove, dry at last. The man was not there. He had gone out before the others woke, leaving the room as he had found it, except for the washed plate on the shelf and a small pencil line beside the copied route. He had added no name. He had marked a point in the forest where the line bent around a ridge.

The point was not on any of their maps.

Luke looked out at the redwoods. Rain still moved through the high branches, but the lower ground was beginning to dry. Somewhere beyond the visible tree line, the unapproved route continued.

He put the copy in the archive under a new heading: A ROUTE PRESERVED BY CONSENT.

The name remained covered. The man remained unnamed. The world had gained a road without gaining an explanation.
