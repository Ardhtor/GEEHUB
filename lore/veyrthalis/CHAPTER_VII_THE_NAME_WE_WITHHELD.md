# Chapter VII — The Name We Withheld

Luke held the photograph by its edges, afraid that touching the face itself might count as a kind of greeting. The stranger stood between two men beneath the redwood’s enormous lower branches, one hand resting on the shoulder of the broad man to his left and the other loosely closed at his side. He was not faceless now; he had a narrow, composed face, dark hair brushed back from his forehead, and an expression that seemed to belong to someone listening to a question he had already heard many times. The broad man was the figure from the damaged corridor photograph, while the taller man on the stranger’s right had the same long frame and powerful shoulders as the second figure there. All three wore ordinary clothes, but the stranger’s shirt had been fastened wrong at the collar, as if he had dressed in haste or someone else had dressed him.

“Do you know him?” Martin asked.

Luke turned the photograph over again. “Apparently I knew him well enough to write this.”

“That doesn’t mean you remember him,” said the older Luke.

“No. It means somebody wanted me to know I’d forgotten.”

The older man did not dispute it. Luke placed the photograph beside the map and examined the glass vessel, which lay in a fitted hollow lined with dark felt. The liquid inside was perfectly clear, but it seemed to gather the lantern light and hold it a moment longer than the glass around it; when Luke moved his head, a thin silver line crossed the vessel’s interior and vanished. There was no label, no stopper seal, and no trace of residue at the neck. It looked less like a sample taken from a laboratory than something prepared for a ceremony whose participants had refused to agree on the words.

Martin kept his position by the doorway. “We should take the map and leave.”

“Leave where?” Luke asked.

“Anywhere that isn’t a room with a chest that knocks.”

“That’s not a direction.”

“It’s a preference.”

The older Luke lifted the map and unfolded it across the workbench. Its paper was thick and fibrous, stained at the creases, and the lines had been drawn in several inks. At first Luke saw only a topographic survey: contours descending from the redwood heights toward a long basin, streams branching through the trees, a broken ridge marked with small crosses. Then the older man turned the map ninety degrees, and the contours became walls, the streams became corridors, and the basin resolved into a plan of rooms arranged around a central void. The same geography could be read as a forest or as a house, depending on which edge one chose as the ground.

“Which is it?” Luke asked.

“Both records are accurate,” said the older Luke. “Neither is complete.”

He set a finger on a black circle near the center. A thin red line ran from it to the place where they stood, then continued beyond the map’s edge. Along the line, someone had written three names in small capitals: the family that protected the baseline, the family that opened it, and the man whose name had been withheld. The first two had been crossed through, not erased. The third had been covered by a strip of paper pasted over the ink.

Luke tried to lift the strip with a fingernail, but the older man caught his wrist. His grip was firm without being painful, and Luke felt the difference between the older man’s strength and his own in the brief pressure of that hand. “Don’t peel it,” the older Luke said. “The name is attached to the paper beneath it. If you remove the cover, you remove the record.”

“Then how do we read it?”

“We ask why it was covered.”

The chest’s lid rested open behind them. Luke could see the empty felt hollow where the vessel had lain, and the pale outline left by the photograph. The objects had been arranged to be discovered in a particular order, but their order did not tell him what to do. He looked again at the map, at the red line joining the chamber to the black circle, and noticed that a second line had been drawn so faintly it only appeared when the lantern was held low. It began at the chamber and travelled outward into the forest, avoiding every marked path.

“What’s that line?” he asked.

The older Luke leaned closer. His shoulder brushed Luke’s, a small contact that steadied them both against the narrow workbench. “A route that wasn’t approved.”

“By which family?”

“Both.”

A dull sound came from the passage. Martin turned sharply, but nothing appeared in the doorway. The sound came again, not a knock this time, but the slow compression of something heavy settling onto a wooden floor. Luke’s shirt tightened across his upper chest as he drew a breath; the sensation was subtle, a measured pull along the seams, and he found himself adjusting his stance to keep the fabric from binding under his arms. He had grown used to these small revisions, the way his balance and reach needed to be recalculated, but now the room seemed to be responding to the same pressure, its beams giving a low creak as if a weight had been placed somewhere above them.

“Is that the guest?” Martin asked.

“No,” said the older Luke. “That’s the house making room.”

“For what?”

The older man looked at Luke rather than the ceiling. “For the version of events that comes next.”

Luke took the map from the bench. He folded it along its existing creases, careful not to cover the faint route, and slipped it inside his shirt. The paper lay against him beneath the fabric, a dry, cool plane over his sternum. He picked up the photograph and kept it in his hand. The vessel remained in the chest, untouched.

“Why leave the Serum?” Martin asked.

“Because we don’t know whether it’s the original,” Luke said. “And because if it is, somebody put it here for a reason.”

The older Luke studied him with a look Luke could not quite read. “That is the first sensible thing you’ve said about it.”

“Good. I’m improving.”

They moved toward the doorway. Martin went first, then Luke, with the older man behind him. At the threshold Luke glanced back at the open chest, expecting the lid to fall or the vessel to move, but the chamber remained still. Only the lantern flame shifted, bending toward the map hidden beneath his shirt. He felt a faint warmth spread through the paper—not from the Serum, he thought, but from his own body—and then the sensation passed.

The corridor beyond the doorway had changed. Its stone walls were interrupted by tall wooden panels, each panel carved with a different version of the same three figures. In one carving, the stranger stood between the two family heirs with their hands joined over a bowl. In another, the heirs faced away from him while the stranger looked straight out from the wood. In a third, the stranger had been reduced to a narrow empty space between the other two men, an absence shaped precisely to fit a body.

Luke stopped before that panel. The carved edges of the gap were smooth, polished by repeated touch. Someone had been tracing the outline for years.

“Was he killed?” Luke asked.

The older Luke’s answer came after a pause. “That was one of the explanations.”

“And the others?”

“That he left. That he changed. That he was never a person. That he was the first baseline and therefore could not be measured. That he was the only one who understood what the Serum was doing.”

“Which explanation is true?”

The older man looked down the corridor. “They can’t all be true. But each one has been used to justify what happened afterward.”

From somewhere ahead came a quiet, deliberate voice. “You’ve brought the map.”

Martin stepped in front of Luke. The passage narrowed between two oak beams, and beyond them stood a man wearing a dark coat, his hands visible and empty. He was broad through the chest, with a heavy, carefully balanced stance, but his face was the one from the photograph: composed, attentive, unmistakably alive. He looked first at the older Luke, then at Martin, and finally at Luke himself.

“You’re early,” the man said.

Luke tightened his hold on the photograph. “And you’re the guest.”

The man’s expression altered—not quite a smile, but the recognition of a word spoken incorrectly on purpose. “No,” he said. “I’m the reason you made that word forbidden.”
