# Chapter VI — The Basement in the Woods

The passage descended at an angle gentle enough that Luke did not notice how far they had gone until the air changed. The smell of wet bark thinned, replaced by cold stone and the mineral odor of water held too long in a closed place. A narrow strip of light ran along the floor, though there were no lamps overhead and no visible source for it. The older Luke walked ahead with one hand sliding along the wall, feeling for irregularities. Martin followed Luke, and the fourth man brought up the rear, occasionally turning to look behind them as if he expected the door to have opened again.

After several minutes, the passage widened into a low chamber. Its ceiling was crossed by thick beams of dark oak, each one scarred with old cuts and pale patches where the bark had been stripped away. A workbench stood against the far wall, crowded with jars, calipers, folded cloth, and small instruments whose uses Luke could not guess. Beside it rested a wooden chest reinforced with black iron, its lid shut and its surface dulled by years of handling. The chamber was so carefully ordinary that Luke found himself searching for the trick in its construction, but the floor was uneven beneath his boots and a bead of water slipped from a beam onto his sleeve.

“Is this the basement?” Martin asked.

“It’s one of them,” said the older Luke.

“How many basements can a house have?”

“Depends what the house is built on.”

The older man crossed to the workbench and took a small notebook from beneath a folded length of canvas. He opened it near the middle, where the pages had swollen and dried in rippled layers. There were columns of measurements, dates, and short observations written in several hands, some neat and technical, others hurried enough to tear through the paper. Luke recognized his own name on one page, followed by a sequence of numbers that meant nothing to him, then a note written in block capitals: BASELINE IS NOT A LIMIT. IT IS THE LAST AGREEMENT.

Luke leaned over the notebook. “Who wrote this?”

“Several people.”

“Did I?”

The older Luke looked at the handwriting. “Not yet.”

Luke straightened. The word yet seemed to hang in the room longer than the others. He could feel his shirt against his chest and shoulders, the small drag of fabric where it crossed him when he turned. The sensation was not painful, nor was it even especially uncomfortable; it was simply present, a continuous reminder that the body was not an illustration on a page but a structure negotiating with gravity, cloth, and the space around it. When he stepped back from the workbench, he misjudged the distance and brushed the edge with his hip.

“Careful,” Martin said.

“I’m fine.”

“I didn’t say you weren’t.”

The exchange should have ended there, but Martin kept looking at him. “You’re moving differently.”

Luke glanced down at himself. “I know.”

“Does it hurt?”

“No. It’s more like I keep getting the dimensions wrong.”

The older Luke shut the notebook. “That’s the first adjustment. Your old measurements still work, but your instincts haven’t caught up. Don’t fight them by pretending nothing has changed. Give yourself room, move deliberately, and pay attention to what your body actually tells you.”

Martin looked at the older man. “You make it sound like learning to use a new tool.”

“It’s closer to learning how to live in a house after someone has moved the walls.”

Luke glanced toward the iron-bound chest. “What’s in there?”

“Records,” the older Luke said.

“Only records?”

“Mostly.”

Luke walked over to it. The chest was waist-high, broad enough that two men could have carried it between them, and its iron fittings were worn smooth at the corners. A small brass plate had been fixed to the front, but whatever had been engraved there was obscured by scratches. Luke ran a finger across the plate and felt the grooves catch his skin. Beneath the lid, something shifted with a muted scrape.

The older Luke moved at once. “Don’t open it.”

Luke drew his hand away. “Because of the guest?”

“Because it isn’t ready.”

“Who decides that?”

The older man did not answer immediately. He took a lantern from the bench, checked its wick, and lit it with a match that flared sulfur-yellow in the damp air. “The chest was made to hold what the families couldn’t agree to keep in the same room. It doesn’t contain a person, if that’s what you’re thinking. It contains a decision that was never completed.”

Martin gave a short laugh. “That’s worse.”

“Yes,” said the older Luke. “It is.”

He led them through another doorway, this one so low that each man had to duck. Beyond it lay a narrow storage room with shelves running from floor to ceiling. The shelves held shoes, folded shirts, belts, coats, and small personal objects arranged in careful groups. Some garments were new; others had been mended so often that the original fabric survived only in patches. A few pieces were unmistakably Luke’s size, while others were cut for a body broader through the chest or longer in the arms, and every item carried a paper tag tied through a buttonhole or belt loop.

Luke took down a dark shirt. The cotton was soft from washing, the collar faintly stretched, and a tiny pale mark sat near the hem. It was identical to the shirt he was wearing. He held the two side by side, searching for some difference in the weave or stitching, but even the loose thread near the left cuff matched. Then he noticed that the shirt on the hanger had been altered across the upper back, its seams let out and resewn with dark thread.

“Why keep clothes?” he asked.

“Because bodies change,” the older Luke said. “And because people lie about how much they’ve changed. A garment keeps a record without needing to remember what it was supposed to mean.”

Luke ran his thumb along the altered seam. “So the clothes are evidence.”

“They’re evidence of contact. That’s not always the same as evidence of truth.”

At the far end of the storage room, a shelf had been left empty except for a folded length of red cloth. The older Luke took it down and spread it across a table. It was not a flag or a uniform, only a broad strip of fabric, dark crimson and slightly glossy where the weave caught the lantern light. A symbol had been stitched into one end: two interlocking shapes, one square and one long oval, joined by a short vertical line.

“Which family?” Martin asked.

“Both,” the older Luke said. “Before they divided.”

Luke touched the embroidery. “What happened?”

“They disagreed about what the Serum was for. One side believed a stable baseline should be inherited and protected. The other believed it should remain open to anyone willing to undergo the change. At first they argued about methods, then about access, then about which bodies counted as proof that the method worked. Eventually they began measuring one another as if measurement could settle the argument.”

“And the man in the photograph?” Luke asked. “The one whose face was scratched out?”

“He tried to make them share the same record.”

“Did he fail?”

The older Luke folded the cloth carefully. “He made them share it. That was the problem.”

A noise came from the chamber outside. Not the knock, not the familiar pattern, but a slow dragging sound followed by a pause. All three men turned toward the doorway. The lantern flame leaned sideways, though there was no wind, and the old beams gave a faint creak above them. Luke could hear water dripping somewhere in the walls, each drop separated from the next by an interval that was almost regular.

The older Luke set the cloth down. “Stay here.”

Luke caught his arm. “No. We’re done with you disappearing around corners and coming back with another riddle.”

The older man looked at Luke’s hand where it gripped his sleeve. For an instant, his expression changed—not surprise, exactly, but a kind of exhausted recognition. “Then stay beside me,” he said. “Whatever happens, don’t let the house separate us by assigning us different explanations.”

They returned to the chamber together. The dragging sound had stopped. The chest remained shut, but the brass plate on its front was now clean, as if someone had rubbed away the grime while they were in the storage room. Its engraving had become legible: LUKE — CONTINUITY COPY. Below the words was a small circular mark, with three short lines above it, three below, and one cut through the center.

Martin backed away. “That’s the pattern.”

Luke stepped toward the chest. “It’s my name.”

“It’s a label,” said the older Luke, though his voice had gone quiet.

Luke looked at him. “You don’t believe that.”

“No.”

The chest gave a single knock from within. The sound was low and solid, like a fist against thick timber. Luke felt it through the soles of his boots. A second knock followed, then a third, and after a pause three more sounded in a measured sequence. The final knock came from somewhere behind them, not inside the chest but from the low doorway through which they had entered.

The older Luke turned toward it. “We have to choose which one to answer.”

Luke looked from the chest to the doorway. “What happens if we answer both?”

“Then the house will decide they mean the same thing.”

The room seemed to contract around that sentence. Luke could still see the damp stone, the workbench, the tools, the folded canvas, the water shining on the floor. He could still feel the weight of his body and the fabric across his shoulders, and those ordinary sensations became the only things he could hold without interpretation. He took one step toward the doorway, then stopped and looked back at the chest.

“I’m not choosing between two things I don’t understand,” he said. “We open the chest together, and we keep the door in sight.”

The older Luke stared at him. Then, slowly, he nodded. Martin moved to the doorway, planting himself where he could watch both the passage and the room. Luke and the older man stood on either side of the iron-bound chest, and when they lifted the lid, the hinges moved without a sound.

Inside lay a folded map, a small glass vessel filled with clear liquid, and a photograph of three men standing in front of a redwood tree. The man in the center had not been scratched out. His face was intact, and it was the face of the stranger from the window.

On the back of the photograph, in handwriting Luke recognized as his own, were the words: **WE AGREED NOT TO CALL HIM THE GUEST.**
