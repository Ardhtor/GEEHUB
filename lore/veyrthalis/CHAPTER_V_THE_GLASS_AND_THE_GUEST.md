# Chapter V — The Glass and the Guest

Luke turned so quickly that his shoulder struck the window frame. The sound was a small, blunt knock, and for one irrational instant he expected the glass to answer it with the familiar sequence: three, three, one. Nothing answered. Behind him, the dining room remained exactly as it had been: twelve places set, coffee cooling in the cups, the bowl of fruit centered on the table, the older version of himself standing with one hand resting on the sill. The chair at the far end was still pulled away from the table. The room had the ordinary, faintly stale warmth of a place where several people had been sitting for a while.

There was nobody behind Luke.

He turned back to the window. The third reflection was still there. It stood a little to the left of the older Luke’s reflected shoulder, close enough that the two figures ought to have overlapped. Its face was indistinct, not blurred like a photograph out of focus but withheld, as though the glass had decided that the face was not among the details it was prepared to reproduce. The raised hand remained against the pane. Luke could see the pressure of its palm in the slight flattening of the reflected light.

“Can you see him?” Luke asked.

The older Luke did not look away from the window. “I can see where he is.”

“That isn’t the same thing.”

“No,” he said. “It isn’t.”

One of the others came nearer. His name was Martin, though Luke had discovered that names in this place seemed to arrive late, like labels attached after the object had already been handled. Martin leaned in beside him, careful not to touch the glass. “There’s someone outside?”

“Not outside,” the older Luke said.

Martin looked at the reflected hand. “Then where?”

The older man finally turned. “That’s what we need to be precise about.”

Luke disliked the way he said it. Precision had become a kind of evasion here: a way to keep a person walking around the edge of an answer until the answer had changed shape. He put his own hand against the window. The glass was cool. He felt the ridges of his palm, the small callus near the base of his middle finger, the faint drag of skin against the surface. His reflection copied him. The older Luke’s reflection copied him too, although the real man stood motionless beside the sill.

The third figure lowered its hand.

Luke’s did not.

He snatched his palm away. The other hand remained visible for another moment, pressed flat to the glass, then withdrew into the reflected room behind them. The window showed only the four men and the forest beyond, its immense redwood crowns descending toward the amber valley. Far below, the lantern-bearing figures had vanished. The lights of the not-yet-built city remained, scattered along the distant slope like windows in a place where people had already gone to bed.

“Was that one of them?” Martin asked.

“No,” said the older Luke.

“Who are they, then?”

“I don’t know.”

It was the first answer the older man had given that sounded unprotected. Luke looked at him more closely. There were fine creases beside his eyes and a faint indentation across the bridge of his nose, perhaps from glasses he no longer wore. His shirt was clean but had softened with repeated washing. One cuff had been repaired with thread that did not quite match. The changes in his body were similarly unannounced: the heavier shoulders, the dense upper arms, the broad chest beneath the fabric. He occupied the room differently from Luke, but not as a stranger would. He stood as someone accustomed to making space without thinking about it, shifting his weight before turning, keeping a careful margin between his body and the table’s edge. The years had altered him through use.

“Then stop telling us what we should do,” Luke said. “Tell us what you actually know.”

The older Luke looked down at the coffee cups. “I know that the house is not a trap in the way you mean. I know that the floors are connected, although the stairs don’t always take you between them. I know that if we go back down now, we’ll find the corridor has changed. I know that the people in the photographs are not all family, but each of them has made a decision that became someone else’s inheritance. And I know that the Serum doesn’t make a body grow by issuing an order. It changes what the body can accommodate, and then it waits to see what the body does with the permission.”

Luke glanced toward his own chest, an involuntary movement. His shirt had felt tighter since they entered the house. Not painfully tight, and not in a way that could be explained by a sudden transformation. It was the accumulating difference he had noticed before: the fabric drawing across his upper torso when he reached, the seams resting a little farther outward, the sense that his shoulders had become more substantial while his hands and face remained unmistakably his. The changes were small enough to ignore individually. Together they altered how he stood. He found himself keeping his elbows closer to his sides when passing between chairs, then letting them relax again when the space opened. His body was teaching him a new estimate of itself.

“Does it always happen?” he asked.

“No.”

“What decides?”

“Use. Stress. Repetition. Sometimes refusal.” The older Luke paused. “The Serum records the difference between what you can bear and what you agree to carry. Those aren’t always the same.”

Martin gave a low, skeptical laugh. “That sounds like something written on a wall.”

“It probably was.”

For a moment the answer brought them back to the table, to coffee and fruit and the absurd comfort of ordinary conversation. Luke noticed that he was still hungry. He took an apple from the bowl and rubbed it against his shirt before biting into it. The skin gave with a crisp snap. The taste was tart, almost sharp. He chewed slowly, aware of the sound in the quiet room, and of how badly he wanted the world to remain composed of things that could be bitten, swallowed, and finished.

The older Luke pulled out a chair. “We should sit down.”

“No,” Luke said. “We should leave.”

“Where?”

“Outside. Back to the forest. We find the tree and go through the door.”

“You can try.”

“I’m not asking permission.”

“I know.” The older man’s expression softened, though Luke could not tell whether it was affection or recognition. “That’s why I think you might manage it.”

They left the dining room together. The corridor beyond it smelled faintly of cedar and old paper. Luke kept his right hand near the wall, not touching it, using the repeated frames as a measure of distance. In the photographs, men stood beside machinery and trees, in laboratories and streets, on porches and at the edges of fields. He began to notice a detail he had missed before: in nearly every picture, at least one man was looking away from the camera. Not distracted, exactly. Looking toward something just outside the frame.

At the staircase, Luke stopped beside a photograph of three men standing in front of a low stone building. The man on the left was broad and square through the chest, with a heavy jaw and a close-cropped head. The man on the right was taller, his frame long and powerful, one arm resting across the other’s shoulders. Between them stood a third man whose face had been scratched away—not torn from the photograph, but carefully abraded until the paper had become pale and fibrous.

“Who did that?” Luke asked.

The older Luke came to stand beside him. “The family on the left.”

“Which family?”

“The one that believed the Serum belonged to them.”

“And the other one?”

“They believed it belonged to whoever could survive it.”

Martin folded his arms. “And what did the man in the middle believe?”

The older Luke studied the damaged face. “He thought those were the same claim.”

Luke looked at the two intact men. Their expressions were not hostile. The broad man had a hand on the other’s shoulder, and the taller man’s head tilted slightly toward him. They could have been brothers, friends, rivals who had spent years learning one another’s habits. Their closeness made the erased face more disturbing. Someone had taken care to preserve the two men and remove the one between them.

“Are they still here?” Luke asked.

“Somewhere.”

“Together?”

The older Luke did not answer.

They descended. The staircase seemed shorter than before, but the landing opened onto a different corridor, one with pale walls and a row of narrow windows. Beyond the windows was not the forest but a long, low room furnished with metal examination tables. The sight of it made Luke’s stomach tighten. He knew the arrangement: the clinical light, the clean floor, the blue cast of screens waking in the dark. The Facility. Or a room that had learned the Facility’s shape.

Inside, a man stood with his back to the window. He wore a white coat over a dark shirt. He was broad through the shoulders, his posture balanced and still. A measuring tape hung from one hand. On the table before him lay a folded shirt, a pair of trousers, and a clipboard.

Luke moved closer to the glass.

The man turned his head.

He was looking directly at Luke.

The window did not reflect the dining room or the staircase. It showed the examination room in sharp detail: the seam in the linoleum, the steel legs of the table, the white coat’s creases at the elbow. The man raised the measuring tape and drew it across his own chest, then wrote something on the clipboard. He repeated the measurement. He wrote again.

“What is he doing?” Martin asked.

“Taking a baseline,” said the older Luke.

“For who?”

The man in the white coat lifted the clipboard. The writing was too small to read, but beneath the ruled lines, in block capitals, Luke could make out a name.

LUKE.

His mouth went dry.

The man set down the clipboard, picked up the folded shirt, and held it against his chest. It was Luke’s shirt: the same dark fabric, the same worn collar, the same tiny pale mark near the hem. The man examined the sleeves, then folded it again with careful, practiced movements. He placed it on the table beside the trousers.

Luke looked down at his own clothes. He was still wearing them.

“Don’t open that window,” the older Luke said.

“I wasn’t going to.”

“You were thinking about it.”

Luke stepped back. “Why does he have my clothes?”

“Because someone has to keep track of what changes.”

“Then why does he need a second set?”

The older Luke looked at the room beyond the glass. “He doesn’t.”

A small red light came on above the examination table. The man in the white coat glanced toward it, then turned a page on the clipboard. Luke saw the movement of his lips. He was speaking, but there was no sound through the glass. A moment later, the fluorescent lights dimmed and returned. The man looked up again. This time he smiled—not warmly, not cruelly, but with the faint satisfaction of someone whose calculation had finally balanced.

He wrote one more line.

Then he turned the clipboard around and held it up to the window.

The message was plain.

**BASELINE REVISED. SUBJECT CONTINUES.**

Luke felt the Serum stir beneath his skin—not as heat or pain, but as a subtle gathering of attention. His shirt drew across his chest when he inhaled. He could feel the fabric’s weave against him, the pressure of the collar at the base of his neck, the weight of his arms hanging at his sides. He took a slow breath and let it out. Nothing dramatic happened. His body remained his body. But the room beyond the glass had measured him, and the measurement had become part of the room’s reality.

“Keep walking,” the older Luke said.

Luke did.

At the end of the corridor stood a door with no sign. The older man reached for the handle, then stopped and looked at Luke.

“You should understand one thing before we go through.”

“I’m listening.”

“The families aren’t fighting over a vial. They’re fighting over who gets to define the baseline. If one family can decide what counts as a normal body, a normal memory, a normal life, then everyone else has to change in relation to them.”

“And the Serum?”

“It makes that decision physical.”

Luke looked back at the examination room. The man in the white coat had disappeared. The table was empty. The clipboard remained, but the message had changed. From this distance Luke could read only the final line, written in a heavier hand than before:

**DO NOT LET THE GUEST ENTER THE HOUSE.**

A knock sounded from the other side of the unmarked door.

Three.

Three.

One.

The older Luke’s hand tightened around the handle.

Luke looked at the door, then at the window, where his reflection stood alone. Behind the reflected Luke, the third figure had returned. Its face was still hidden. This time it was not touching the glass. It stood with both hands at its sides, waiting patiently, as though the next move belonged to Luke.

“Who’s the guest?” Luke asked.

The older man opened the door.

“I think,” he said, “that’s what he wants us to call him.”

Beyond the threshold was a narrow passage sloping downward. The air that came up from it smelled of wet bark, cold stone, and something faintly metallic. Luke stepped forward. Behind them, the window gave a soft, settling click, like a latch finding its place.

Then the door closed, and the house continued without them.
