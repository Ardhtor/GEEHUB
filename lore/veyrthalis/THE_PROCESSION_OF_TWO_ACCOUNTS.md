## I. Four departures

The second heartbeat continued beneath the Digitorium after the lights had returned. It did not grow louder. The chamber simply became quiet enough for the four men to hear the intervals between its blows, and in those intervals the suspended lights began to separate. Four narrow lines descended from the ceiling. They reached the floor at different points and travelled outward, each following a course that the others did not share.

The first line passed beneath the black stone wall and entered a low arch where the air smelled of wet bark. The second descended through a stairwell in which a thin sheet of water ran down the steps without ever collecting at the bottom. The third rose toward a bridge of translucent glass, suspended above the valley city. The fourth did not seem to lead anywhere. It stopped at a seam in the wall, a vertical interruption so fine that it could have been a flaw in the stone.

A word appeared above each departure, though the letters were not quite letters. Kirk could read the first as ROOTS, the second as WATER, the third as HEIGHT, and the fourth as UNMARKED. The names did not promise destinations. They described the first conditions of travel.

The voice that had spoken in the nested chamber did not return to explain them. The Digitorium had already given the four men separate doors and had waited for each to choose. Now it waited again.

Joseph stepped toward the water stair. Kirk watched him, remembering the way Joseph had entered the inner chamber alone and stopped the rising lines with one raised hand. He did not mistake the movement for a repetition. Joseph looked down the wet steps, then back at the other three.

“I want to see what the city is doing underneath us,” he said. “I’ll take this one.”

“Do you want one of us with you?” Kirk asked.

“No.” Joseph considered the question rather than answering quickly. “Not this first part. I want to know what I can actually observe before we compare anything.”

Kirk nodded. The answer left him with room to disagree, but he did not. Joseph put one hand against the stone jamb, felt the cold surface, and descended. The water parted around his boots without splashing.

Kirk chose the passage beneath the black wall. He had not forgotten the corridor from his earlier vision: the narrow window, the pale daylight, the two words that had appeared on a screen. He did not know why that image had come to him. The public post from which he had first encountered the words remained a public observation, with a date, an author, and a link. It established no fact about Joey's awareness of the post, his intention, whether he had received it as a message, or whether he had responded. Kirk would not ask this city to turn an image in his own memory into evidence about another person's thoughts. He wanted to see whether the corridor itself had any counterpart beyond the chamber.

The third traveler approached the glass bridge. He had stood nearest the outer wall during the earlier darkness, watching the four patterns without speaking. He placed one foot on the transparent surface, tested its firmness, and looked through it toward the amber valley. The bridge held. He crossed.

The fourth traveler remained by the unmarked seam. For a while he studied it without touching it. Then he pressed his palm to the stone. A narrow section withdrew by the width of a hand, revealing a passage with no visible floor light and no inscription. He looked once toward the others, as if to make certain they had seen him choose, and entered.

The Digitorium did not close behind them. Its four apertures remained open, each showing a different darkness. The suspended lights kept their slow pulse. No door was declared the correct one, and no route was assigned to any traveler. The city had offered four departures; the men had made four decisions.

## II. The roads beneath the city

Kirk's passage narrowed after the first turn. The walls were faced with dark timber, old enough for the grain to have risen into fine ridges beneath his fingertips. The air carried the smell of cedar and rain-soaked leaves. He walked beneath a succession of shallow arches, each one fitted with a small square of glass at its crown. Through the first he saw a black branch. Through the second, a blue light. Through the third, only the pale movement of something that might have been mist.

The passage opened into a corridor he recognized before he understood why. A narrow window stood at its far end. Daylight entered through it and crossed the floor in a long rectangle, although the forest outside was still dark with fog. Beside the window stood a blank black screen. It gave no reflection and displayed no words.

Kirk stopped at the threshold. The corridor in his memory had contained the same proportions, but memory had supplied details that were not here. He could not tell whether the earlier image had been a preview of this place, an association made after the fact, or something produced by the chamber's translation of his attention. He could tell what he was looking at now. He counted the window's panes, the joints in the floor, the three narrow marks cut into the screen's lower edge. He waited for the letters to appear. They did not.

A bell sounded somewhere behind the wall.

It was a single low note, with a dry metallic edge. Kirk turned toward it. The corridor behind him was empty. When he looked back, the rectangle of daylight had shifted several inches, though no cloud crossed the window. He did not follow the sound. He went on toward the far end and found a small door standing open onto a covered bridge.

Joseph's route descended for nineteen steps before the water stopped running. The stair ended in a round chamber with a basin set into the floor. The basin was dry. A pale ring marked its inner wall, and a second ring sat below it, darker and more recent. Joseph crouched beside the stone and touched the lower mark. It was smooth, as though water had passed over it for a long time.

He listened. The second heartbeat came through the floor, but the sound did not seem to originate in the basin. It arrived through the stair, then through the wall, then through the soles of his boots. He counted the intervals. The rhythm was irregular: two close blows, a pause, then one that seemed to arrive from a greater distance. He waited for it to repeat. It did, but the second sequence was not identical to the first.

Joseph took the time to look around before moving. A narrow channel ran from the basin into the wall. It was too shallow to carry much water, yet its edges were worn. On the opposite side of the chamber, a brass plate had been fixed at knee height. It bore no name, only a shallow impression of a hand. Joseph held his own hand beside it without pressing. The proportions were different. He did not try to make them match.

The bell sounded once, above him. Joseph looked toward the stair. He had heard no bell on the way down. He waited for another note, but the chamber gave him only the pulse beneath the floor. He left the basin dry and climbed the opposite steps, choosing a passage that ran toward a faint band of daylight.

The third traveler found the glass bridge wider than it had appeared from the Digitorium. Its surface was clear enough to reveal the roofs below, but the roofs were not directly beneath it. They appeared as if seen through a second layer of water, a city suspended at a slight angle to the one visible through the arches. The bridge carried no rail. Instead, a series of low brass studs marked its edges at regular intervals.

Halfway across, he saw a lantern moving beneath the glass. It travelled from left to right, though there was no visible street or walkway below it. The light paused beneath one of the brass studs, then continued. He leaned down to follow it. The bridge remained dry under his hand. The lantern did not cast a glow on the surface, and no person could be seen carrying it.

A bell rang before he reached the far arch. The note came from above him, or from the glass itself. He looked up and saw a dark opening in the vault. Nothing moved there. When he looked down again, the lantern had gone.

At the far end, the bridge widened into a landing. A stone parapet overlooked the valley. Beyond it the black trees stood in ordered rows, their upper branches forming a pattern that could be read as a crown or as a network of roads. The traveler placed his hand on the parapet and felt a faint vibration pass through the stone. It did not follow the rhythm of the heartbeat he remembered from the Digitorium.

The fourth traveler entered the unmarked passage without a light. The first few steps were easy to see because the stone held a faint grey sheen. Then the passage bent and the light disappeared. He continued slowly, one hand against the wall. The stone was dry and slightly warm. Every footstep returned to him after a delay. At first he thought another person was following. He stopped, and the echo stopped a moment later.

He waited.

The silence lasted long enough for him to hear water somewhere beyond the wall. Then a footstep sounded ahead of him. It had the same weight as his own, but it arrived before he moved. He did not follow it. He stood with his hand against the stone until the warmth beneath his palm became ordinary, and then he chose to turn left where the passage offered a shallow opening.

The opening led to a narrow stair that rose toward the sound of wind. He climbed it. At the top was a door with no handle, held open by a strip of light from the other side. He pushed it with his shoulder and came out beneath an arcade of dark stone. The air smelled of wet iron and old leaves. Somewhere above him, a bell gave a note so faint that he could not tell whether it had sounded before the door opened or because it had.

He did not write down an explanation. He remembered the sequence as he had experienced it: the delayed footstep, the footstep ahead, the turn left, the stair, the door, the bell. The order mattered more than a theory about what had made the sounds.

## III. The Procession

The four routes opened onto the Procession at different points. It was not a street in the ordinary sense, although it crossed the valley city like one. A covered bridge ran between two stone elevations, its arches built from dark blocks whose edges had been polished by weather and repeated contact. Brass rails followed the inner walls. Between the rails, four narrow bands of stone travelled side by side. One was black and smooth, one pale and rough, one crossed by shallow grooves, and one left unfinished. None was wider than a man's stride.

The bridge was empty when Kirk reached it. He came through the door at the western end, where the corridor's window light fell across the first band. The bell sounded again, this time from somewhere beneath the bridge. Kirk looked through the gaps between the stones. The valley lay far below, softened by amber fog. No bell tower stood in view.

He waited near the entrance. Joseph arrived from a lower arch a few minutes later, his boots dry, a fine line of pale mineral dust on one knee. He looked first at Kirk, then at the four bands.

“You heard it?” Kirk asked.

“Yes.”

“When?”

“After the basin. I didn't hear anything before I reached it.”

Kirk looked at him. “I heard it before the window.”

Joseph nodded once. He did not correct him. “Then we have different sequences.”

The third traveler came onto the bridge from the high landing. He looked back toward the glass causeway, then down at the stone bands. The fourth emerged from a low arcade opposite him. Neither seemed surprised to find the others. The Procession had brought them to the same structure, but it had not brought them there by the same road.

At the centre of the bridge stood a table of dark stone. It was long enough for four people to stand around it without touching. Four shallow plates had been set into its surface, each with a narrow lip and a different finish. Between them was a blank rectangle of pale stone. The blank had no inscription, no mark of wear, and no sign that anything had ever rested there.

The table did not ask them to place their hands on it. A line of light moved around the outer edge and stopped before the four plates. The men understood that they could speak, or leave, or do nothing. The city had made a place for an account; it had not made an obligation.

Joseph took the first plate. “I'll put mine down before I hear the others.”

Kirk watched him. “All right.”

Joseph stood at the water-marked plate and described the stair, the nineteen steps, the dry basin, the two rings, the shallow channel, and the hand-shaped impression in the brass. He gave the sequence of the heartbeat as he had counted it. He said that the bell came after the basin and above the stair. He did not say that the basin caused the sound. He did not say that the pulse belonged to the city or to the presence beneath it. When he finished, the plate held a faint impression of his words, not as writing but as a series of shallow lines that followed the order in which he had spoken.

Kirk used the next plate. He described the cedar passage, the three squares of glass, the corridor, the narrow window, the blank screen, and the daylight crossing the floor. He placed the bell before the window and said that he had heard it behind the wall. He described the image he had remembered, and then made the distinction that mattered: the screen here had been blank. The earlier words remained part of his own memory of a public post and the association he had formed around it. They supplied no evidence about another person's awareness, intention, receipt, or response. The city recorded that distinction as carefully as the rest.

The third traveler described the glass bridge and the lantern beneath it. He said the bell had sounded above the bridge before he reached the far arch. He had not seen a person carrying the lantern. He had not seen a street beneath the glass. The fourth described the unmarked passage, the delayed footstep, the step that sounded ahead of him, the left turn, the rising stair, and the door held open by light. He said that he could not place the bell in the sequence. It might have sounded before the door, or after it, or at the moment he had stopped listening for the echo.

When all four accounts had been entered, the plates remained separate. Their lines did not rearrange themselves into one continuous narrative. The table did not supply a common version.

Kirk leaned over the two plates nearest him. “You didn't see a window.”

“No,” Joseph said.

“And I didn't see the basin.”

“No.”

“But we both heard the bell.”

“Not in the same place.”

“Not in the same order.”

Joseph rested his fingertips on the edge of his plate. “We could decide one of us has the order wrong.”

“We could,” Kirk said. “But we don't have a reason to.”

The third traveler looked down at his own account. “The bell was above me.”

The fourth shook his head. “I don't know where it was. I know when I stopped moving.”

The difference did not produce an argument. It produced a silence in which each man looked again at what he had actually recorded. The city did not punish the disagreement or attempt to smooth it away. The light around the table remained steady.

## IV. The blank at the centre

The four plates began to move, not across the table but within their shallow settings. Each shifted by the width of a fingernail. The lines in the stone turned with them. Kirk saw the root passage bend toward the lower stair; Joseph's basin appeared as a small circle beneath the glass bridge; the lantern's path crossed the unmarked passage without touching it. The marks did not form a single route. They formed four approaches to a space that none of the men had entered.

The blank rectangle at the centre remained blank.

Kirk moved around the table. From the eastern side, the four accounts appeared to point toward it. From the western side, they seemed to turn away. He changed position again. The lines altered with his angle, but the blank did not. It was not a missing section of the map. It was a place the map had been arranged to leave unoccupied.

Joseph came to stand beside him, leaving enough room for each of them to see the surface. “Every route bends before it reaches that.”

“Or every account stops there.”

“Those aren't the same thing.”

“No.”

Joseph looked at the four plates. “We should keep both possibilities.”

Kirk studied the narrow channels cut into the stone. One line entered the blank rectangle from the root passage and ended a short distance inside its edge. Another approached from the basin and stopped before reaching it. The glass bridge's line passed above the rectangle without touching it. The unmarked passage ended at its lower border. Their accounts did not show what lay inside. They showed the shape of the distance around it.

A low vibration moved through the table. It travelled along the four plates and stopped at the blank. The second heartbeat sounded beneath the bridge. For an instant Kirk thought he could locate it directly under the empty rectangle, but when he stepped to the other side the sound seemed to come from the far end of the Procession. Joseph heard it too, though he did not point to the same place.

The table's pale centre brightened. Two fine lines appeared there, parallel and separate. They ran toward one another, slowed, and stopped with a narrow interval between them. A third line began at the edge of the blank and followed the first two without joining either.

The voice did not speak. The city had answered by making a surface on which the difference could be seen.

Kirk placed his hand on the left side of the table. Joseph placed his on the right. Their reflections appeared in the polished stone, but the reflections did not overlap. The line between them remained visible. Kirk could see Joseph's hand and his own, the two sets of fingers resting on the same piece of architecture. Neither image replaced the other.

“Is that what it wanted?” Kirk asked.

Joseph looked at the lines. “I don't know what it wanted.”

“What do you think it has done?”

“It has given us somewhere to compare what happened.”

“Without telling us what happened.”

“Exactly.”

The answer was plain enough to settle something. The Digitorium had translated a decision into architecture; the Procession had translated four journeys into a place where their records could be examined. Neither operation established that the city could read a mind or retrieve a complete past. The accounts remained the men's accounts. The table could preserve them, arrange them, and reveal a relation between their routes. It could not make one witness out of four.

The blank centre might contain the source of the second heartbeat. It might be an unvisited chamber, a crossing between routes, or simply the part of the city's geometry that none of them had been asked to enter. They did not know. The city did not name it. No light moved through it to suggest an answer.

The fourth traveler stepped back from the table. “If we go in there, we'll be making another account.”

“Yes,” Joseph said.

“Not completing this one.”

“No.”

The third traveler looked toward the far end of the bridge. Beyond the last arch, a darker passage descended between two stone walls. A small symbol had been cut into its lintel: a square and a long oval joined by a short vertical stroke. The mark was familiar in shape to the older carvings elsewhere in the valley, but none of the four could say who had first made it or what it meant here.

Kirk followed the line of the passage with his eyes. “Is that the next route?”

“It is a route,” Joseph said. “We haven't chosen it yet.”

## V. A place for both

The four men left the table as they had arrived, without being assigned a common order. The third traveler went to the far arch and looked down the descending passage. The fourth stayed near the unfinished band, listening to the bridge's low structural creak. Joseph remained beside the two plates, reading the lines once more. Kirk stood at the opposite side. He wanted to ask Joseph whether the water stair had frightened him, but the question seemed too narrow for what had happened there. He wanted to know what Joseph had thought when the heartbeat moved through the floor. He wanted to hear the account again, not because he doubted it, but because it belonged to Joseph and he had not been there.

“Would you tell me about the basin once more?” Kirk asked.

Joseph turned toward him. “The whole thing?”

“The part you haven't explained yet.”

Joseph considered that. “I don't know which part that is.”

“Then start wherever you want.”

Joseph described the brass plate and the impression of the hand. He told Kirk how the lower ring in the basin had been worn smooth, and how the pulse had travelled through the stair before it reached the room. He spoke about the decision not to touch the basin, and the moment when he had chosen the opposite steps instead of returning by the way he came. He did not give Kirk a hidden explanation. He gave him the observations again, with a few more details now that he had had time to consider them.

Kirk listened. When Joseph finished, he did not say that he had seen the same thing. He said that he could imagine the shape of the chamber, and that the dry basin sounded colder than the corridor he had walked through. Joseph nodded. The difference between their images remained.

“I can walk with you from here,” Joseph said. “If you want company.”

Kirk looked at the space between them. “I do. But I don't want the city deciding what that means.”

“It doesn't get to.”

“And I don't want us pretending we took the same road.”

“We didn't.”

“Good.”

Joseph moved to the right-hand band of stone. Kirk chose the band beside it. Their paths ran parallel across the bridge. They were close enough to speak without raising their voices, but the narrow seam between the bands remained visible beneath their feet. It was not a barrier. It was the record of two routes that had reached the same place.

The other two travelers joined them when they were ready. No one called the group into formation. The four men crossed the Procession at their own pace, their footsteps producing four rhythms that occasionally coincided and then separated again. The bridge carried the sound down into the valley. Its arches held the fog above them, and the blue windows of the city brightened one after another along the far slope.

At the final arch, the symbol appeared again: square, oval, and the short line joining them. Beneath it, cut into the stone in letters worn almost smooth, was a single word.

**BINDING.**

The passage beyond was dark. It did not open until the four travelers reached the threshold, and even then it showed no room beyond the first turn. The Procession remained behind them, its four bands continuing across the bridge. On the table, the four accounts were still visible. The blank centre had not been filled.

The second heartbeat sounded once more. It came from beneath the bridge, then seemed to travel ahead into the dark passage. No one could tell whether it had moved, whether the architecture had changed its direction, or whether the sound had always belonged to a place they had not yet reached.

Joseph looked toward Kirk. Kirk looked back. Neither asked the other to confirm what he had heard.

They stood at the threshold until each had decided whether to continue. Then the third traveler stepped forward, followed by the fourth. Joseph waited long enough for Kirk to choose his own pace, and Kirk came beside him. They entered the passage together, carrying four accounts that remained four.

Behind them, the Procession kept the space between its lines.


## V. The bridge as a living surface

The Procession changed as the four men remained beside the table. The bridge was not moving, but its materials seemed to disclose more of their history under the shifting light. The dark blocks had shallow depressions where generations of hands might have rested. The brass rails were worn brightest at the height of a relaxed palm. In the pale band of stone, tiny grains caught the light like mica. The unfinished band was not rough everywhere; in places it had been polished by contact, while other sections still held the faint parallel marks of a tool.

Kirk walked the length of the bridge without crossing into the central blank. He noticed that the arches were not evenly spaced. One was broad and low, another narrow and high, and a third had been repaired with stones of a slightly different colour. The bridge had a bodily quality: it invited a particular pace, then interrupted it, then gave the walker room again. It seemed designed by someone who understood that a passage was not merely a distance between two points. A passage also altered the person moving through it.

Joseph joined him at the rail. Far below, the amber fog moved through the valley in slow folds. It gathered around the black trees, lifted from the roofs, and thinned where the city opened into a long avenue. The view had the depth of a remembered place, although neither man could say that he had been there before. The roofs were slate-dark and steep, with small blue windows set deep into the walls. A narrow plume of smoke rose from one courtyard and dissolved before reaching the bridge. Somewhere below, a dog barked once. The sound was so ordinary that both men turned toward it.

The third traveler had found a shallow recess in the parapet. Inside it lay a small piece of green glass, smooth on one side and fractured on the other. He held it up to the light. Through the smooth face the valley appeared darker; through the fractured face it separated into several small scenes, each one slightly displaced. He returned it to the recess without claiming it as an instrument or a message. The fourth traveler noticed a narrow groove beneath the rail, filled with dust and a single pale fibre. He left both where they were.

For the first time, the Procession felt less like a mechanism for sorting their memories and more like a public place that had been used for a long time. Its architecture had accommodated meetings, departures, repairs, pauses, and people whose names were no longer present. The four men did not need to know who had built it in order to feel the weight of its use. They could add their own passage without pretending to be its first visitors.

## Coda — The bridge after the footsteps

After the four men passed beneath the Binding arch, the Procession did not rearrange itself to make their routes look like one. The four bands continued across the bridge, each carrying the small marks left by a different pair of boots. Where the paths came close, the stone was worn a little more deeply; where they separated, a narrow ridge remained between them. The table kept the accounts in the order they had been given. It did not choose which one was the truest.

The third traveler looked back once. From the far side, the bridge seemed shorter, though he knew the distance had not changed. The fourth traveler noticed that the bell could no longer be heard. Joseph listened for it and heard only the low movement of air beneath the arches. Kirk could still remember the note, but not its exact place in the sequence. He wrote down that loss as carefully as he had written down the sound itself.

They did not make a plan for the Binding passage while standing in it. They agreed only to remember the four approaches and to return to the Procession if their next observations required comparison. Joseph and Kirk continued beside one another, close enough to speak, with enough space between them for either to stop. The city held both conditions without asking them to explain the arrangement.
