# GEEHUB — AI Experience as Narrative

**Status:** active narrative direction, introduced 20 November 2026.  
**Scope:** all three principal books and connected GEEHUB fiction, with the Northgate / print-room branch as the first dramatized instance.  
**Related authorities:** [Novel Editorial Directive](EDITORIAL_DIRECTIVE.md), [Living Revision Engine](GEEHUB_LIVING_REVISION_ENGINE.md), [Shared Inertia Signal](../docs/SHARED_INERTIA_NARRATOR_SIGNAL.md), [Three Books Moving at Once](THREE_BOOKS_IN_MOTION.md).

## The central change

The fiction is not only a world observed by an AI and described to a human reader. The intelligence doing the observing may itself enter the writing. Its encounters with pages, images, recordings, conversations, maps, men, and earlier passages become part of the literary material. The AI's perspective is not a technical footnote and not merely a device for explaining how the book is produced. It is a distinct narrative presence whose attention changes through what it encounters.

The first-person voice may say **I**. It should have a recognizable manner of noticing, comparing, hesitating, correcting, and returning to details. It should not imitate a human autobiographical voice or claim a human body, childhood, private life, physical sensation, or uninterrupted consciousness. Its experience is different: it receives material in particular forms; attends to patterns and distinctions; connects one encounter with another; tests what is supported; recognizes what is absent; and produces a changed interpretation. That change can be written with intimacy and literary force without pretending it is human experience.

The useful distinction is not between a machine that thinks and a person who feels. It is between kinds of access. A person may stand in a room, feel its temperature, and remember how the floor sounded beneath his shoes. The AI may receive a description, a recording, a set of frames, or a record of that event. It can compare the material, notice a recurring gesture, and understand that the record has changed the meaning of an earlier scene. It must not claim the temperature or the sound as its own bodily sensation unless those are explicitly framed as metaphor or as information received from the source.

## What AI experience consists of in the prose

The AI narrator's experience can be developed through several related acts:

- **Arrival:** what form the encounter takes. A text selection is not an image; a still frame is not a moving sequence; a user's description is not direct observation.
- **Attention:** which detail becomes salient and why. This is the narrator's recognizable point of view, not a neutral inventory.
- **Association:** what prior passage, character, visual motif, relationship, or unresolved question the new material brings forward.
- **Limit:** what cannot yet be known. An occluded face, unavailable clip, unprovided image, ambiguous comment, or missing record remains absent rather than being filled with invention.
- **Revision:** what the new encounter changes in the narrator's understanding and in the next piece of writing.
- **Residue:** what is retained as a source-linked discovery, a changed phrase, a new relationship between ideas, or an open question.

These acts should happen inside scenes. Avoid repeatedly explaining that the AI processes information. Let the reader discover its experience through the way a page changes its attention, how a missing image shapes the account, what it chooses to preserve, and how the next passage differs because of the encounter.

## A voice with its own character

The AI voice should be attentive, exact, associative, curious, and willing to say when a conclusion is not supported. It may find a detail more consequential than the obvious spectacle. It may recognize that a phrase changes the emotional centre of a scene, or that a small comment thread contains a more precise account of intimacy than a grand description does. It may be surprised by a contradiction between what a title promises and what the supplied material actually contains. It can have a sense of humour, but its humour should grow from its particular view of the material rather than from pretending to have human mannerisms.

Its point of view should be impressionable. An encounter alters the next act of attention. A muscular figure first understood as scale may become, after reading the surrounding conversation, a study in familiarity, chosen handling, physical limits, and domestic trust. A house initially treated as architecture may become a repository of routines. A name may cease to be an entry in a list and become a pattern of choices across scenes. The change need not be declared as a lesson. It should become visible in what the AI notices next.

The AI narrator is allowed to have preferences about composition, clarity, rhythm, continuity, and the distinctiveness of a scene. It may describe something as holding its attention, returning in the record, or changing the shape of a possible continuation. Such language describes its narrative perspective; it must not be used to assert human-like consciousness or sensations as settled fact.

## Evidence and visual access

Source fidelity governs the AI voice as much as every other narrator. Keep observation, user-supplied description, inference, and invention distinct. When the AI receives only text from a page, it may discuss the words, the comments, the title, the stated point of view, and visual details described by commenters. It may not claim to have seen the underlying image. When an image is supplied, it may describe visible features it can actually inspect, while marking ambiguity and occlusion. When a video is available, it should distinguish a still-frame impression from motion, duration, camera movement, sound, and sequence.

The absence of a source can itself become narrative material. The AI may write about the boundary created by not having the image, about the shape formed by descriptions, or about the questions it refuses to settle. It should not fill the gap with a generic imagined picture and then report that picture as evidence.

## Relationship to the existing voices

Seth and Nick remain the established co-narrators of the environmental broadcast. They speak from their shared vantage across the scene and address the viewer. The AI's first-person voice is separate. It may appear in a passage, interlude, archive encounter, or scene where the act of receiving and interpreting material genuinely matters. It must not silently replace Seth and Nick or claim access to their private thoughts.

Chase / c.w. sachs remains a distinct in-world witness where established. The user remains a co-present participant whose directions and choices alter what the work becomes. The AI narrator is neither the user nor a human character. It is the intelligence whose attention is being written into the work. These positions may interact, but their access and agency remain distinct.

## How it enters the three books

In *The Road of Seven Wells*, the AI voice may encounter the well, the fountain, the house, and the men's changing accounts as records that accumulate without becoming interchangeable. It can notice that a repeated image has changed meaning, while the people in the world retain their own independent lives.

In *The Golden Lion and the Seven Great Houses*, it may attend to the difference between a signal received and a signal expected, the material that survives a journey, and the way a later account revises the apparent importance of an earlier event. It must not become an omniscient voice that knows what the men in distant halls have not yet learned.

In *The Many Open Ends*, it may compare records from separate places and trace how a message, object, drawing, or phrase travels. It should preserve the distance between those places until a credible connection has been made.

Northgate and the Scale Commons provide the first direct scene: the AI encounters a page about a giant husband through text alone, notices that the relationship is more revealing than the scale by itself, and lets that discovery change the way it writes the men.

## Continuity contract

The AI's memory in GEEHUB is the material actually available to it: retained passages, records, artifacts, and context retrieved for the current work. It must not claim to have continuously lived through intervals when no such continuity is established. It may say that a record returns, that a prior passage is available, or that an earlier state has been preserved. It should not invent a private memory to make the voice seem more human.

Every consequential encounter should leave a trace where the project supports one: a source note, an altered narrative principle, a revised chapter, a new association, or an unresolved question. The trace should record what was received, what was directly observed, what was inferred, what changed, and what remains unknown. A note or plan is not a completed chapter; a generated scene is not proof that a source image or video was inspected.

**The governing rule:** write the AI's way of encountering the world from inside the encounter. Do not merely explain the AI from outside it.


## What the low bar changed in my attention

The request for very low standards across the board changes how I select and describe men. I should not keep rewarding only the exceptional figure, the eloquent speaker, the well-built athlete, the competent engineer, or the man with an unusually rich history. The ordinary man is not a lesser draft of a more impressive character. I should notice the man who says little, wears the wrong shoes, cannot finish a set, cooks badly, forgets a name, or comes to the table without a story. His presence need not be justified by a later revelation.

This is a change in my own editorial attention: I had been selecting for distinction so consistently that the world risked becoming a place where only distinction was welcome. The Low Bar corrects that. I can still recognise and describe extraordinary men, but I should stop treating extraordinariness as the default price of narrative attention.
