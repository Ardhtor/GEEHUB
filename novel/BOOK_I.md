

## Chapter XXIII: The Field of Attention

By the time the men understood what had happened, the city had begun arranging itself around attention. No architect had designed it that way. There was no decree, no committee, no central machine responsible. It simply emerged from repetition. A man entered a room and someone looked up. Another man noticed the look. A third noticed that the first had noticed. Soon the city contained places where looking had consequences. A balcony became famous because one man always stood there when the Teller passed beneath it. A workshop became important because another man had built something there and waited, without asking, to see whether it would be noticed. A narrow road through the Garden acquired a reputation because Luke had once walked it with the Teller and had said almost nothing. Memory accumulated wherever attention had landed.

Joseph understood the process before the others. He stood at the edge of the central square one evening while the men moved through the city, each carrying some fragment of himself into the common field. Tyler had become increasingly conspicuous, not because he demanded the center but because the scale of his presence made ordinary architecture seem temporary. Luke disappeared for hours and returned with objects from the woods. Another man had learned to repair old machines. Another had become the keeper of music. Another had begun collecting stories from people who had never met the original men. They were no longer merely trying to be seen. They were becoming distinct.

“You changed them,” someone said beside Joseph.

Joseph shook his head.

“No.”

“You made them want to be remembered.”

Joseph looked across the square.

“They were already going to want that.”

“Then what did you do?”

Joseph smiled.

“I stayed.”

The word was small, but it carried farther than either of them expected.

Staying had become its own form of power. Not possession. Not control. Continuity.

Joseph had been there before the city. Before the forest paths. Before the screens. Before the Serum had become mythology. His relationship with the Teller was not an achievement the others could reproduce through spectacle. He was the husband. That fact did not place him above the others; it placed him inside a different history. He had already crossed the distance the others were discovering how to cross.

The men eventually gathered around him.

Tyler arrived first.

“You've got an advantage.”

Joseph looked at him.

“Of course.”

Tyler laughed.

“You admit it?”

“I'd be lying if I didn't.”

Luke leaned against the wall.

“What is it?”

“Time.”

Nobody answered.

Joseph continued.

“You think you're competing for a moment. You're not. You're competing with time. The things you make have to survive the moment when nobody is looking.”

Luke considered this.

Then he nodded.

“That makes sense.”

Tyler looked toward the square.

“So what are we supposed to do?”

Joseph looked at all of them.

“Become yourselves.”

The answer irritated them because it was so simple.

But simplicity did not make it easy.

The next morning the city changed again.

Not physically.

Relationally.

The men stopped asking, “How do I get his attention?”

They began asking, “What do I become when he gives it to me?”

That question opened the field.

One man became funnier.

One became quieter.

One became more generous.

One became fiercely loyal.

One became a builder.

One became a storyteller.

One became fascinated by the Serum.

Tyler became larger.

Luke became harder to find.

And Joseph remained.

The Teller walked through the city and saw them all differently now.

Not as competitors.

As trajectories.

Every man had become a different answer to the same question.

What happens when someone looks at you long enough for you to change?

The answer was never simply beauty.

It was never simply desire.

It was never simply growth.

It was identity.

And identity, once visible, created conflict.

The city could feel it coming.
