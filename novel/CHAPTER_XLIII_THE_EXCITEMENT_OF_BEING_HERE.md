# The Excitement of Being Here

By late afternoon, the western residence had acquired a different atmosphere. The doors stood open, music travelled between floors, and the long windows caught the amber light coming through the sequoias. Men were arriving from the gym in loose groups, carrying their bags over one shoulder and continuing conversations that had begun hours earlier. Someone had taken over the kitchen and was preparing an enormous meal. Someone else stood in the entrance hall, laughing loudly enough for the upper floors to hear. There was no particular event on the calendar. That had become one of the defining characteristics of the place: things happened because people were there, because they wanted to see one another, because the ordinary business of being together had developed its own momentum.

Marcus was among the first to notice the change. He had been sitting near the timber staircase, watching the steady procession through the front doors. His presence had become a landmark in the building: broad, composed, and unmistakably confident, with a way of making an ordinary greeting feel like a reunion. He recognised one of the new arrivals immediately and stood to welcome him. They began talking about training, food, and the prospect of spending the evening together. Within minutes, three other men had joined them. Their conversation moved from one subject to another with interruptions, jokes, challenges, and increasingly elaborate claims about what they intended to accomplish in the coming months. Nobody needed to organise the gathering. The gathering organised itself around the pleasure of recognising familiar faces.

The newer residents were discovering that the place offered more than a room, a gym, and somewhere to work. It offered an entire social environment. There were familiar faces in unfamiliar rooms, people who remembered their names, people who asked how their day had gone, and people who seemed genuinely pleased when they appeared. It produced a particular sensation: the feeling of becoming recognisable. A man could walk downstairs and find that someone had saved him a seat. He could enter the gym and be greeted by someone who remembered his previous performance. He could spend an evening talking with a stranger and discover, a week later, that the stranger had become one of the people he most looked forward to seeing.

Joey understood this through the small intervals between encounters. He noticed when someone became more comfortable in a room, when a reserved person began contributing to the conversation, when two men who had previously exchanged only brief greetings started seeking each other out. There was excitement in witnessing those changes. It was not always loud. Sometimes it appeared in a glance across the dining room, a familiar gesture, or the simple decision to remain a little longer rather than return upstairs. Joey had begun to understand that familiarity was not the absence of excitement. It was one of its most durable forms. The first encounter might be memorable, but the tenth carried a different pleasure: the knowledge that the other person had become part of the shape of the day.

By evening, the gym had become another centre of activity. The equipment was in constant use, but the atmosphere was more social than competitive. Men compared their progress, exchanged advice, and made plans to train together again. A few were theatrical about their ambitions, announcing increasingly ridiculous targets while their friends challenged them to demonstrate what they could actually do. Reagan had developed a reputation for turning even a routine training session into an occasion. His confidence was infectious, although he was discovering that the other residents were quite capable of matching his energy. They were developing their own styles, reputations, and reasons for returning. The strongest personalities did not need to occupy every conversation. Their presence gave the room a charge, and the room returned that charge in different forms.

The excitement was not confined to the gym or to physical achievement. It travelled into the dining room, where the evening meal had become a noisy, generous affair. Men arrived in stages, some still wearing training clothes, others freshly changed, each bringing a different account of the day. The table grew longer as chairs were borrowed from adjoining rooms. A disagreement about music turned into a demonstration; a discussion about travel became a plan to visit somewhere together; an argument about who had improved most during the week ended with three men agreeing to train before breakfast. The pleasure was partly in the subjects themselves and partly in the ease with which the conversation kept finding new ones. Nobody had to perform enthusiasm on command. It was enough to be interested, to be funny, to listen, to challenge someone, or to discover that another person had been waiting to tell the same story.

What made the place compelling was that excitement did not belong to one person. It circulated. One man's enthusiasm encouraged another to participate; another man's confidence made someone else less self-conscious; a particularly good evening became a story retold over breakfast the following morning. The social world was becoming self-sustaining, not because everyone behaved alike, but because their differences gave them something to discover in one another. Some men were immediately magnetic, loud and physically expressive. Others were quieter, taking their time before revealing their humour or ambition. Some preferred a crowded room; others found their place in a conversation of two. None of these temperaments had to become the standard for everyone else. The building was large enough to hold them together without making them interchangeable.

Later, Joey found himself beside the windows overlooking the darkening woods. The common room remained occupied behind him. Music had softened, coffee was being made despite the late hour, and a few residents were discussing what they wanted the next season of the place to become. Joey watched the reflections move across the glass. He thought about the small changes that had taken place since the first arrivals: the names people now knew, the rooms that had acquired their own associations, the routines that had become shared without anyone formally declaring them. The place had begun to remember its inhabitants through use.

A man who had spent much of his life feeling that he was watching other people's social worlds from the outside could begin to imagine something different here. He could become part of the atmosphere rather than merely observe it. He could be appreciated, challenged, remembered, and welcomed. He could have a place in the building that was not conditional on being the strongest, the loudest, or the most impressive person in the room. There was room for confidence without domination, attraction without entitlement, competition without humiliation, and affection without the demand that every friendship take the same form. The men were allowed to be distinct, and the relationships were allowed to develop at their own pace.

That was the deeper source of male excitement among the men who lived there: the discovery that there was room to become more fully oneself in the presence of other men. It lived in physical confidence, in friendship, in admiration, in flirtation, in shared ambition, and in the anticipation of seeing someone again tomorrow. It was not a single mood and it did not need to be reduced to a single image. It was a social weather, changing with the hour and the people who entered the room. The residence did not need to manufacture these feelings. It needed to give them somewhere to grow, and to make that growth visible in the life of the building.

By midnight, the western windows reflected the warm interior lights against the blackness of the trees. A few residents were still talking downstairs. Others had gone to bed, already making plans for the morning. The building continued to hold all of them, their separate lives gradually becoming part of a common history. Somewhere on the upper floors, another door opened and someone called down to ask whether anyone was still awake.

Several people answered at once.
