# Chapter XLIII — The Movement Between Frames

The next morning, the coach arrived before the screens had warmed. He found the contact sheet still open on the side monitor, the selected frame enlarged in the centre, and the notebook lying where it had been left the previous evening. The crossed-out measurements were still visible. Beside them, someone had written three questions in a firmer hand: what turns, what translates, and what only appears to change because the camera has moved? The questions had been left overnight without answers. That was useful. They had become the first thing the room could work on.

He put his coffee on the far end of the table, well clear of the keyboard, and studied the sequence. The source had been divided into a short interval, not because the rest of the clip was unimportant but because this was the portion where the shoulder seemed to change its relation to the chest. At one point the outline widened. A few frames later the near arm occupied more of the image. The head remained recognisable, but the neck angle shifted and the line of the collar moved against the background. Seen quickly, the change looked like a simple increase in breadth. Seen slowly, it was a negotiation among rotation, depth, camera perspective, and the ordinary compression of a moving body.

The coach leaned back. “There are at least two things happening.”

The person at the desk brought the sequence to the beginning. “The torso turns.”

“Probably. And?”

“The camera moves a little to the left.”

“Good. So don't give the rig all the work. The camera has to move too.”

The answer was modest, but it changed the construction. Until then, the rough model had been arranged as though the camera were fixed and the body were responsible for every difference in the silhouette. That assumption had made the shoulder too complicated. It had encouraged the modeller to pull the deltoid outward, enlarge the upper back, and adjust the rib cage until the later frame could be forced into place. The result could match one still, but it would not explain the intervening frames. It was a solution to the wrong problem.

They opened the source properties and checked the dimensions again. The original remained in its protected location. The working proxy had a separate name, a recorded relation to the source, and a clear note that it had been slowed for inspection. They verified the frame rate, duration, aspect ratio, and the precise interval selected for the test. The measurements in the notebook were not transferred as unquestioned facts. Each one was attached to the frame from which it had been estimated, and the estimates that could not be repeated were marked as uncertain. A measurement without a frame reference was only a number. A measurement with its source, method, and confidence attached could become useful evidence.

The coach watched the process without interrupting. He was not impatient with careful work, but he had little tolerance for ritual. When the sequence was ready, he pulled the chair close and asked for the first and last frames to be shown side by side. The shoulder in the first image seemed narrower. The shoulder in the last seemed broader. The difference was obvious enough to invite a quick conclusion, which was exactly why he did not accept it.

“Now put the middle frames back.”

The images returned to the screen, one after another. The body turned. The near arm came forward. The far shoulder disappeared behind the line of the neck, then emerged again as the camera shifted. The chest rotated beneath the head. The collar line changed angle. What had looked like a single expansion was a compound movement, and the apparent increase in width came from several smaller relations becoming visible at once.

“There,” the coach said. “That's the event. Don't sculpt the answer into the first frame. Build the thing that can travel through all of them.”

The first structural pass was made from separate parts. The torso remained a central volume. The shoulder girdle was not fused into it. The upper arms could rotate independently. The neck and head retained their own axes, and the camera was placed on a track whose movement could be adjusted without altering the body. The rig was deliberately plain. No skin texture, no elaborate materials, no decorative surface detail. The model had the proportions needed to ask the question and no more.

The person at the desk set the rig into the first frame. The silhouette was not perfect, but it was coherent. The shoulder sat where it should. The arm had room to move. The head and chest agreed about the direction of the body. When the camera track was advanced by a small amount, the background shifted in a way that resembled the source. The rig was not yet reproducing the clip, but it had stopped fighting the evidence.

For a while they worked quietly. The room filled with the familiar sounds of a production morning: a key pressed twice because the first press had not registered, the small click of the mouse wheel, a drawer opening, the wheels of a chair crossing the floor seam, the kettle beginning in the adjoining room. Outside, a delivery truck idled and then moved on. The screen light reflected in the window, placing the moving sequence over the pale outline of the yard. It was a strange double exposure, the workshop and the source occupying the same pane of glass, but nobody mentioned it. The sequence needed attention.

The first test failed at the fourth frame.

The near shoulder moved too quickly. The upper arm appeared to slide through the torso, and the chest seemed to turn independently of the spine. The error was small in duration but large in consequence. If they allowed it to remain, the later frames would require a compensating adjustment, and the next adjustment would require another. The rig would accumulate corrections until nobody could say which element was responsible for the movement. They stopped the test and returned to the frame where the error began.

The coach pointed at the junction between the upper arm and the shoulder.

“You've made the joint do the whole turn.”

“It needs more range?”

“It needs the shoulder blade to have somewhere to go. The joint isn't a hinge stuck into a wall. The whole arrangement moves.”

They separated the shoulder blade from the simplified rib cage and gave it a controlled path. The change was not an anatomical dissertation. It was a practical recognition that the visible shoulder was produced by several connected structures, not a single ball attached to a torso. They added only enough control to represent the observed motion. The model did not need every detail of a real body to make this pass. It needed the correct relationships and a way to reveal where those relationships were still inadequate.

They ran the four frames again. The arm no longer cut through the torso. The shoulder travelled, the upper back followed, and the chest turned beneath the neck. The movement still lacked the small settling motion visible in the source, but the principal transition held together. The coach nodded once.

“Keep that.”

The instruction mattered because the earlier version would not be deleted from history. It was retained as a test that had shown the cost of making one joint carry an entire movement. The next version was derived from it, with a note about what had changed. The failed pass became evidence. Someone opening the project in a month would be able to understand why the shoulder control existed and why it had been separated from the torso. The archive did not need a dramatic account of every correction. It needed enough truth that the correction could be inherited.

By midday, the first complete movement test was ready. It lasted only a few seconds. The model turned, the camera shifted, the near arm advanced, and the shoulder girdle followed. The materials were still a neutral grey. The environment was a simple plane with a few vertical markers placed at the positions of visible background features. The result was not handsome, but it was legible. When the coach watched it, he did not look at the model alone. He watched the spaces between the body and the markers, the way the silhouette crossed the background, the angle of the head, and the moment the far shoulder became visible again.

“Run the source beside it.”

The two sequences played together. Their timing was close. Their shapes diverged at the sixth frame.

The coach did not immediately say what was wrong. He watched the interval again, then asked for the playback to stop at the exact point where the divergence began. The model's chest rotated too far. The camera had been moved correctly, but the body had been asked to compensate for a perspective change that the camera already explained. The result was a familiar production error: two systems independently solving the same difference. Each was reasonable on its own. Together they exaggerated the movement.

The person at the desk adjusted the camera track by a fraction and returned the torso closer to its previous orientation. The comparison improved. The head still moved too abruptly, however, and the collar line remained a little too level. They adjusted the neck axis, not to make the model more expressive, but to preserve the relationship between head, neck, and chest as the body turned. Then they checked the next frame, and the next.

It was slower than simply forcing the final pose into place. It was also faster than spending the afternoon repairing a model built on an assumption that could not survive inspection.

The coach took his coffee from the far end of the table. It had gone cold. He drank it anyway, then stood and moved to the window. The yard had brightened. A man was unloading lengths of timber from a van across the road, carrying them one at a time and leaning them against a fence. The coach watched for a moment, then returned to the screen.

“That's the difference between carrying a thing and showing how it gets carried,” he said. “The weight has to go somewhere. The rest of the body answers it.”

The remark sent them back to the source, where the transfer of weight was visible in details that had seemed secondary at first: the near foot settling, the pelvis shifting, the chest following a fraction later. The shoulder was not the beginning of the movement. It was one of the places where the movement became easiest to see. The actual sequence began lower, with a slight change in support. By the time the shoulder broadened in the frame, the body had already started to turn.

They extended the inspection window backwards by several frames. The earlier interval changed the interpretation again. The apparent shoulder expansion was no longer the first event. It was a consequence of a weight transfer that began at the foot and travelled through the pelvis and torso. The sequence had been telling them this from the beginning. They had been looking at the most visually obvious part rather than the earliest meaningful part.

The coach came back to the table. “Start earlier.”

They did. The model now included the foot contact and a simplified pelvis control. The first movement was small: a shift of balance, almost easy to miss. The pelvis followed, then the torso. The shoulder arrived later, and the camera completed its own passage alongside the turn. The resulting motion had more continuity. It also made the character feel less like a collection of parts being adjusted by a technician and more like a person whose balance, reach, and attention belonged to one another.

That was the first moment the room became quiet for a different reason. The test was still unfinished, yet something had crossed a threshold. It was possible to watch the figure and understand what it was doing. The movement did not merely resemble the source at a few selected points. It had a cause that could be followed.

They let it play again.

This time the coach watched the whole interval without stopping. When it ended, he moved the chair beside the desk and sat down. He asked for the source to be hidden and the model played alone. Then he asked for the model to be hidden and the source played alone. The two versions were not identical, but both retained the same basic event. When they were placed together again, the differences became specific enough to work on.

They made a comparison sheet. Each row held a reference frame, the corresponding test frame, the estimated body state, the camera state, and a short note about the remaining discrepancy. The sheet did not pronounce the model correct or incorrect as a whole. It gave each mismatch a location. The shoulder was still slightly too high in the final turn. The pelvis began its movement too early. The camera drift was close but not exact. The head settled a fraction too soon. Those were four different problems, and they would be treated as four different problems.

The coach read the notes. “Good. Now we know what we're asking.”

A meal arrived in the middle of the afternoon. Nobody had arranged a formal break, but one of the men from the adjoining room brought a tray and set it between the notebook and the monitor. There were sandwiches, cut fruit, a bowl of crisps, and a large jug of water. The coach shifted the comparison sheet to make room. The person at the desk closed the playback window and stood, stretching their back after hours in the chair.

The men ate in the workshop. They talked about a camera lens that had been difficult to source, a set of shelves that needed reinforcement, and the possibility of moving the larger monitor to the wall so that several people could study the same frame without crowding around the desk. The conversation wandered, as it should. The work did not need every minute of the day, and the people doing it did not need to become extensions of the software. By the time the tray was empty, the next practical change had become obvious: the comparison needed a larger shared display, while the detailed controls could remain at the desk.

Two men measured the wall. Another went to find the mounting hardware. The coach helped move the monitor stand, testing the base before allowing the display to be lifted. They worked without making the task ceremonial. The monitor went up, the cables were routed, and the source and reconstruction could now be seen together from the centre of the room. The small screen remained available for the timeline and rig controls. The workshop had changed its arrangement because the work had changed its needs.

When they returned to the sequence, the larger display revealed another issue. The model's apparent accuracy at desk distance had concealed a difference in the full silhouette. The far arm emerged too soon. The shoulder line was close, but the elbow angle made the figure feel as though it were turning faster than the source. The discrepancy had been difficult to see while concentrating on the joint. On the wall, the entire movement was visible at once.

The coach stood beneath the screen, arms folded. “That's why we don't polish the close-up before we check the whole figure.”

They adjusted the elbow and tested the complete interval again. The model now held the movement more convincingly, although its surfaces remained plain. A temptation arose to add detail: fabric folds, skin response, light, a more elaborate face. The person at the desk opened the material panel, then paused.

“Not yet,” the coach said.

He was not opposed to detail. He was protecting the order of operations. The head needed to remain recognisable through the turn. The body proportions needed to stay consistent. The camera and rig needed to agree. Surface work would be valuable once those foundations had become stable, but it could not be allowed to distract from a motion that was still being solved.

They saved the state as a new working version. The source remained untouched; the proxy remained a proxy; the rig, camera, comparison sheet, and render all carried references to the same test interval. The new version was not labelled final. It was labelled as a structural pass with movement continuity improved and four known discrepancies still open. The distinction was visible in the project itself. Nobody would mistake the test for a completed reconstruction.

At the end of the afternoon, the coach asked them to play the sequence once more, beginning several frames before the shoulder moved. The figure shifted its balance. The pelvis followed. The chest began to turn. The shoulder travelled. The camera moved with it. The near arm came forward, the far arm changed its overlap, and the head settled after the body had found its new direction.

The movement lasted only a few seconds.

It was enough to make the room feel larger.

Not because the model had become impressive, and not because the reconstruction had reached some polished final state. The difference was that they could now see a path through the problem. Each visible change had a relation to the one before it. The next correction could be made without destroying the previous one. They had stopped trying to manufacture a convincing still image and had begun to construct a body capable of moving through time.

The coach watched the final frame and then looked at the first. “Tomorrow we test the turn from the other side.”

The person at the desk glanced at the comparison sheet. “That means another camera position.”

“And the far shoulder. And the back. And what happens when the arm crosses the chest.”

He said it as a list of ordinary tasks, but the scope of the work had changed. The first test had been an isolated transition. The second would have to establish whether the same rig could carry the movement from another view without being rebuilt to suit it. If it could, the model would begin to hold as a continuous structure. If it could not, the failure would tell them where the missing relationship remained.

They closed the source playback and left the wall monitor on the first reference frame. The frame no longer looked like a finished picture. It looked like the beginning of a movement whose consequences could be followed. Beneath it, the comparison sheet remained open, with the four discrepancies and the next test clearly recorded.

Outside, the man across the road had finished unloading the timber. He was carrying the last length toward the gate, one end balanced against his shoulder. The coach watched him for a moment through the window. The timber shifted. The man adjusted his stance, moved his hand, and brought the load through the gate without striking the post. His body made a small series of corrections that were too ordinary to attract attention unless someone was looking for them.

The coach turned back to the screen.

“That's what we're after,” he said. “Not a shape that happens to match. A shape that knows how to get there.”

The workshop lights stayed on while the room emptied. The new monitor held the frame. The desk held the rig. The notebook held the questions, now accompanied by a record of what had been learned. Nothing had been declared finished. Nothing had been lost. In the project, the failed test remained available beside the improved one; the source remained where it had always been; and the next camera position had become a real task rather than a vague intention.

The following morning, they would turn the figure away from the camera. They would have to account for the back, the far shoulder, the arm crossing the chest, and the changing distance between the body and the background. They would discover whether the same proportions could survive a new view, or whether the first pass had only learned to imitate one fortunate angle.

The room had been prepared for that question.

And now the work could ask it.
