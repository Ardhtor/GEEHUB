# Chapter XXIII: The Seven Rules

By morning they had stopped arguing about whether the Serum was alive.

The argument had become irrelevant. They had spent most of the night beside the open chest, observing the pale substance and the way its light moved through the clearing. It had not spoken, threatened them, or asked to be worshipped. It had not behaved like an ordinary liquid either. It had altered its vessel, the ground beneath the oak, and the bodies of the seven men without producing a single event that could be separated neatly from the others. The oldest of them had eventually said that the question of life was too small for what they had witnessed. The youngest had objected that a thing could be alive without being understood. The seventh man had let them argue until the fire was nearly cold, then asked what they could actually say with confidence.

By dawn, the question had changed. What mattered was that the Serum behaved consistently.

The seven men returned to the chamber beyond the clearing. The chest had remained open, and the substance inside it had settled into a slow, luminous movement. They carried the vessel to a broad table in the room, although the chest itself seemed to grow enough to accommodate the movement. Nobody wanted to separate the Serum from the object in which they had found it, but they wanted a place where they could write, compare, and test what had happened. The room's walls were pale stone, and the damp had left irregular dark patches near the floor. A shallow shelf ran along one side. There was chalk, a slate, a length of cord, a measuring stick, and the map that had survived the journey through the woods.

The largest man stood beside the table. His shirt had become tight across the chest, and the fabric no longer rested naturally over his shoulders. When he leaned forward, the seams pulled; when he straightened, they eased again. He had spent the previous day treating the change as a discomfort that might pass. Now he could no longer separate the discomfort from the larger event. The youngest man had begun to carry his pack differently. The map-carrier's fingers sat more heavily around the edges of the paper. The man with the compass had noticed that his stride had lengthened. The seventh man seemed unchanged at first glance, but the others had begun to suspect that he simply noticed changes before they were visible.

The seven men gathered around the vessel and began writing observations on the walls.

The first rule was written by the seventh man. He took the chalk and drew a short line beneath the heading, then stepped aside so the others could read it.

**RULE ONE: THE SERUM AMPLIFIES WHAT IS ALREADY PRESENT.**

It did not invent a new person. It did not replace one state with another. It found an existing tendency and increased it.

Strength became greater strength.

A room became more room.

A memory became more persistent.

A connection became more connected.

A desire became a direction.

The youngest man read the lines and looked toward the largest man.

“Does that mean it only increases what was already there?”

“That's what the rule says,” the seventh man replied.

“Then what about the room? It wasn't larger before.”

“The capacity for a room to become larger was already present.”

The compass-carrier frowned. “That sounds like a way of making the rule impossible to disprove.”

The seventh man gave him the chalk. “Then test it.”

They began with the simplest thing they could find: the folded map. The map-carrier unfolded it on the table and placed a stone at the point where they had entered the forest. The paper had been softened by moisture, and the ink had blurred along one fold. He showed the others how the old route had been drawn, then traced the path they had actually taken. The two lines did not match. Some of the difference could be explained by memory, but the map had also changed. A ridge was missing. The stream was drawn in the wrong direction. The clearing occupied a place that should have been an open slope.

The youngest man said that the map had not been a very good map to begin with.

The map-carrier looked at him. “It was good enough to get us here.”

“Not good enough to get us back.”

“That is a different test.”

They argued for a moment, then placed the two versions side by side. The map had already been a record of travel, and the Serum had made its relationship to the journey more visible. It did not provide a perfect route. It amplified the difference between the intended passage and the one they had actually travelled. The map-carrier did not like the result, but he wrote it down.

The second rule was written beneath the first.

**RULE TWO: NO ESTABLISHED CHANGE IS ERASED.**

The old state remained part of the new state.

A person who had crossed a threshold still contained the person who had stood before it.

A house that had expanded still remembered its original walls.

A road that had divided still contained the first road.

The Serum did not overwrite.

It accumulated.

The men considered this rule more carefully than the first. The largest man looked at his shirt. Its original seams remained visible, although the body beneath it had changed. The youngest man took off one glove and placed it on the table. His hand no longer filled it in the same way, but the glove still held the shape of his earlier fingers. The map-carrier opened the notebook to the first measurements he had recorded. The numbers had not disappeared. They were simply no longer current.

The oldest man took the glove and folded it carefully.

“Would you keep it?” the youngest asked.

“Yes.”

“Even if it never fits again?”

“Especially if it never fits again. It tells us what it did fit.”

The youngest took the glove back. He had not expected that answer. He placed it beside the notebook rather than returning it to his pack. The men understood that the old state was not a competing version of the present. It remained part of the same history. A man could outgrow a shirt without making the shirt meaningless. A room could widen without making its first walls a mistake. A road could branch without losing the path from which it had emerged.

The third rule required a diagram.

The seventh man drew seven circles, each slightly larger than the last, and connected them with a line. He asked the others to describe the stages they had observed. The first circle represented the beginning. The second represented the first established change. The third represented the next change measured from the second. Then another. Then another. The distance between the circles did not necessarily remain constant. Some thresholds had arrived quickly; others had taken most of a day. Their changes had not been uniform across the body, and the environment had not changed at the same rate as the men.

**RULE THREE: EVERY CHANGE CREATES A NEW BASELINE.**

The largest man looked at the circles.

“So the first measurement doesn't disappear.”

“No,” said the map-carrier. “But it stops being the one we compare against.”

The youngest man pointed to the second circle. “Then each new one becomes the beginning of another sequence.”

The seventh man nodded.

They used the measuring stick to compare the largest man's current shoulder width with a mark he had made on the previous evening. The mark remained visible. It did not change when the new measurement was written beside it. The difference was not huge, but it was consistent. The largest man stood with his arms relaxed and allowed the others to take the reading. He did not enjoy being measured in front of the group, but he understood why they were doing it. The others took turns. The youngest man had broadened through the hands and chest; the map-carrier had gained enough across the shoulders that his pack straps sat differently; the compass-carrier's stride had changed, though the measurement of his chest had not increased as much as the others.

The seventh man marked the current readings and drew a second line beneath them. The men now had two sets of measurements. Neither had been erased. The new baseline had not made the old one false. It had made the next comparison possible.

Then came the fourth rule.

**RULE FOUR: THE SERUM COMPOUNDS.**

Each completed transformation altered the conditions for the next one.

The larger body required a larger room.

The larger room permitted larger movement.

The larger movement created new thresholds.

The new thresholds gave the Serum something else to amplify.

This was why acceleration appeared.

The Serum was not becoming impatient.

The system was becoming capable of more.

The men did not accept the rule simply because it sounded plausible. They tested it with the room. The largest man stood beside the table, then walked from one wall to the other. His shoulders passed close to a stone projection near the doorway. He had to turn slightly to avoid touching it. The room was not yet too small, but it was no longer generous. The younger man moved the table a few inches away from the wall so that the largest man could turn without catching his arm. The movement opened a little more floor space. The largest man walked the same path again. The room had not grown, but his movement had become easier.

The compass-carrier watched the passage. “The room permits more movement when the table moves.”

“And more movement makes another threshold possible,” the map-carrier said.

“Only if the body changes again,” the youngest replied.

The seventh man marked the sequence on the wall. They were beginning to see that one change could alter the conditions surrounding another. A wider chest might require a larger doorway. A wider doorway might make it possible to carry a longer piece of timber. The timber might become a beam for a larger room. A larger room might allow the men to train together in a different way. The next threshold did not appear from nowhere. It emerged from the conditions created by the one before it.

The fifth rule concerned thresholds.

**RULE FIVE: CHANGE OCCURS WHEN A THRESHOLD IS CROSSED.**

The Serum did not respond continuously to every passing second.

It responded when something became meaningfully different.

A door became too small.

A corridor became too narrow.

A body crossed a measured proportion.

A memory became stable enough to survive independently.

A relationship became strong enough to alter the behaviour of two people at once.

At that point the Serum recognised the new condition.

Then it established it.

The men tested the idea with the doorway. They placed the measuring stick at the upper frame and asked the largest man to walk through at his ordinary pace. He cleared it. Then he carried a long branch from the chest's woodpile. The branch passed through only when he turned his shoulders and angled the end. The doorway had not become too small for his body, but it had become restrictive for a task he wanted to perform. That was a different threshold.

The youngest man tried the same movement. He was narrower, but the branch caught against the frame because he held it too high. The compass-carrier showed him how to lower one end. The branch passed through. The two men exchanged places and repeated the test. The outcome depended on body, object, angle, and movement. The doorway was not simply adequate or inadequate. It was adequate for one action and restrictive for another.

The seventh man added another note beneath the rule. A threshold was not always a dimension. Sometimes it was the point at which a familiar action became difficult, or a relationship became capable of changing what two men could do together. The men considered that last example. The largest man remembered how the youngest had steadied him at the stream. He had not needed the help, but the act had changed the way they walked afterward. They had begun to watch for one another rather than only for the path.

They tested the relational threshold with the long branch they had carried through the doorway. The largest man took one end; the youngest took the other. At first they moved at different speeds. The branch turned, its upper end striking the frame. The youngest stepped back, and the largest man stopped before the movement could pull him off balance. They looked at one another.

“Again,” the youngest said.

This time they agreed on the turn before lifting. The largest man counted the first movement, the youngest took the second, and both lowered the branch at the same moment. It passed through the doorway without touching the frame. They repeated the action with the compass-carrier walking ahead to clear the path. The third attempt was smoother still. Each man had changed his movement in response to the others, and the task had become possible because they had learned a common rhythm.

The seventh man watched them. “The threshold wasn't in either man's strength.”

“No,” the map-carrier said. “It was in the way they moved together.”

The largest man set the branch down. “We could have carried it through alone if we'd taken it apart.”

“But then it wouldn't have been the same task,” the youngest replied.

They considered this. The Serum had been changing individual bodies, but the men were discovering that a stable condition could also arise between people. The shared movement had become repeatable. It could be remembered, taught, and used again. Their relationship had altered the range of what they could do together, just as the widened doorway altered what could pass through the room. The men did not claim that this proved the Serum was measuring affection or trust. They recorded only what they had observed: a coordinated action had become possible after the men changed the way they attended to one another.

The seventh man added a short note beneath Rule Five: **SOME THRESHOLDS ARE REACHED THROUGH COORDINATION.** The line did not replace the rule. It made its implications harder to ignore.

The sixth rule was the one that frightened them.

**RULE SIX: THE ENVIRONMENT MUST ACCOMMODATE A STABLE CHANGE.**

They tested it with a chair.

The largest man sat down. The chair widened.

They tested it with a doorway.

He approached.

The frame rose.

They tested it with the room itself.

He stood.

The walls moved.

Nobody touched them.

The building simply incorporated the new measurement.

The men did not treat the changes as an invitation to test every object in the chamber. They watched carefully, then moved the chair back to its place. The largest man stood again. The seat remained wider. The doorway remained higher. The room retained the new dimensions. They could not make the changes reverse by asking them to. The environment had accepted a stable condition and incorporated it.

The compass-carrier was troubled by this. He wanted to know whether the room could keep expanding forever, whether the floor would remain strong, and whether the walls would remember their original arrangement. The seventh man replied that the rule did not say every structure could support every change. It said the environment had to accommodate a stable one. The distinction mattered. A change that could not be supported might remain incomplete, or it might require a different place.

The largest man walked toward the doorway again. He was now able to pass through without turning. He stopped on the other side and looked back at the men. The room seemed to have made space for him, but it had not become an empty hall. The table, the chair, the vessel, and the other men remained where they belonged. Accommodation had not meant erasure. It had meant a changed arrangement in which the same place could continue to be used.

The seventh rule took the longest to write. The seventh man finally took the chalk.

**RULE SEVEN: THE SERUM SEEKS THE NEXT STABLE STATE.**

Not the largest possible state.

Not the fastest state.

The next state that could hold.

That distinction changed everything.

Growth without stability collapsed.

Growth that could be supported became permanent.

The Serum therefore pushed toward thresholds that could become architecture.

Body into room.

Room into house.

House into city.

City into world.

World into branch.

Branch into history.

The men stared at the seven rules. The room had become a little larger, the table had been shifted, the chair had widened, and their bodies had each taken on a new proportion. None of the changes looked like a final form. The vessel continued its slow movement. The seventh man set the chalk down and looked at the others, waiting for someone to challenge the rule.

The compass-carrier spoke first.

“How do we know it seeks stability rather than simply stopping when it has done enough?”

“We don't,” the seventh man said. “Not yet.”

“Then why write it as a rule?”

“Because every change we've observed has either held or required another accommodation. We have not seen a stable change collapse.”

“That doesn't prove the next one will hold.”

“No,” said the seventh man. “It tells us what we should test.”

The largest man looked toward the vessel. “And what if the next state is too large for the place?”

“Then the place must change, or the state is not stable.”

The youngest man frowned. “That sounds as though the Serum can always make another room.”

“It may be able to,” said the map-carrier. “That doesn't mean we should assume it will.”

The room became quiet. The seven men understood that the rule described a direction, not a guarantee. The Serum might seek a state that could hold, but the men still had to learn what support required. A body could become larger before its balance adapted. A room could widen before its furniture was rearranged. A road could extend before anyone had surveyed the ground. Stability was not the same as size. It was the ability to continue without losing the conditions that made the change livable.

Then the largest man pointed toward the vessel.

“There is one more.”

Nobody had written it.

The Serum was already moving. He watched the liquid climb the glass. The light gathered at its upper edge, then travelled downward in a pale band. It did not overflow. The vessel seemed to widen slightly where the liquid pressed against it.

“The rules describe what it does,” he said. “They don't describe what it wants.”

The room became quiet.

The seventh man looked at the vessel.

“No,” he said. “They describe exactly what it wants.”

He touched the first rule.

“More of what exists.”

The second.

“Nothing lost.”

The third.

“New baseline.”

The fourth.

“Compounding.”

The fifth.

“Threshold.”

The sixth.

“Accommodation.”

The seventh.

“Stability.”

He turned toward the others.

“It doesn't need a desire.”

The Serum brightened.

“It has a method.”

For several seconds nothing happened. The men stood around the table, reading the seven rules in the light from the vessel. Their clothes had changed. The room had changed. The relationships among them had changed through the work of the morning. Each man had become a witness to the others, and the record on the wall now belonged to all seven of them. The rules did not explain everything. They gave the men a way to continue asking without pretending they had already reached the answer.

Then the vessel became too large for the table.

The table widened.

The room widened.

The building widened.

Far below them, the streets of the city shifted by a fraction to accommodate a structure that had not existed when they woke that morning.

The change began at the table's legs. They shifted outward, then settled into a wider stance. The vessel's base remained level. The map-carrier caught the notebook before it slid, and the youngest man moved the measuring stick away from the edge. The compass-carrier watched the doorway while the largest man stepped back to keep clear of the widening wall. The seventh man did not reach for the vessel. He watched the room itself, looking for the point where the new dimensions stopped changing.

The room gained a little more depth. The table moved with it, leaving the same distance between its edge and the wall. The doorway rose and widened. The floor remained level beneath their feet. One of the men tested the boards with a careful step, then looked to the others. There was no cracking, no loose stone, and no sudden shift in weight. The new arrangement held.

“That's the test,” the youngest said.

“What is?” asked the map-carrier.

“Whether we can still use it.”

The largest man walked to the doorway and back. The compass-carrier followed with the measuring stick. They checked the same route they had used before the change. The space was larger, but the table remained within reach, the vessel remained accessible, and the doorway still opened onto the road. The room had not simply become bigger. It had preserved its function while changing its dimensions.

The seventh man looked at the seven rules on the wall. “A stable state is one you can live in.”

The others looked at him.

“Not just one that stays still,” he continued. “One that lets us keep doing the things we came here to do.”

The largest man nodded. “Then we need to know what the room is for before we decide what it needs to become.”

No one contradicted him. They had been measuring the room as a container, but it was also a place where they gathered, wrote, ate, and made decisions. A larger room that broke those relations would not be an improvement. The rule of accommodation had to include use.

Nobody panicked.

They had finally learned how to read the change. The table did not break. The floor did not buckle. The walls moved with the vessel, and the doorway retained enough clearance for the men to pass through. The map-carrier reached for the notebook before it could slide toward the table's new edge. The youngest took the measuring stick and held it against the wall. The compass-carrier checked the doorway. The largest man stood back to allow the others room. The seventh man watched the vessel.

The Serum had not begun a new event.

It had completed a rule.

The largest man looked toward the opening doorway. Beyond it lay another road. The road had not been there yesterday. Its surface was dark and smooth, and it extended into the trees beyond the building. It did not seem to lead back to the clearing. It seemed to lead farther into the country.

“Where does that go?” someone asked.

The seventh man smiled.

“Next baseline.”

The youngest man picked up his pack. The map-carrier folded the old map and placed it inside the dry cloth. The compass-carrier checked the direction, then looked at the seventh man. The largest man waited until everyone was ready. They did not leave the seven rules behind. The words remained on the wall, and the vessel remained on the widened table. They had written them to understand what had already happened, but the rules would continue to be tested as the world changed around them.

Before leaving, the map-carrier folded the old route and kept it beside the new one. The youngest picked up the gloves. The compass-carrier checked the first turn. The largest man waited until all six were ready, and the seventh man stepped through the doorway last, looking once at the rules on the wall. They had not solved the Serum. They had learned how to continue without pretending they had.
 
The seven rules remained behind them, not as a finished explanation, but as the first account they could carry forward together.

And they followed the road.
