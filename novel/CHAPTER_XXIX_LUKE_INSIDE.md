# CHAPTER XXIX — LUKE INSIDE

That evening Luke disappeared for eleven minutes.

There was no alarm, no flash of light, and no dramatic change in the woods. The clearing remained where they had left it. The two families had gone back toward their separate camps, and the marked line in the soil had been left undisturbed beneath the redwoods. The metal case had been secured. The instruments were packed. The last voices travelled through the trees and faded. Luke had been beside me when we started back, but at the bend where the path passed between two enormous trunks he slowed, looked toward the lower ground, and then continued in a direction that seemed to have no particular destination. I thought he wanted a moment alone. When I reached the camp, he was not there.

I waited. The first few minutes passed without concern. Luke sometimes walked ahead when he needed to think, and he had become better at recognising when a question required quiet rather than another conversation. The forest was full of shallow routes and small clearings. A man could move a little way beyond the camp and remain within hearing distance without being visible. I checked the path, then the water containers, then the place where we had put the spare coats. Nothing suggested an emergency. Still, the interval grew longer. After eleven minutes, I went looking.

The route descended through a stand of trees where the ground was softer and the air colder. Fallen leaves had gathered along the edges of the path, darkened by rain and pressed flat by the passage of feet. The trunks stood close together, their roots lifting the soil in low ridges. Beyond them the land dipped toward a pool of dark water. I heard no movement as I approached. The pool was almost perfectly still, held inside a shallow basin of stone and earth. Its surface reflected the branches above it with such clarity that the dark water seemed to continue upward into another forest.

Luke stood beside it.

He had removed his shirt and folded it over one arm. His shoulders were bare in the cool air, broad enough that the shape of his upper back changed the line of his silhouette against the water. The light was low, but I could see the familiar proportions: the slope from neck to shoulder, the depth of his chest, the long line of the upper arm. He was not posing. His hands rested loosely at his sides, and his attention was fixed on the reflection.

“I'm inside it,” he said.

I stopped behind him.

“The Serum?”

He nodded.

“Not around me. Not on me. Inside.”

The phrase should have sounded impossible, but by then impossibility had become a poor measure of certainty. I looked at the pool. The water remained still. Luke's reflected body stood at the same distance from the edge, though something about the image made the relationship between the two figures feel wrong. The reflection seemed to have more depth than the water could contain. It was not simply a second Luke appearing beneath the surface. It was as though the pool had become a view into a place that happened to contain him.

Luke put one hand against his chest. He did it slowly, with the attention of a person testing a change rather than demonstrating it. His palm rested over the sternum. The skin beneath it moved with his breathing. His chest rose, settled, and rose again. The muscles did not hold a fixed pose; they responded to the movement of air and the small adjustments of balance that kept him upright.

“It's not changing me from the outside,” he said.

He pressed his palm flat and waited.

“It keeps finding the place where the new version of me can already exist.”

The water reflected him a fraction of a second late.

Luke noticed.

“So did you.”

I looked at the surface. My reflection moved normally. His moved after. The delay was small enough that I might have missed it if he had not said anything. Luke raised his right hand. The reflection raised its right hand a moment later. He lowered it. The image followed, but not quite at once. There was no visible current, no wind crossing the water, no insect touching the surface. The delay remained tied to Luke and not to the reflected trees.

He stepped closer to the pool.

The second image stepped closer too.

Luke smiled without amusement. “That's what I mean.”

He shifted his weight from one foot to the other. In the reflected view, the movement arrived after the physical one, but the balance seemed different. The reflected figure settled into the posture before Luke himself had finished adjusting. He watched the difference. Then he turned his shoulders a fraction to the left. The image turned after him, but the reflected torso appeared to carry more depth. The change was subtle, not grotesque or sudden. It resembled a camera choosing a wider lens, except that the background did not distort in the same way. His shoulders became a little broader. His chest seemed deeper. The proportions remained his. The changes looked like the same person being rendered at a new scale.

Luke watched it happen.

Then he stepped back.

The reflection did not.

For a long moment neither of us moved. The physical Luke stood a pace away from the water. The reflected Luke remained near the edge, shoulders squared toward the surface, his attention fixed on something that was not visible from where we stood. There was no theatrical menace in the image. It did not reach toward us. It did not change its face. It simply remained in a position that Luke had already left.

“Luke.”

“I know.”

The image in the water turned its head toward us.

It was still him.

That was the frightening part. The face was recognisable, the angle of the jaw familiar, the set of the mouth unmistakable. The reflected shoulders and chest were larger, but they belonged to the same arrangement of body and gesture. Nothing had been substituted. The Serum was not producing an imitation. It was preserving continuity so carefully that even the impossible version remained recognisable.

Luke looked at me, then back at the water. “Don't come closer yet.”

I stopped where I was. He was not telling me to stay away because the pool was dangerous. He was trying to keep the observation intact. The distance between us, the edge of the water, the position of the reflected figure—each had become part of what he was seeing. If I stepped forward, I might interrupt it. If he moved, the image might respond. He wanted another few seconds before either of us changed the arrangement.

The reflected Luke raised one hand and placed it against the reflected chest. The gesture was almost identical to the one Luke had made earlier, but the timing was reversed. The physical Luke had his arms at his sides. In the water, the larger figure was still touching his chest. The reflection appeared to be remembering a movement that had already ended, or anticipating one that had not yet happened. The surface held both possibilities without choosing between them.

Luke crouched beside the pool. His movement was careful. His knees bent, his hips moved back, and one hand rested briefly against his thigh to steady the descent. The reflection crouched after him, but this time the delay seemed shorter. Its shoulders remained broad, and the deeper chest changed the angle at which the arm passed across the torso. Luke looked at his own hand, then at the hand in the water.

“I think everybody else is studying the Serum from outside,” he said.

“And you?”

He touched the water.

“It keeps teaching me what outside means.”

The surface closed over his fingertips. The water was cold enough that he drew a small breath. The reflected hand met his from below, though the depth of the image made the contact look less like a reflection than a second hand reaching from a great distance. For an instant the reflected woods seemed to continue downward beneath the pool, as though the entire forest had another forest underneath it. Roots descended into dark soil. Trunks continued below the surface. A pale strip of sky appeared beneath the reflected canopy. The perspective did not make physical sense, but it remained consistent long enough to be seen.

Luke pulled his hand back. The image remained for a moment, then returned to the ordinary surface. The second forest disappeared. The reflected branches settled into their familiar positions. The delay remained, though it was now so slight that it could have been overlooked.

He stood.

“Tomorrow,” he said, “they'll all want to know what happened.”

“What are you going to tell them?”

He looked at me. “The truth.”

“And that is?”

Luke glanced once more at the water. His expression had changed. The fear was still there, but it had been joined by a concentration that I recognised from the earlier days in the clearing. He had found a question he could not put down.

“I don't think the Serum is growing toward a final body.”

He turned toward the path.

“I think it's growing toward a world where that body makes sense.”

We began walking back. Luke carried his shirt folded over one arm. The pool stayed behind us, invisible once the trunks closed around the path. He did not look back. After several steps he slowed, and I matched his pace. The forest had darkened, but the way back was clear enough to follow. We could hear the distant camp: a metal cup set on a table, a low exchange between two men, the brief scrape of a chair being moved across the ground.

Luke's shoulders filled the space between the trees differently than they had in the morning. A branch that had brushed his upper arm on the way down now passed close to his back. He noticed and turned slightly. It was an ordinary adjustment, except that he had begun to understand it as part of a larger process. The body changed; the forest received the change; the movement changed again. Nothing stood alone. The space was not a container waiting passively for a person to enter it. It was part of the event.

At the edge of the camp, Luke stopped and put his shirt on. The garment had been folded carefully, but the shoulders resisted when he drew it over his arms. He eased the fabric down rather than forcing it. The seam settled a little farther outward than it had before. He looked at the cloth and shook his head.

“It's going to need another alteration.”

“Tomorrow?”

“Eventually.”

He folded the lower hem once and let it fall. The shirt was still wearable, though it would not remain so indefinitely. The men had kept earlier garments as records, but Luke had not yet decided what he wanted to preserve. He did not want every discarded shirt to become a museum piece. He wanted to remember what it had been like to wear it. The distinction was becoming important.

The others were awake around the small campfire. Tyler had the notebook open on his knee. The younger man from the second family was checking the pegs used to mark the crack in the soil. One of the older men had set the metal case beneath a dry covering. They looked up when Luke returned. Nobody spoke at once. His absence had not been long, but they had noticed that he was missing.

Tyler closed the notebook. “You found something.”

Luke sat on a low timber. The wood creaked beneath him, then settled. He did not rush to explain. He looked at the fire, at the dark line of trees beyond it, and at the men waiting for him. The pool's image remained clear in his mind, but he could already feel the difficulty of translating it into language. If he described the reflection as a second Luke, they would imagine a copy. If he called it a future version, they might treat it as a prediction. If he said that the Serum lived inside him, they might assume he meant it had become part of his anatomy in a literal and ordinary sense.

He chose the simplest account.

“The reflection moved after I did. Then it moved before I did. It was still me, but it was larger.”

Tyler looked at him. “How much larger?”

“I can't measure it from the water.”

“Did you compare it with the trees?”

“I watched the background. It didn't distort like a lens.”

Tyler wrote that down.

The older man from the first family leaned forward. “Did it speak?”

“No.”

“Did it touch you?”

Luke glanced at his fingertips. “The water did.”

The man nodded, not entirely satisfied but unwilling to press beyond what Luke had seen. The second family's representative asked whether the reflected figure had repeated the same movements. Luke said that it had, but with a delay that changed. Sometimes it followed. Sometimes it seemed to anticipate. At one point it had stayed in a position Luke had already left. That was the detail that troubled him most.

Tyler looked down at the notebook. “We should record the location and return in daylight.”

Luke shook his head. “Not yet.”

“Why?”

“Because I don't know whether the pool is showing us something we can repeat, or whether it was responding to me. I don't want a group standing around it tomorrow trying to make it happen again.”

Tyler considered the answer. “We can record the site without repeating the test.”

“Maybe. But I want to think about it first.”

The men accepted that. It was a small exercise in restraint. They had learned how easily an observation could become a performance when too many people wanted to see the same thing. The pool did not owe them a second display. Luke's experience did not need to be reproduced before it could be taken seriously. They could preserve his account, mark it as a personal observation, and leave the phenomenon unresolved.

The fire settled into low coals. The clearing beyond the camp was dark. Luke sat with his hands resting on his knees, occasionally flexing his fingers as though checking that they still answered in the ordinary way. His body felt familiar, but the reflection had given him a new relation to it. He could no longer think of growth as a simple increase in dimension. A larger body required a world that could hold it, and the world itself might be part of the process by which that body became possible.

The Serum was not merely altering the figure that other people saw. It was changing the relationship between the figure and the place in which it appeared. Perhaps that was what Luke had meant when he said it was inside him. He was not describing a substance moving through his muscles. He was describing an experience in which the boundary between body, reflection, memory, and environment had become less secure. The body remained his. The forest remained the forest. But each had begun to contain an image of the other.

When the others finally went to sleep, Luke stayed beside the fire. I sat with him for a little longer. We did not attempt to interpret every detail. The darkness had become too complete for that, and the night air carried the smell of damp leaves and ash. The men had made a place to rest beneath the trees, but beyond the ring of firelight the forest continued in every direction. Luke looked once toward the slope where the pool lay, then lowered his gaze.

“Do you think it was me?” he asked.

“The reflection?”

He nodded.

“I think it was recognisably you.”

He accepted that, though it did not answer everything. He rubbed his palms together, feeling the warmth return to his fingers. The next baseline was still unknown. The reflection had not given him a destination or a measurement. It had shown him that the process could preserve identity while changing the conditions in which identity was recognised. That was enough to make tomorrow different.

Before we went to sleep, Luke placed his folded shirt beside the blanket rather than wearing it. The fabric was still damp at the collar from the cool air. In the morning he would try it again and decide whether to alter it. There would be measurements, food, and another visit to the clearing. The families would ask what he had seen. Tyler would want to record what could be recorded. The older men would compare the account with their memories. None of that would make the pool less strange.

The water remained dark beneath the trees. Its surface held the forest in place, and somewhere beneath that image the deeper landscape might still be waiting. Luke had seen a version of himself that was not a replacement, not a stranger, and not a finished form. It was the same man in a world that had begun to make room for more of him.

He had not yet decided whether that was reassuring.

He had only decided that it was true to what he had seen.
