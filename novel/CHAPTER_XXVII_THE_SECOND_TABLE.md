# CHAPTER XXVII — THE SECOND TABLE

By noon the two families had stopped pretending that the clearing was neutral. Someone had brought a folding table from one of the vehicles, another man had found a length of canvas, and within an hour the space between the redwoods had become a temporary council chamber. The men arranged themselves around it according to old habits they had not yet admitted were habits. The older generation took the far side. The younger men stood first, then sat when the standing became uncomfortable. Luke remained with me near the center, close enough to hear both sides and far enough that nobody could mistake him for a delegate.

On the table lay the objects each family had carried into the woods: photographs, notebooks, tailor's measurements, old belts, altered shirts, a set of keys that no longer fit their original doors, and one small metal case whose surface had gone almost black with age. The second family had brought almost nothing physical. They had digital records, dates, graphs, scans, and a series of body measurements preserved with obsessive precision. They believed the body was an instrument and that every instrument deserved a proper ledger. The first family believed the opposite. They had preserved everything because they thought the objects remembered the people who had worn them.

Luke reached for a photograph.

It showed four men on a porch, each one broad-shouldered, each one unmistakably related to the others. The photograph had been taken decades apart from anything on the table beside it, yet Luke could see the same posture appearing in all four generations.

“That's the line,” he said.

An older man nodded.

“No. That's the record of the line.”

Luke looked at him.

“What's the difference?”

“The record tells you what happened. The line tells you what happens next.”

The room went quiet.

Then the metal case opened.

Inside was a single folded shirt.

It was enormous by the standards of the photograph, but ordinary by the standards of the men now standing around the table. The cloth had yellowed. The collar had been repaired twice. Someone had written a date on the inside seam.

Luke held it up.

“It fit him?”

“It was tailored for him.”

Luke spread the shirt across his chest. The garment stopped well short of his shoulders.

Nobody laughed.

The Serum had made the past physically small.

That was the first thing the families could agree on.

The second came when the man who had been measuring the younger men set his notebook on the table and drew a single line down the page.

“Two baselines,” he said. “Different origins. Similar direction.”

Luke looked at the line.

“That's not rivalry.”

The man smiled faintly.

“No. That's why you're all frightened.”

Outside, a bird crossed the clearing and disappeared behind the trees.

Luke put the shirt back into the case.

“We need to stop asking which family is bigger.”

“And ask what?”

Luke looked toward the woods.

“What the Serum is comparing.”

No one answered.

Far below them, something shifted beneath the roots.
