# CHAPTER XXVII — THE SECOND TABLE

By noon the two families had stopped pretending that the clearing was neutral. The place had seemed unclaimed when they first arrived: a broad patch of ground beneath the redwoods, level enough to stand on, open enough to see the approach from either road. But neutrality had lasted only until the first vehicle stopped beneath the western trees. Someone brought a folding table out of the back, another man found a length of canvas, and a third began clearing wet leaves from a fallen log so that it could serve as a bench. Within an hour the space between the redwoods had become a temporary council chamber. No one had formally called a meeting. The work had simply acquired the shape of one, as though the clearing had been waiting for the men to arrange themselves around a surface.

The morning rain had left the trunks dark and glossy, and beads of water gathered along the lower branches before falling at intervals onto the canvas. The air carried the smell of wet bark, crushed needles, cold soil, and the faint smoke of a fire that had been lit for coffee. The table stood on uneven ground. One leg sank into the soft earth, so a man folded a spare piece of board beneath it and pressed down until the surface held steady. The correction was ordinary, almost invisible, but it mattered. Whatever was put on the table would be compared there, and a table that rocked could make an honest measurement look uncertain.

The men arranged themselves according to old habits they had not yet admitted were habits. The older generation took the far side, not because anyone had assigned them authority but because they had arrived first and carried the records. The younger men stood at the ends, then sat when standing became uncomfortable. A few remained behind their relatives, studying the objects before they studied the faces. Luke stayed near the centre with me, close enough to hear both sides and far enough that nobody could mistake him for a delegate. He had learned to occupy a meeting without allowing the meeting to claim him. His shoulders had broadened again, and the shirt he wore had been altered at the back; a second seam ran just inside the original line, leaving the garment's history visible. When he sat, the chair creaked and he shifted his weight to keep it from rocking. He looked at the table, then at the two groups, and waited.

On the table lay the objects each family had carried into the woods: photographs, notebooks, tailor's measurements, old belts, altered shirts, a set of keys that no longer fitted their original doors, and one small metal case whose surface had gone almost black with age. The first family had arranged its objects by generation. A photograph from a porch sat beside a shirt from the same period; a belt was folded beneath a paper label; a notebook with a cracked spine had been opened to a page where an old measurement was written in careful ink. The objects were not valuable in the ordinary sense. Their value came from being retained. Each one had survived a change that might otherwise have made the earlier body difficult to imagine.

The second family had brought almost nothing physical. They had digital records, dates, graphs, scans, and a series of body measurements preserved with obsessive precision. Their records were printed on matte sheets and protected by clear sleeves. Every measurement had a date, a reference position, and an instrument note. One man carried a small calibrated device in a hard case. Another had a tablet with the same records arranged into a timeline. They believed the body was an instrument and that every instrument deserved a proper ledger. The first family believed the opposite: that the body was a life and that the things which had touched it could remember something a measurement could not. Neither side had yet said this plainly, but their arrangement on the table already made the argument visible.

Luke reached for a photograph. He asked before lifting it, and the older man closest to him nodded. The image showed four men on a porch, each one broad-shouldered, each one unmistakably related to the others. The porch rail was low against their waists; the door behind them had been extended upward, though the join in the frame was visible. One man rested his hand on the shoulder of the youngest. Another leaned against the post with a looseness that suggested the photograph had been taken after everyone had stopped posing. It was not a picture of a single exceptional body. It was a picture of a family in which the proportions had become ordinary across generations.

Luke studied their faces for a long time. He turned the photograph slightly, catching the light on the glossy surface. The men looked different from one another: different hairlines, noses, expressions, and ways of holding themselves. Yet the structure of the shoulders and upper torso repeated with a precision that was easier to see when the picture was held beside the later measurements. He placed the photograph down carefully, leaving it aligned with the edge of the table.

“That's the line,” he said.

An older man shook his head.

“No. That's the record of the line.”

Luke looked at him. “What's the difference?”

The older man rested two fingers on the photograph, not covering any of the faces. “The record tells you what happened. The line tells you what happens next.”

The words were delivered without drama. He had probably said them before, perhaps to a son or a grandson who had asked why the cabinets were full of clothes that could no longer be worn. But at the table they sounded like a claim that needed to be examined. Luke glanced toward the second family’s measurements. A line of dates ran down the page, each one joined to a set of values. The graph did not resemble the porch photograph, yet it described a related continuity: one state followed another, and the next had been measured from the previous one.

The second family’s eldest representative opened a folder. He did not dismiss the photograph. He placed a printed graph beside it and explained that the family had tracked shoulder breadth, chest depth, arm circumference, height, stride, and weight distribution across several generations. The data had been collected at consistent intervals, with older readings retained even when the measuring equipment changed. The current body was never treated as the only body. It was the latest recorded state in a sequence. He showed Luke two lines that had begun far apart and gradually converged. The pattern was not perfectly smooth. Some periods showed little change; others showed sharper increases. But the overall direction remained legible.

“That's what we have,” the man said. “A sequence.”

Luke looked at the line. “A sequence of what?”

“Of established changes.”

“Not just changes you expected?”

The man paused. “No. Only the ones we measured.”

Luke nodded. It was an important distinction. He had heard enough theories spoken with the confidence of fact. A family could remember a body one way and later discover that the memory had become exaggerated. A photograph could be affected by distance, lens, and posture. A measurement could be precise and still fail to describe how a man moved. Neither artifact carried the whole truth by itself.

The metal case remained at the centre of the table. Its edges were worn smooth, and one hinge had been repaired with a darker piece of metal. The first family had brought it in both hands, and they had placed it carefully between the photographs and the measurement sheets. No one had explained what was inside. The case seemed to have become the centre of attention by agreement rather than by declaration. Luke looked toward the man who had carried it.

“Is that part of the record?”

The man studied the case. “It's part of what we kept.”

“Can I see?”

The older man glanced toward the others. After a moment, he nodded.

The lock opened with a small click. Inside was a single folded shirt. It was enormous by the standards of the porch photograph, but ordinary by the standards of the men now standing around the table. The cloth had yellowed. The collar had been repaired twice, and the stitching around the armholes had a different colour from the original thread. A narrow label had been sewn inside the neck. Someone had written a date on it in dark ink. The fabric smelled faintly of cedar and old paper, as though it had spent years in a cabinet with the doors kept closed.

Luke lifted the shirt by its shoulders. He did not pull at the seams. He held it open and looked at the proportions: the width across the chest, the depth of the armholes, the length of the body. He spread it across his own torso. The garment stopped well short of his shoulders. The collar sat too low; the sleeves would not have reached the shape of his upper arms. It belonged to a man who had once seemed large enough to define the limits of his house, and yet the shirt now looked like an artifact from an earlier scale of life.

“It fit him?” Luke asked.

“It was tailored for him.”

Luke lowered the fabric slightly, examining the old repairs. “And you kept it.”

The man nodded. “We kept all of them.”

A younger relative spoke from behind him. “Not all. Some were damaged. Some were lost when we moved.”

The older man did not correct him. “We kept what we could.”

Luke returned the shirt to the case. Nobody laughed. The Serum had made the past physically small. That was the first thing the families could agree on. It did not make the old shirt meaningless. It made the relationship between the shirt and the present body more difficult to ignore. The garment had not failed. It had served its time, carried the shape of one man through his days, and remained as evidence after the man had moved beyond it.

The second family’s representative turned to his measurement sheets. “We did not keep the clothing. We kept the values.”

“And what did you lose?” Luke asked.

The man looked at him. It was not a hostile question, but it was not easy.

“The feeling of it,” he said after a moment. “The fabric. The way a sleeve changed. The fact that a man could put on the same shirt and know that it had been part of him for years. We thought the numbers would be enough.”

“Were they?”

“No.”

The man ran his thumb along the edge of the folder. “They tell us how much. They don't tell us what it was like to live through it.”

The first family listened. One of the younger men looked down at the table, perhaps surprised to hear the other side admit a loss. The distinction had not dissolved the disagreement, but it had changed its temperature. Their doctrines were not simply correct and incorrect versions of one idea. Each had protected one kind of evidence and allowed another to disappear.

The man who had been measuring the younger men set his notebook on the table and drew a single line down the page. It was not a graph. It was a simple division between two columns. At the top of one he wrote ORIGIN. At the top of the other he wrote PRESENT. Beneath them he placed two sets of measurements, one from each family. The lines were different, but their directions were similar. He placed the paper in the centre so both groups could see.

“Two baselines,” he said. “Different origins. Similar direction.”

Luke looked at the line.

“That's not rivalry.”

The man smiled faintly. “No. That's why you're all frightened.”

No one answered. The sentence did not accuse either family of cowardice. It named what had been hidden beneath the comparisons. If the two lines were moving in similar directions, then the rivalry had no obvious endpoint. It could not be resolved by finding which family had started larger or which had reached a threshold first. Each new measurement would create another comparison. Each comparison would produce another reason to continue. The body could become an argument that never finished because the conditions kept changing.

Outside the table's narrow circle, a redwood released a slow fall of water from one of its upper branches. The drops struck the canvas and ran toward the edge. A man rose to shift one of the supports so the table would not collect water beneath the papers. Another held the graph while he did it. The small act brought the men close together. Their shoulders nearly touched. One moved aside, then found another man at his back. There was no collision, but the movement made them aware of how differently the clearing now received them. Their bodies had grown; their habits of spacing had not always caught up.

Luke watched the adjustment. He saw the man carrying the notebook move his arm across his chest with less clearance than he expected. The movement was not difficult, but it had changed. He looked at the others, then at the table. The meeting had begun with an argument over what to preserve. It was now revealing how each man occupied the space between them.

“We need to stop asking which family is bigger,” Luke said.

An older man raised his eyes. “And ask what?”

Luke looked toward the woods. The trunks seemed to extend upward without ending. The clearing had room for the table, the benches, and the men, but the edges were no longer as far from the group as they had seemed when they arrived. He could feel his own shoulders against the shirt, the fabric resting more firmly across his back. He could remember how the same garment had fit weeks earlier. The measurement was not only on the page. It was in his reach, his balance, and the amount of room he needed to turn without brushing the person beside him.

“What the Serum is comparing.”

No one answered at first. The words shifted the subject away from a contest between family histories and toward the mechanism that had made those histories visible. The Serum might be comparing bodies, but it might also be comparing the conditions around them: the clothing that fitted, the furniture that held, the rooms that accommodated, the way men stood together, and the expectations they brought into the same space. The families had assumed that they were the subjects of the comparison. Perhaps they were also part of its environment.

The man with the measuring notebook looked down at the two columns. “We can compare the current measurements again.”

“We can,” Luke said. “But don't make the measurement answer a question it wasn't designed to answer.”

The man nodded and wrote that down. He was beginning to understand the value of a limit. A number could establish a dimension. It could not tell him whether the two families were destined to compete, whether one lineage had inherited a greater capacity for change, or whether the Serum was responding to the fact that they had come together. Those questions would require more than a graph. They would require observation over time, and the men would have to take care not to manufacture the result they expected.

The first family began to gather its photographs again, but Luke asked them to leave the shirt case open. He wanted the older garment to remain beside the latest measurements for a little longer. The men agreed. An old shirt and a current measurement belonged on the same table, not because they could be made equivalent, but because each described a different part of the same history. One remembered the body through use. The other described it through a repeatable observation. The two forms of evidence could correct one another.

The second family placed its tablet beside the case. On the screen, a sequence of measurements moved across several years. The older man from the first family placed a photograph beside it. The contrast was striking. The graph was clean and abstract; the photograph held weather, posture, light, and the particular expressions of four men who had been standing close together on a porch. Neither displaced the other. Together, they made it harder to pretend that a person's history could be preserved by only one method.

Luke stepped away from the table. The clearing looked different from a few paces back. The men had rearranged themselves without being told. Some stood beside their relatives; others had moved closer to the opposing family. The old boundary between the groups was still present, but it no longer governed every position. The youngest man from the first family had begun asking the second family's representative how they calibrated their measuring device. The representative showed him the hard case and explained the reference points. An older man from the second family was examining the stitching on the preserved shirt. He asked how many times the collar had been reconstructed. The first family told him. For a while the two men spoke as craftspeople rather than as representatives of competing doctrines.

Luke returned to me at the edge of the clearing.

“You see what happened?”

“The families found something they both lost.”

He nodded. “And something they kept.”

I looked at the table. “The shirt?”

“The fact that it belonged to a man.”

He watched the older man close the case, but not lock it. “Not a baseline. Not a number. A man who wore it, worked in it, sat at a table, and had to find a new shirt when it stopped fitting.”

The remark seemed to settle the meeting more effectively than the graph had. The men did not suddenly agree. They still disagreed about what should be measured, what should be saved, and how much authority the past should have over the next change. But they had begun to speak about the men who had lived through those changes instead of treating them as examples in a theory.

A soft movement passed beneath the roots of the oldest redwood. It was too slight to disturb the table, and none of the men noticed it at first. The ground gave a low settling sound, like a beam accepting weight. The man nearest the trunk looked down. A line of damp soil had opened between two roots, narrow as a pencil mark. He crouched and touched the edge without pulling it wider.

“What is that?” someone asked.

The man shook his head. “I don't know.”

Luke came closer, but he did not touch the ground. The line was visible now, extending a short distance through the soil before disappearing beneath a mat of needles. It was not a collapse. Nothing had fallen. The roots remained in place. Yet the clearing had acquired another measurement, and the men had no instrument ready to describe it.

The families looked from the soil to the table. The photograph, the shirt, the tablet, and the handwritten notebook were still arranged between them. Their evidence had not been invalidated. It had become incomplete in a new direction.

Luke stood beside the tree and looked at the dark line in the ground.

“That's new,” he said.

The oldest man from the first family stepped forward. “Does it mean the Serum is changing the forest?”

Luke considered the question. He looked back at the table, at the two sets of records, and at the men who had begun to stand together rather than opposite one another.

“It means we have another thing to observe.”

Nobody disputed that.

The meeting did not end with a vote. It ended with a shared decision to keep the table in the clearing until the next morning. The photographs and shirt would be returned to their cedar cases before nightfall; the measurement records would remain in their protective sleeves; and one copy of the comparison sheet would be kept by each family. The two groups would not merge their archives, but they would agree on a common set of reference conditions for future measurements. Their records would remain distinct and comparable. They would no longer use the absence of the other family's evidence as proof that it did not matter.

Before anyone packed away the records, the older man from the Continuity family asked Luke to help him lift the table's far end while the support board was moved. Luke took the weight without effort, but he waited for the other man to position the board rather than lowering it at once. The older man's hands were still strong, though his fingers moved more slowly than Luke's. They worked together until the table stood level. The younger representative from the Escalation family checked it with the measuring cord and found that the surface had shifted by less than a finger's width. He looked satisfied.

“That's close enough,” he said.

The older man smiled. “Close enough for a table.”

“Not for a measurement.”

“Then don't measure the table.”

The young man laughed. He had begun to understand that the old man's precision was not universal. A table only needed to remain steady enough for the work placed on it. A measurement needed a known reference. A memory needed an honest witness. Each had its own conditions. Luke released the table and brushed the damp bark dust from his palms. The older man thanked him, then offered him a cup of coffee from the kettle. They stood together at the edge of the clearing, talking about the old porch photograph and the house it had been taken in. The young man joined them after putting the cord away. For a few minutes the three men were not representatives of separate doctrines. They were simply men remembering a place, comparing what they had been told, and discovering that each had a different part of the story.

As the men began packing the chairs, Luke lifted the metal case and carried it to the driest part of the clearing. He set it on a low bench beneath the branches, away from the damp edge of the table. The case looked small in his hands. He placed it down gently, then checked that the lid was properly seated. The garment inside had survived because someone had made the effort to keep it. That effort did not become less valuable because the man who wore it had grown beyond its dimensions.

The table remained between the redwoods, its canvas cover moving slightly in the evening air. The two families departed by different roads, but several of the younger men stayed behind to finish the measurements. The older men watched them from the edge of the clearing. Nobody asked them to stop. The line in the soil remained visible beneath the roots.

By the time the light had begun to fail, the clearing was quiet again. The two families had arrived expecting to compare size. They left with a more difficult question: what, exactly, had been measuring them all along? The Serum had found a comparison they had not intended to make. It might have been the two families, the distance between their bodies, the history preserved in their objects, or the new conditions created by bringing those histories together. They could not decide yet.

Beneath the oldest redwood, something shifted once more. The ground held. The roots remained. But somewhere below the clearing, the forest was beginning to count space differently.
