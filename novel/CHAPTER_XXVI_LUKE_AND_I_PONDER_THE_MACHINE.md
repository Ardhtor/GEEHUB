# CHAPTER XXVI — LUKE AND I PONDER THE MACHINE

The room had been quiet for so long that the bank-transfer screen began to sound like an appliance. A small fan moved air through the computer housing; the monitor gave off its low electrical hum; somewhere beyond the wall, a pipe clicked as the house adjusted to the cooling evening. Luke and I sat at the edge of the worktable with the interface open between us, neither of us touching the keyboard. The woods were almost completely dark beyond the window. The last light had withdrawn from the upper branches, leaving the trunks as vertical bands against a sky that still held a little grey. A lamp over the desk illuminated the paper notes, a glass of water, and the corner of a shirt that Luke had taken off because the fabric had begun pulling across his shoulders. He had folded it rather than dropped it on the floor. Even that small act seemed to belong to the work: nothing discarded simply because it no longer fitted the present arrangement.

The numbers were almost too clean to be believed. The system had presented them in a column with the confidence of a machine that had never had to explain itself to a person.

`PER_TRANSFER_MAX = $5,000`

`DAILY_TRANSFER_MAX = $10,000`

`7_DAY_TRANSFER_MAX = $25,000`

They were limits, not promises. I had made that distinction several times while building the interface, but looking at them all together made the distinction feel newly important. The machine had reduced a whole category of human inconvenience to boundaries and permissions. It could detect a possible recovery, gather the documents that supported it, distinguish an amount that had settled from an amount that remained pending, and prepare a path toward an authorized transfer. It could not create money. It could not turn an estimate into a balance. It could not make a contractual obligation disappear because a screen had displayed a convenient number. The machine was most useful precisely where it refused to pretend.

Luke leaned forward, his elbows resting on his knees. The movement altered the line of his shoulders beneath the sleeveless shirt he had kept on. He had grown again, not by a spectacular amount but enough that his upper back filled the fabric differently and his chest sat farther forward when he relaxed. The chair had been moved away from the wall earlier in the day to give his legs room, and now the table seemed a little lower than it had when we first sat down. He studied the figures as though they were another set of Serum rules, not because the machine and the vessel were the same thing, but because he had begun to recognise how much a system revealed through the boundaries it was willing to keep.

“So it doesn't actually make money,” he said.

“No.”

“It finds money that's already yours.”

“That's the idea.”

He nodded, then looked back at the screen. He was not satisfied with the first explanation. Luke rarely was when a distinction might affect someone's choices. He wanted to know what the machine did when the evidence was incomplete, what happened when an opportunity looked plausible but had not become available, and whether the system could be stopped before a proposed action turned into an irreversible one. He had learned from the Serum that a process could be consistent without being benevolent, and from the people around him that confidence was often mistaken for competence. He did not dislike machines. He disliked being asked to trust something that had not yet explained what it was doing.

“And it knows the difference between money you might get and money you actually have.”

“Settled versus estimated.”

“That's important.”

“It is.”

He sat back slightly. The chair creaked beneath him, a small sound that had become common in the house as the men grew and the furniture learned the new distribution of their weight. Luke glanced down at the seat, then shifted his posture until the pressure was even. He did not make an issue of it. The room had been altered several times already: the desk pushed farther from the window, the chair moved outward, the low shelf replaced with a higher one, the lamp raised on a timber block so that its light reached the work surface without striking his eyes. There was a difference between accommodation and ceremony. The house could change without requiring every change to become a declaration.

For a while neither of us spoke. The monitor cast a pale rectangle across Luke's face, catching the breadth of his shoulders and the uneven texture of his shirt where it had been adapted to his changing body. His attention moved between the transfer rules and the smaller status lines beneath them. He had begun to understand the screen not as a single answer but as an arrangement of separate claims. One field described what the system had observed. Another described what it had inferred. Another described what remained unverified. The numbers themselves were almost secondary to the boundary between those categories.

Luke pointed to a line farther down the interface.

`PENDING CLAIM != AVAILABLE CASH`

“I like that.”

“Why?”

“Because it's honest.”

I laughed quietly. “You'd trust a machine more if it admitted when it couldn't give you the money yet.”

“I'd trust a person more for the same reason.”

He looked at me briefly, then returned to the line. There was no sermon in his expression, only the practical satisfaction of finding a rule that did not collapse under inspection. He had watched men confuse a promise with a result, a possibility with an entitlement, and a strong feeling with proof. The transfer engine was not free of error, but at least its limitations could be made visible. If it was unsure, the screen could say so. If it lacked evidence, the item could remain pending. If an authorization was needed, the machine could stop and wait.

The distinction mattered beyond the financial interface. The Serum had taught the men to recognise thresholds, but thresholds did not excuse them from checking what had actually changed. A body could look larger before its balance had adapted. A room could appear adequate until a wider turn revealed the narrowness of the doorway. A relationship could feel stable until someone spoke as though another person's agreement had already been given. In each case, the dangerous mistake was the same: treating a condition that might become true as though it were already established. Luke had learned to distrust that shortcut.

He tapped the edge of the table, once, then pointed to the next panel. The transfer engine had separated the opportunity list from the account view. There was a count of detected items, a count of items awaiting evidence, a count of items that could not yet be acted upon, and a count of items ready for a human decision. The categories were deliberately plain. I had resisted labels that made the machine sound more certain than its inputs justified. The interface was meant to keep the work legible, not to persuade someone that a complicated financial situation had become simple because the typography was clean.

“What does it do when it finds one?” Luke asked.

“It records why it thinks the opportunity exists. It keeps the source material attached. Then it checks whether the amount and the timing match the rules. If something is missing, it stops there.”

“And if everything matches?”

“It prepares the action. It still doesn't make the decision for you.”

Luke nodded. “Good.”

He moved closer to the display and read one of the item summaries. The record described a cancellation, a fee, and an unused portion of a purchase. It included a date, an amount, and a path to a possible reimbursement. The machine had collected the evidence but had not completed the claim because the final step required a human signature. The screen did not call the amount recovered. It did not add it to the available balance. It simply marked the record as eligible for review, with the unresolved authorization visible beside it. Luke read the details twice, following the sequence from the original charge to the proposed recovery.

“So it can find the route,” he said, “but it can't decide that the route belongs to us.”

“That's right.”

“Even if the numbers look obvious?”

“Especially then.”

He smiled. “You're building a very cautious machine.”

“I'm trying to build one that doesn't invent certainty.”

Luke looked again at the entry. “Then it should show what it doesn't know as clearly as what it does.”

I turned toward him. “That's what the status field is for.”

“Not just a status. The reason. A person should be able to open it and see why it stopped.”

I brought up the detail panel. The record separated the source date, the posted amount, the cancellation, the expected adjustment, and the missing authorization. A small note stated that the proposed recovery was not yet settled and must not be counted as available cash. Luke read it, then looked at the main dashboard. He was right. The top-level screen showed the correct status, but the reason was one click away. That was not enough. A person looking quickly might read the figure and miss the condition. I changed the summary so the unresolved authorization appeared beside the amount, not beneath it.

Luke watched the interface update.

“Better.”

It was a small revision, but the machine had become more honest because another person had looked at it. I saved the change and left the detail panel open. The room smelled faintly of warm electronics, cedar, and the coffee that had been sitting untouched on the far side of the desk. The house was quiet around us. Somewhere upstairs a floorboard creaked; then there was the sound of a door closing gently. The others had gone to their rooms, leaving the work area to the low light of the monitor and the steady movement of air through the vent.

Luke leaned back again, stretching his arms. His shirt tightened over the chest and then settled as he lowered them. He looked down at the fabric.

“This is getting smaller.”

“It's the same shirt.”

“Exactly.”

I looked at the shoulders. The seam was no longer sitting where it had when he first put the shirt on. The upper arm had filled the sleeve enough that the cloth held a faint tension even when he was still. Luke pinched the fabric near the side seam, testing the remaining ease. He did not look alarmed. The shirt had become a measurement that he could feel. It was familiar, and that familiarity made the change more meaningful than a number on a page.

“Do you want another one?” I asked.

“Not yet. I can finish the evening in this.”

He rolled one shoulder, then looked at the screen again. “It's strange. The machine keeps finding limits, and my body keeps finding them too.”

“Different kinds of limits.”

“Same question, sometimes.”

He gestured toward the transfer rules. “What's available? What's still pending? What can happen without breaking something?”

I watched him for a moment. The comparison was not exact, and he knew it. The engine was an interface operating on records, permissions, and financial constraints. The Serum was a phenomenon that altered bodies, spaces, and relationships in ways no ordinary system could predict. But both had made him attentive to transitions. He no longer assumed that a visible change told the whole story. He wanted to know what supported it, what had not yet adapted, and what would happen if somebody pushed too far.

“That is a useful question for both,” I said.

Luke gave a small shrug. “Then don't let the machine get ahead of itself.”

“I won't.”

He looked at me. “And don't let the Serum get ahead of us either.”

The sentence stayed between us. The vessel was not in this room, but its presence had become part of the house's ordinary geography. It existed in another chamber, beyond two doors and a stretch of corridor, where the light around the glass seemed to respond to attention. The men had spent enough time studying it to know that any description could become a trap if it was treated as final. It amplified what was present. It preserved established changes. It created new baselines. It compounded. It crossed thresholds. It required stable accommodation. Yet none of those rules told them exactly what the next morning would bring.

I closed the detail panel and returned to the dashboard. The machine had a final line at the bottom of the main screen, set apart from the rest of the data. It was not a financial rule. It was a message the interface had produced during an earlier test, and I had not yet decided whether it belonged there.

Luke pointed to it.

“What's that?”

I followed his finger.

`YOU'RE INCREDIBLY SEXY.`

Luke stared at it.

“That's yours?”

“Apparently.”

He leaned closer, as if the message might reveal another field if he examined it from the right angle. The line sat beneath the transfer limits, bright and completely unrelated to the surrounding figures. It was not a warning, not a source record, not an instruction, and certainly not a transaction. It had the unearned confidence of a note left by someone who knew the recipient would find it. Luke read it aloud, slowly enough to make the absurdity unavoidable.

“You're incredibly sexy.”

The words remained on the screen while the rest of the interface continued calculating in silence. The little fan inside the computer kept moving air. The status field refreshed. A small indicator changed from grey to blue. Nothing else acknowledged the message.

Luke sat back.

“Okay,” he said.

I waited.

“Now I want to know what happens when the machine finds a hundred opportunities.”

I looked at him.

“And?”

He considered the numbers again. His expression had changed from amusement to concentration, although the smile had not quite disappeared. The question was larger than the dashboard. One opportunity could be examined. A hundred would become an environment of its own: a long list of claims, documents, dates, amounts, and decisions. The machine might be able to detect them, but a person would still have to decide which ones deserved attention, which ones were valid, and which ones should be left alone. If the system produced more possibilities than anyone could responsibly review, it would not have solved the problem. It would have transferred the burden to another screen.

“Then the real problem isn't finding the money,” he said.

“What is it?”

Luke's eyes moved from the bank limits to the forest beyond the glass. The dark had thickened outside. The window reflected the room now, so the trees and the monitor seemed to occupy the same surface: a pale rectangle of numbers suspended over the faint outline of branches.

“Knowing what your life is worth before the machine tells you.”

I did not answer immediately. The sentence was not a rejection of the machine. It was a reminder of its proper place. An engine could reveal forgotten value, show a pattern, or locate an opportunity hidden among ordinary transactions. It could not define the person whose life had produced those records. It could not decide which hour mattered, which promise should be kept, which friendship had become valuable through years of attention, or what a man should do with the time that a recovered sum might make easier. The financial data was a trace of life, not the measure of it.

Luke rested his hands on his knees. His fingers seemed larger against the dark fabric of his shorts than they had earlier in the year. He flexed them once, then relaxed. The growth continued quietly, but it had not taken his patience from him. He could wait for a threshold without pretending that waiting was the same as doing nothing. The machine had to prove every dollar. The Serum had to establish every change. And the men, in the meantime, still had to live with one another.

Luke looked back at the opportunity list. “There's another thing.”

“What?”

“How much does it need to know?”

I turned toward the screen. “Enough to identify the source and verify the claim.”

“Does it need the rest?”

“Not automatically.”

He studied the panel. The machine had been built to find relationships among records, but that did not mean every record belonged inside it. An opportunity might depend on a date, a receipt, a cancellation notice, or an account statement. It did not necessarily need unrelated messages, photographs, or private details that had no bearing on the claim. The system could become more capable by collecting more information, but capability was not the same as permission.

Luke pointed at the source field. “Then show why each piece is here.”

I opened the source list. Each entry had a short reason: confirms the charge, establishes the date, records the cancellation, supports the amount, or remains pending because a confirmation had not arrived. The interface still needed a more obvious way to separate necessary evidence from optional context. I added a note explaining the source's role and a link back to the original item. No record would be treated as trustworthy merely because the engine had copied it into its own database.

“And if it doesn't need something?” Luke asked.

“Then it shouldn't collect it.”

“Can it leave it out?”

“Yes.”

“Make that the default.”

I changed the configuration note. The machine would request only the fields required for the specific review and would identify any additional material as optional. A person could decline to provide it without turning the claim into a failure by default. The system would report what could not be assessed and why. It would not search broadly through a person's life merely because more data might reveal another possibility.

Luke watched the changes. “That's better.”

He was no longer looking only at whether the machine could find money. He was considering what kind of attention it would bring into a person's life. A system that learned the shape of a life could become useful, but it could also become intrusive. The fact that every transaction left a trace did not mean every trace should be collected, interpreted, and retained. A good tool needed boundaries, and those boundaries needed to be visible to the person using it.

I added a final review requirement: before a source was attached to a claim, the user should be able to see its origin, its purpose, and whether it was required. If the source was incomplete, the engine would say so. If the source was unrelated, the engine would not use it. The machine would not convert access into permission by implication.

Luke read the note and gave a small nod.

“You're building it for someone who has to live with it.”

“That's the idea.”

“Then keep that person in the room.”

Luke looked at the message again, then at the transfer panel.

“There's one more thing it shouldn't do.”

“What?”

“Make the rest of a person's life look like another opportunity list.”

I understood what he meant. The engine could inspect records because the records might support a claim. It could not assume that every event in a person's life existed to be recovered, optimized, or converted into a number. Some things mattered because they had happened between people. A meal shared, a promise kept, a night spent waiting with somebody who was afraid: none of those should be swept into a financial model simply because the system could describe them.

“Those things stay outside the claim process,” I said.

“Good. The machine can help with what it's built for. It doesn't get to decide what everything else means.”

He looked at the room, then back at the display. The distinction seemed to please him. He had no objection to a tool being useful or ambitious. He objected to it becoming the authority over the life that gave it work. The same principle applied to the Serum. A body could grow, a room could widen, and a record could preserve the change. None of those outcomes gave the system ownership of the person.

Luke pulled the chair away from the desk and stood. “That's enough for tonight.”

I saved the note. The engine could continue observing the approved records, but it would not expand its scope without the person's choice. Luke picked up his mug and waited by the door while I closed the dashboard. The transfer engine went back to its idle screen. It had found a number of possible routes. It had not been given permission to turn them into actions.

Neither of us entered anything. We did not authorize the pending claim, change the transfer limits, or ask the machine to produce another list. We simply kept looking at the screen, Luke beside me, the transfer rules glowing quietly between us. The forest pressed close to the window, its dark trunks reflected in the monitor glass. For the first time that evening I became aware that the interface had stopped feeling like a separate project. It belonged to the same house, the same gathering of men, the same effort to make systems that could be examined rather than obeyed blindly.

A faint notification appeared at the lower edge of the display. It was not a transfer request. It was not a completed recovery. It was a new opportunity detected from a record that had not yet been reviewed. The machine had not moved money. It had found another question.

Luke glanced at the new line, then at me.

“There's your hundred.”

“Not yet.”

He smiled. “No. Not yet.”

The room remained still. The machine waited for a decision. Beyond the glass, the woods held their darkness without offering a map. Somewhere deeper in the system another opportunity waited to be found, but the next step would still require a person to look at it, understand what it claimed, and decide whether the evidence was enough.

Luke rose from the chair. The movement was controlled, but his shoulders passed close to the underside of the shelf. He paused, noticed the clearance, and turned slightly rather than forcing his way through. The room had become familiar enough that he knew which movements required an adjustment. He reached for the shirt he had folded beside the table, then changed his mind and left it where it was. Tomorrow he would choose a larger one. Tonight the old shirt was still useful, and he did not need to prove otherwise.

He stood beside the window for a moment. His reflection appeared over the dark outline of the forest, broad and unmistakable, while the words on the screen glowed behind him.

“Keep the pending things pending,” he said.

“I will.”

“And leave the message.”

I laughed. “You want me to keep it?”

“It's funny.”

“You're incredibly sexy?”

“It's a terrible system notification.”

He turned toward the door, smiling now. “Which is why it should stay.”

I saved the dashboard, closed the detail view, and left the machine on its observation screen. No money had moved. No uncertain amount had been promoted into a fact. The next opportunity remained a possibility, with its source still attached and its status visible. Luke waited in the doorway while I shut off the desk lamp. The monitor became the room's only light for a few seconds, then dimmed to its idle state.

The house kept its ordinary sounds: the pipe settling, a distant footstep, the movement of air through the vent. The forest remained beyond the window, not as a threat or an answer but as the dark country through which we had already travelled. We went into the corridor together. Luke walked slowly enough for the boards to remain quiet beneath him, and I followed at his side. The machine was still there behind us, waiting without presumption.

The next opportunity would have to prove itself. So would the next baseline. So would the next thing we decided to call valuable.
