# CHAPTER XXV — RIVALING FAMILIES

The first family arrived at the edge of the woods before dawn, and the second arrived from the opposite road shortly afterward, so that for several minutes neither group knew the other was there. They were all men. Fathers, sons, brothers, cousins, uncles: an entire architecture of resemblance moving through the trees in different generations of the same physical vocabulary. Their faces were not identical, but there were recurring structures among them—the same set of the jaw, the same heavy shoulders, the same way of standing with the chest slightly forward as though the body had learned, over decades, to occupy more space than the rooms designed for it. The Serum had not made them copies. It had made inheritance visible.

The first family had travelled in a loose formation, with the older men near the front and the younger ones carrying the heavier cases. They did not walk in silence. Their conversation moved from the condition of the road to the breakfast they expected to make at the next stop, then back to the old measurements that had been copied into the leather-bound book one of them kept under his coat. Their clothing showed a history of repeated alteration. A dark jacket had been let out at the back; a pair of trousers had been lengthened with a band of slightly different cloth; one shirt had been repaired with a seam that ran visibly along the shoulder. The men wore these changes without embarrassment. They seemed to regard each garment as a record of work done and days lived.

The oldest man carried a cedar case. It was not especially large, but he held it close and kept one hand on the lid whenever the path narrowed. A younger relative offered to take it at a steep section. The older man refused, then changed his mind when the ground became slippery. He passed the case over carefully and told the younger man where the hinge was weak. The exchange had the ease of people who had done such things many times. No one asked why the case mattered. They knew.

When they reached the clearing, the men stopped to let the last of their group catch up. The youngest had been walking with a slight stiffness in his shoulders, and one of the older men asked whether the straps were pressing too tightly. The young man said he was fine. The older man adjusted the strap anyway, then stepped back and let him decide whether it felt better. They did not make a spectacle of the change in his build. They had seen it happen before, but each new alteration still required attention.

The second family came from the opposite road with a different kind of order. Their men carried hard cases, flat folders, and a set of instruments wrapped in dark cloth. Their coats were less visibly altered, but several had been cut with unusual allowance at the shoulders and chest. They moved with a measured pace, leaving space between each other so that no one had to change direction suddenly. Their eldest representative walked at the centre. He carried no case himself. The younger men looked to him when they reached the edge of the trees, and he gave a small nod before they entered the clearing.

The two groups saw one another almost at the same moment. Neither turned away. The first family closed its ranks slightly, not in hostility but out of habit. The second family stopped at the line of trees and waited. For a moment the clearing held two histories without any shared language for describing them.

Luke noticed the second family first.

He stopped.

Behind him, the others stopped too.

Across the clearing stood six men in dark coats, watching them through the redwood trunks. The tallest man stepped forward. His coat had already been altered at the shoulders, the seams displaced outward and the front panels sitting farther from his body than ordinary tailoring would have allowed. He looked at Luke's chest, then at his shoulders, then at the way his shirt pulled across his back.

“You've been growing,” he said.

Luke looked down at himself.

“So have you.”

Nobody laughed.

That was the moment they understood that this was not a meeting between strangers. It was a measurement.

Luke had been speaking with the man who carried the notebook when he saw the movement at the opposite edge of the clearing. He stopped mid-sentence. The others followed his gaze. The six men in dark coats stood between two trunks, their faces partly shadowed by the early light. The tallest of them stepped forward. His coat had already been altered at the shoulders, the seams displaced outward and the front panels sitting farther from his body than ordinary tailoring would have allowed. The cloth was expensive but practical, chosen for movement rather than display. He looked first at Luke's chest, then at his shoulders, then at the way his shirt pulled across his back.

“You've been growing,” he said.

Luke looked down at himself.

“So have you.”

Nobody laughed. The words were ordinary, but the attention beneath them was not. Each man was trying to determine whether the other had arrived at the same point by the same process. The tall man glanced toward the older men behind Luke, then toward the cedar case. He seemed to recognise its significance, though he did not ask about it.

One of the younger men from the second family stepped closer. He was broad through the upper body, with a much narrower waist, and his coat had been tailored with a double pleat at the back. He looked at Luke's shoulders and then at his own. His expression was not hostile. It was the expression of a man who had found another person whose body might answer a question he had been carrying for some time.

“Does it keep going?” he asked.

Luke considered him. “I don't know.”

The young man nodded. “That's what we keep saying.”

The tallest man turned toward him. “We say it because it is true.”

“And because we don't have a better answer.”

The older man did not disagree. He looked back at Luke. “How long since your last stable measure?”

Luke glanced toward the others. He did not want to pretend he had an exact figure. “Long enough that the shirt has started telling me before the notebook does.”

The tall man smiled faintly. “That's a familiar stage.”

The first family's older representative watched the exchange. He stepped forward and introduced his group by lineage rather than by a list of names, describing the generations that had preserved their records. The second family's representative answered with the methods his own family used: regular measurements, fixed positions, repeatable instruments, and a chronology in which each new baseline was compared with the last confirmed one. The introductions were courteous, but the contrast was already visible. One group trusted things that had been kept; the other trusted readings that could be repeated.



The older men began comparing records. One family had kept photographs. The other had kept measurements. One had preserved clothing from every baseline, carefully labeled and stored in cedar cabinets. The other had destroyed almost everything that no longer fit, believing that the old proportions had no authority over the new ones. Their doctrines were incompatible, but their evidence was the same: every generation had crossed another threshold.

The Serum had entered their families differently. In one lineage it was spoken of as continuity. A grandfather's broadened shoulders became a father's starting point; the father's starting point became his son's ordinary frame. Nothing was considered extraordinary for long. In the other family, every increase was treated as a challenge. A new baseline was not merely accepted; it was tested against everyone else's.

That realization made the clearing strangely quiet. The words did not erase the differences between them, but they weakened the assumption that one family had a right to define what the substance meant. The oldest man from the first family looked at the second family's instruments and asked whether their records included the earlier generations. The representative said that they had only what could be verified. The first man asked whether a photograph could verify a change in the body. The second replied that a photograph could support a comparison if the camera, position, and scale were known. The first man said that a camera could still fail to show what it felt like to wear the shirt. The second agreed.

For a moment the men were surprised by the agreement. Then the conversation became more specific. They compared the dates of the first recorded changes, the kinds of clothing that had been retained, and the ways each family had checked a new baseline. One family had saved old garments because they believed the objects held memory. The other had kept measurements because they believed the numbers would survive the loss of objects. Each had protected something valuable. Each had also left something out.

The tall man from the second family walked toward the oak at the edge of the clearing and looked up into its branches. His shoulders sat heavily beneath the coat. He removed one glove and flexed his hand, then looked at Luke.

“Do you ever feel as though the room is waiting for you to change before it changes itself?”

Luke understood the question. “Sometimes.”

“Does that make you want to change faster?”

“No.”

The tall man nodded. “Good.”

The answer seemed to surprise the younger men. The first family had expected the second to treat every increase as a challenge. The second had expected the first to be resistant to any further change. But the tall man was not asking Luke to compete. He was trying to find out whether the process had a pace that could be lived with. Luke's reply had been simple, and the older man seemed to respect it.

The first family's representative opened the cedar case just enough to show the folded edge of a garment inside. He did not remove it. The second family's representative asked whether the family could see the measurements associated with it. The old man said he would show them later, once he had confirmed the dates. He did not want the object separated from its history. The other man accepted the condition. The case closed again, and the men moved back toward the centre of the clearing.



The youngest men began moving around one another, not aggressively, but carefully. They noticed the practical differences first. One man's stride was wider. Another turned his entire torso before turning his shoulders. One had learned to sleep differently because the new breadth of his body made his old position uncomfortable. Another had developed a habit of standing farther from tables. Their bodies were not sculptures. They were systems carrying accumulated history.

One of the younger men from the first family approached a member of the second and asked how he had adjusted his sleeping position. The second man laughed, surprised by the question, then described how he now slept on his side with a longer pillow beneath his upper arm. His shoulders had become broad enough that lying flat on his back made his arms rest awkwardly. The first man said he had experienced something similar, but had solved it by changing the mattress. They compared the two approaches. Neither was declared better. One had altered the body’s immediate support; the other had altered the bed.

Luke watched the exchange. It was the first time the two families had spoken about their changes as ordinary problems rather than as evidence in a contest. He moved closer to the men and asked how long the adjustments had taken. One said several nights. The other said he had needed a week. The difference mattered. A stable measurement did not mean a person had mastered the new way of sleeping. The body could change before the habits around it had caught up.

A few paces away, the tall man from the second family had removed his coat. His shirt was stretched across the back, and the seam beneath the arm had been let out twice. He stood beside one of the older men from the first family. They compared the tailoring without touching each other's clothing. The older man asked whether the changes had been made by one tailor or several. The tall man said that the first alteration had been done by a family member, the second by a professional, and the third by himself after he had learned how the cloth responded. He described the difference between opening a seam and adding a panel. The older man listened with the interest of someone who understood the work.

Luke watched one of the older men remove his coat.

Underneath, his shirt had been tailored repeatedly. There were visible lines where earlier seams had been opened and reconstructed. The garment contained the record of his growth more clearly than any photograph.

“You kept the old shirts?” Luke asked.

“All of them.”

“Why?”

The man looked at the forest.

“So we could prove that we didn't become someone else.”

Luke understood.

The rivalry had always been about the wrong thing.

They had thought they were competing over size. They were actually competing over continuity.

The younger men continued talking. One described how his gloves had become too small; another showed the reinforced heel of a shoe that had been rebuilt to accommodate his changing foot. A man from the second family explained that he had stopped keeping clothes once the measurements became his primary record. He had not realised how much he missed the feel of the old fabric until he saw the cedar case. The first family's younger members were surprised to hear this. They had assumed that the second family had discarded old clothing because it no longer mattered to them.

The first family had brought a small bench with them from the road. They placed it near the tree so that the older men could sit while the younger ones continued talking. The second family offered to help move it. The bench was heavy, and its legs had been reinforced with an additional crossbar. Two men lifted one end; two more took the other. They moved it a few feet and set it down. The bench rocked slightly. A young man crouched to inspect the leg, then found a flat piece of wood to slip beneath it.

“You've done this before,” one of the second family remarked.

“We've had to,” the first man replied.

The younger man smiled. “That's what you say about everything.”

“Because it's true about most things.”

They laughed. The bench stopped rocking. One of the older men sat down, then invited the tall man from the second family to sit beside him. Their shoulders nearly touched. Neither moved away. For a while they watched the younger men comparing their gloves and shoes. The first family had expected the meeting to expose the differences between their histories. Instead, they were beginning to find ordinary points of contact: the same inconvenience of a tight sleeve, the same need for a wider chair, the same question of how long a new body needed to settle into its new habits.

The largest man remained standing. He could feel the new pressure across his chest, but he did not want to sit yet. He watched the bench and the two men sharing it. There was an ease in their posture that seemed to have nothing to do with measurement. He wondered whether the Serum was also responding to the fact that the men had begun to occupy the clearing differently. The two groups were no longer positioned only by family. They had started to arrange themselves according to who they wanted to speak with and where they felt comfortable standing.

Then someone in the back of the clearing felt the first change.

Not dramatic.

A pressure across the upper back. A deeper breath. A shirt becoming fractionally tighter beneath the arms.

Everyone looked toward him.

He waited.

Nothing stopped.

The Serum had found the comparison.

The two families watched the change settle into the man's posture. His shoulders shifted outward by an amount too small to measure immediately, but large enough to change the way his coat hung. He rolled one shoulder, then the other. The man beside him reached out as though to steady him, then stopped when he saw that the movement had not disturbed his balance.

“New baseline,” one of the fathers said.

“No,” Luke answered.

Everyone turned toward him.

Luke looked from one family to the other.

“Next baseline.”

For the first time, the families stood together rather than opposite one another. The man whose shoulders had changed looked from Luke to the people around him. He was not being congratulated, and no one called it an achievement. They simply gave him room to settle. The old shirt case remained closed. The measurement instruments stayed in their wraps. The next reading could wait until the change had become clear enough to record.

The man who had changed was embarrassed by the attention. He looked down at his coat and tried to settle the fabric at the shoulders. The tall man from the second family approached, but he stopped at a respectful distance.

“Do you want us to measure it now?”

The man shook his head. “Not yet. I can feel it, but I don't know where it's settled.”

The tall man nodded. “Then we wait.”

The older man from the first family brought the cedar case closer, though he did not open it. He asked whether the changed man wanted to sit. The man said he would rather keep standing for a little while. Luke moved the bench a few inches so the path remained clear. The younger men made room without being told. They had begun to understand that the right response to a change was not always a measurement. Sometimes it was allowing the person to find his balance before everyone tried to describe what had happened.

Tyler was not yet part of the scene as the formal recorder he would become later, but one of the men from the second family had a notebook ready. He asked whether he should note the time. Luke said yes, but the entry should say that the change was observed and not yet measured. The notebook-holder wrote the distinction down. The first family agreed that its own record would contain the same note. Neither archive would be asked to accept the other's interpretation.

The tall man looked toward the clearing's edge. “We'll need a proper surface tomorrow.”

“A table?” the older man asked.

“A place where both records can be set beside each other.”

The first family considered it. One of their men had a folding table in the vehicle. The second family had a broad sheet of canvas and a set of level boards. They agreed to bring them in the morning. No one insisted that the documents be copied or surrendered. The objects would remain with the people who had preserved them. The measurements would remain with the people who had taken them. For a few hours, at least, they would all be able to look at the evidence together.

The young man who had felt the change rolled his shoulders once more. The pressure had begun to settle. He looked toward Luke.

“Does it always happen like that?”

Luke shook his head. “I don't know. It hasn't happened the same way twice.”

The man nodded. “That's a better answer than pretending.”

Luke smiled. “You'll get used to saying it.”

The men began collecting their equipment. The cedar case was carried by the older man and his younger relative. The second family packed its instruments into the hard cases and checked that the wraps were dry. Several of the younger men remained together for a while, talking about the new change and comparing how their coats fitted. The conversation had lost its edge. They were still curious about one another, and some of them were still measuring themselves against the other family, but the comparison no longer required a winner.

The woods remained silent around them.

The rivalry had become something else.

And somewhere beyond the clearing, beneath roots older than either family, the Serum was already responding.
