# CHAPTER XXXIII — THE MAN WHO KNEW HOW TO WAIT

*Narrated by Bill: an older white Southern man, speaking from inside the room. His voice is measured, dry, observant, and affectionate without being sentimental. Regional speech appears naturally and sparingly; he is not a caricature.*

The lid came up another inch, and I put my hand on Luke's arm.

Not hard. I wasn't trying to stop him. A man doesn't get to be my age without learning the difference between stopping somebody and asking him to wait a second.

“Hold on there, son.”

Luke looked down at my hand, then at me. The amber light was running along the grain of the box now. It didn't shine out like a lamp. It seemed to be coming from somewhere inside the wood, the way a coal holds its heat after the fire has gone low.

I had seen a good many things in my time that people were in a hurry to name. Most of them got named wrong. A storm gets called a miracle when it breaks a drought, and a machine gets called a monster when nobody can explain what it wants. I had learned to give a thing a little room before deciding what it was.

“Bill,” Tyler said, “you know what that is?”

“No,” I told him. “But I know what it ain't.”

He waited.

“It ain't asking us to be brave.”

That settled the room more than I expected. The younger men had drawn close without quite meaning to. Luke stood nearest the box, broad through the shoulders, his gray shirt sitting tight across a chest that had outgrown the shape the cloth was made for. There was nothing careless about him. His arms hung loose at his sides, his hands open. He had the size to make a room feel smaller, but he wasn't trying to take the room from anybody. That distinction mattered to me.

The old house had been built by men who knew how to make things last. Oak beams, thick doorframes, a stair that complained in the same place every winter. It had been altered so many times that no two doorways were quite alike. A newer brace met an older post; a widened hall still showed the marks where the first wall had stood. I liked that about the place. It didn't pretend it had always been this way. It showed you the work.

I looked at the box again.

“Y'all keep talking about what comes next,” I said. “I want to know what came before anybody thought to ask.”

The machine made a small sound beneath the floor. Not a warning. More like a motor finding its idle.

Luke glanced at me. “You think it remembers?”

“I think it kept something.”

“What's the difference?”

“Memory can be a story a man tells himself. Keeping is when the thing is still there after he's done talking.”

I heard somebody shift behind us. One of the younger fellows had brought a notebook down from the upper room. He held it against his chest like he was waiting for instructions. I almost told him to put it away. Then I thought better of it. A record can be useful, provided it doesn't get mistaken for the event.

The lid opened.

Inside the box lay a strip of cloth, folded once. It was gray, worn pale at the crease, with a small loose thread caught along one edge. Luke leaned closer. His expression changed before he touched it.

“That's mine.”

“From when?” Tyler asked.

Luke didn't answer right away. He was looking at the stitching.

“Before the first growth pass. Before any of this.”

I knew the shirt. Not because I had kept it, but because I remembered the day he wore it. He had stood in the kitchen with his shoulders slightly forward, trying not to knock a glass off the counter. He had laughed when the old ceiling fan caught the loose fabric at his sleeve. Back then, the room had seemed large enough.

It wasn't the shirt that made me remember. It was the way Luke had reached for the glass afterward: careful, two fingers around the rim, as though strength were something he had to keep from spilling.

The Hub printed a line above the box.

**FIRST CONTACT: OBJECT RECOVERED.**

Then another.

**IDENTITY: CONTINUOUS.**

Luke lifted the cloth. The amber light passed over his knuckles and settled in the weave. The shirt didn't change. The man holding it had changed a great deal, but the little piece of cloth had kept the shape of an earlier afternoon.

“That's a funny thing,” I said. “Everybody's been looking for the first version of you as if he was some smaller fellow you left behind.”

Luke looked at me.

“But there he is,” I said. “Not gone. Not waiting to be replaced. Just part of the same account.”

The machine answered with a low pulse. Along the walls, the shelves began to show more clearly. The old ruler had pencil marks on it. There were dates, initials, a few measurements crossed out and written again. A cup had been repaired with a dark seam. The photograph of Luke at the redwoods had a crease through one corner. None of it looked holy. It looked handled.

That was what got to me.

A world can make a grand performance of its mysteries. It can put lights under the floor and give a man a voice from a room nobody knew was there. But the truth of a place is often in what somebody bothered to mend.

I stepped around the table. My knee complained, as it had been doing since before the Hub had a name. I rested my palm against the wood and felt the old grain beneath the polish.

“Now, listen,” I said, mostly to the machine. “If you're fixing to show us the beginning, don't give us a picture that makes it look easy. Show us the room. Show us who was there. Show us what they had to move out of the way. A man's life ain't just the part that photographs well.”

The screen went dark.

For a moment I thought I'd offended it.

Then the room above us appeared on the wall—not as it stood now, but as it had been on that earlier afternoon. The old fan turned slowly. Sunlight lay across the counter. The glass stood near the edge. Luke was there in the gray shirt, younger in scale, familiar in every other respect.

And there was another man in the kitchen with him.

Not one of us.

He stood with his back half-turned to the image, broad and older, his forearms resting on the counter. He was watching Luke with the patient look of somebody who had already decided there was time enough.

The image did not give us his face.

It gave us his voice.

“Take your time, son. Ain't a thing in this house needs doing all at once.”

Luke went still.

I knew that voice. Not the man, not yet. The voice itself had the weight of a memory that had been carried a long way without being spoken aloud.

The Hub printed:

**WITNESS LOCATED.**

Then, beneath it:

**ASK HIM WHAT HE BUILT.**

I looked at Luke. He was still holding the shirt.

“Well,” I said, “seems we've got somebody else to meet.”

The image of the kitchen brightened. The old man in it turned—not toward Luke, but toward the place where we stood.

And this time, he looked straight at me.
