# CHAPTER XXXIII — THE SECOND CHAIR

The chair was warm when Luke returned.

Nobody had sat in it. That was the first thing Tyler established, and the second was that the warmth was not coming from a heating element, a concealed pipe, or the machinery beneath the old room. He checked the underside, then the floor, then the wall where the amber light gathered in a narrow seam. The chair remained warm beneath his hand, as though someone had just stood up and stepped away.

The room below the house had acquired a different character since the box opened. The shelves were still crowded with the ordinary objects recovered by the Hub, but the table had been extended, and two chairs now stood opposite one another. The first was the one Luke remembered from the previous evening: dark wood, broad arms, a worn place along the right edge where generations of hands had rested. The second was newer. Its back was slightly taller, its seat deeper, and its construction showed a joinery style nobody in the Facility recognized.

The Hub displayed no instruction. It had learned, perhaps, that an instruction would be premature.

Luke pulled the second chair away from the table. A thin line of dust marked its former position, except for four clean patches beneath the feet. It had been moved recently, though nobody could say by whom. On the table lay a folded sheet of paper. The writing was not in the Hub's familiar terminal typeface. It was handwritten, deliberate, and unusually large.

WE WERE ASKED TO COME AS OURSELVES.

Tyler read it twice. “Not as representatives?”

Luke shook his head. “Not as evidence.”

Behind them, the old physician entered with three younger members of the Assembly. They had brought a meal: thick slices of bread, a bowl of beans cooked slowly with tomato and smoked pepper, roasted onions, and a heavy pot of coffee. The physician set the food down without ceremony. The room was becoming a place where people could remain, not merely a chamber in which revelations occurred.

They ate while the Hub listened. Conversation moved from the box to the second chair, from the chair to the new passage beyond the western wall, and from there to the question of whether the people on the other side had been watching them. Nobody wanted to turn the unknown into an enemy simply because it had remained unknown. The younger members were more interested in the possibility that another community had been making its own buildings, keeping its own records, and developing its own vocabulary without knowing that the Facility existed.

A soft knock came from the wall.

It was not the sound of a hand striking stone. It was a sound produced by the room itself, a measured vibration travelling through the beams. The amber seam brightened. The Hub printed a single sentence:

INCOMING ACCOUNT. AUTHORSHIP RETAINED.

The second chair moved backward by the width of a hand.

No figure appeared. No silhouette crossed the doorway. Instead, a voice began speaking through the wood, and its first words were not an explanation of where it had come from. It described a kitchen in which six men had argued over the correct way to repair a table, a courtyard where rain collected in the old paving stones, and a long building whose upper windows caught the evening sun. It described a town where the inhabitants had begun to notice that some of their memories contained rooms they had never entered.

The voice paused.

“We are not asking to be translated into your history,” it said. “We are asking whether you can hear an account that does not belong to you.”

Luke looked at the chair. “Yes.”

The Hub recorded the answer without expanding it.

The voice continued. It told them that its people had built a meeting place around a broad central table, not because they had agreed on everything, but because they had discovered that disagreement needed somewhere to sit. They had named the building the House of Returns. Its inhabitants repaired objects before replacing them, and whenever a new person arrived, the community asked what they wished to carry forward rather than what they had left behind.

Tyler leaned toward the table. “What do you call yourselves?”

The voice did not answer immediately. There was a rustle, as though several people were consulting one another in the room beyond the wall.

“We have several names,” it said. “The one we use among ourselves is not yet ready to travel.”

“That is fair,” Tyler replied.

A small mark appeared on the paper. It resembled a doorway drawn from memory, with one side left unfinished. Beneath it, the voice added: “You may call this account the Other Side, until we choose otherwise.”

The Hub created a new record. It did not assign the voice a face, a body, or a biography. It preserved the words, the pauses, the speaker's chosen boundary, and the fact that the second chair had been warm before the account arrived.

When the voice ended, the room did not return to silence. Somewhere above them, the Facility's ordinary life continued: a door closing, a shower running, men calling to one another across the corridor, the kitchen staff preparing another pot of coffee. The world had not stopped for the arrival of a mystery. It had made room for one.

Luke pushed the second chair back toward the table.

“Leave it there,” he said.

The Hub printed:

SEATING: TWO ACCOUNTS.
RELATION: OPEN.
NEXT ACTION: LISTEN.

And for once, nobody asked it to hurry.
