# CHAPTER XXXIV — THE HOUSE OF RETURNS

The next account arrived with a floor plan.

It came folded into four sections, each drawn on a different kind of paper. The first showed a courtyard and the kitchen that opened onto it. The second showed a long dining room with a table large enough for twenty men. The third showed a workshop, a laundry, and a covered passage connecting the two. The fourth contained a building whose upper storeys had been left blank.

The voice from the second chair explained that the blank floors were not unfinished through neglect. Their inhabitants had decided not to determine every use before the building was occupied. They had built the lower levels carefully, tested the stairs, checked the light, and made the roof watertight. The rooms above would be shaped by the people who came to live in them.

Luke studied the drawing. “A building that expects to be changed.”

“A building that expects to be inhabited,” Tyler corrected.

The distinction was entered into the record.

The Facility's architects were invited to examine the plan. They arrived carrying measuring tapes, notebooks, and the habit of looking at every wall as a possible structural problem. Their first discovery was that the House of Returns had been designed around movement rather than spectacle. The kitchen was near the courtyard, the dining room near the kitchen, and the workshop near the laundry because the inhabitants had learned that the practical routes of a building determined much of its social life. People met where work required them to meet. They became familiar through repetition. Friendship developed in the intervals between tasks.

A week later, the Facility began constructing its own House of Returns.

It was placed beyond the western gardens, where the ground rose gently toward a line of old trees. The building used dark timber, pale stone, and broad windows. Its central room had a high ceiling supported by heavy beams, and the kitchen was fitted with long counters, deep sinks, and a range large enough to serve the whole residential wing. The dining table was made from joined oak planks. Its surface retained the natural variations of the wood, and the carpenter refused to cover them with a uniform stain.

The first meal was an experiment in hospitality. There was roast chicken with lemon and herbs, potatoes crisped in the pan, a large dish of beans, bread still warm from the oven, and a cake that had been prepared by three people who disagreed about how much coffee belonged in the icing. The disagreement was preserved as a recipe note. Nobody was required to settle it before taking a slice.

Men began to use the building without waiting for an official opening. One arrived to repair a chair and stayed for lunch. Another came to ask about the upper floors and ended up helping the gardeners move a stack of stone. A pair of residents from the eastern dormitory began meeting there after their shifts, bringing books and comparing the routes they took through the grounds. The building acquired a social rhythm before it acquired a formal programme.

The voice from the Other Side returned through the second chair. It asked whether the Facility had understood the blank floors.

“We are trying not to fill them too quickly,” Luke said.

“Good,” the voice replied. “We once made the mistake of furnishing every room before anyone had arrived. The furniture was beautiful. Nobody knew where to sit.”

Tyler laughed, and the laugh was carried into the record as an event rather than a decoration.

The House of Returns soon became the point at which the Facility's separate departments encountered one another without being summoned. The construction crew came to discuss materials with the gardeners. The kitchen staff asked the surveyors to measure a new outdoor oven. The men responsible for the archives requested shelves, while the younger residents asked whether one of the upper rooms could become a music room. Their requests were entered as proposals, not orders.

The Hub noticed that the building had begun to generate its own history. It recorded repairs, meals, conversations, furniture changes, and the names of people who had used the rooms. It also recorded the spaces that remained empty. An empty room was not a failed room. It was a possibility that had not yet met its inhabitants.

At the end of the month, Luke walked through the upper floor. The windows looked toward the trees and the long amber light beyond them. One room contained a workbench and a stack of timber. Another had a bed, a wardrobe, and a small desk. A third remained bare except for a chair facing the window.

He stood in the doorway for a while.

The Hub asked whether the room should be assigned a purpose.

“No,” Luke said. “Let it meet somebody first.”

The system marked the room as available, not vacant.

That evening, a new line appeared in the House of Returns ledger:

A PLACE IS NOT COMPLETE WHEN IT IS BUILT.
A PLACE BEGINS WHEN SOMEONE CAN RETURN TO IT.

The voice from the Other Side added one final sentence.

“We have a room like that, too.”

The two accounts remained separate. Their buildings did not become identical, and neither community claimed to have invented the other's customs. But the plans now contained a shared feature: a room whose meaning would be decided by the life that entered it.
