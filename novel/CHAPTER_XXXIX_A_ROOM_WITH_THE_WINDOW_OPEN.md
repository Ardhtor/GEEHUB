# CHAPTER XXXIX — A ROOM WITH THE WINDOW OPEN

The university was quiet before breakfast.

A pale light had settled over the courtyard, and the trees beyond the dormitories were still holding the last of the night. Someone had left a book on the long table. A mug stood beside it, washed and turned upside down on a folded cloth. In the kitchen, the first kettle began to hum.

Emil was already awake.

He had been living in the eastern residence for six weeks, long enough to know which stair creaked and which window caught the morning sun. He was an adult student in the history and media programme, with a particular interest in old recording equipment and the way communities preserved their own stories. He also took prescribed antidepressant medication. It was part of his ordinary routine, kept beside his keys and the small notebook in which he wrote down things he did not want to forget. He did not consider it the most interesting fact about himself, and neither did the people who knew him.

Some mornings were easier than others. This one had begun slowly. He had woken with a familiar heaviness and no particular explanation for it. He washed, dressed, opened the window, and made a cup of tea. He did not demand that the morning become wonderful. He let it become a morning.

The grey cat appeared on the balcony ledge.

“You're early,” Emil said.

The cat looked at him with the calm disapproval of an animal that had never agreed to a schedule. Emil moved his chair away from the window so there was room for it to pass. The cat stepped inside, inspected the rug, and settled near the warm patch of sunlight.

At breakfast, Dan Roman was sitting at the end of the long table with a plate of eggs, toast, tomatoes, and a generous serving of roasted potatoes. He had finished an early training session and was reading through the revised plans for the university gymnasium. His shoulders filled the back of his chair, but he was concentrating on a small detail in the ventilation drawing.

Emil sat opposite him.

“Is it better?” he asked, nodding toward the plan.

“Much better. We moved the upper vents and made the maintenance walkway accessible from both sides.” Dan folded the paper. “The room should work properly even when it's busy.”

They spoke about the building for a while. Dan asked about Emil's research, and Emil explained that he was collecting accounts of ordinary places: dining rooms, workshops, shared kitchens, corridors where people stopped to talk. He was interested in the way a building became meaningful through repeated use.

Dan listened without interrupting. Then he said that the Iron Room had changed more through small habits than through its original design. People returned equipment, adjusted benches for the next person, and learned to leave the central lane clear. The room had become easier to share because people had learned how to use it together.

Emil wrote that down.

After breakfast, he went to the library. He had planned to work on a chapter, but the first hour passed slowly. He read a page, made a note, and then spent several minutes looking out at the courtyard. He considered going back to his room. Instead, he sent a short message to Joseph asking whether the archive had any photographs of the old service road.

Joseph replied that there were several, and suggested they look at them together after lunch.

That was enough of a plan.

The archive was cool and pleasantly dim. Joseph had placed the photographs on a large table, along with a map showing the road before the Convent Way was built. They examined the images one by one. There were pictures of muddy tracks, delivery vehicles, old timber fences, and the first stone culvert. Emil noticed a man standing at the edge of one photograph, holding a small dog under one arm. Nobody knew who he was.

They did not solve the mystery. They wrote a note and kept looking.

At lunch, Bill served a thick vegetable soup with white beans, rosemary, and bread toasted in olive oil. The dining room was full, but not noisy. A group of students were discussing a film. Two builders had brought a sample of timber to show the architects. The ginger cat slept beneath the table, entirely indifferent to the importance of the conversation above him.

Emil ate slowly. He found that he was hungry after all.

Later, he returned to the library and wrote three paragraphs. They were not the paragraphs he had intended to write, but they were clear, and they belonged to the chapter. He saved the file and added a note for tomorrow. He did not try to turn a modest afternoon into a triumph. It was simply work completed at a reasonable pace.

Before dinner, Dan found him in the courtyard.

“We're taking a walk along the new road,” Dan said. “A few of us. You can come if you like.”

Emil considered it. The idea of a long walk seemed less appealing than the idea of fresh air, so he asked how far they were going.

“Only to the redwoods and back. No expedition.”

They walked in a small group. Dan kept an easy pace, and Joseph pointed out the new drainage channel. The evening light reached through the trees in long bands. Near the bend, the grey cat appeared again, travelling along the low stone wall as though it had been following the same route all afternoon.

Nobody made a ceremony of the walk. They talked about the next university term, the unfinished upper-floor studio, and whether the House of Returns needed another coffee machine. Dan argued that it did. Joseph said the current machine was perfectly adequate. Emil suggested that the real problem was the queue.

By the time they returned, the lights were on in the dining room.

Emil took his usual place at the table. He had not become a different person during the day. The heaviness had not vanished in a dramatic scene, and nobody had tried to make it disappear for him. But he had eaten, spoken with friends, found a useful photograph, written three paragraphs, and walked beneath the trees. Those things were allowed to count.

When he went upstairs, the room was cool. He set his notebook beside his medication, prepared his things for the morning, and left the window open a little. The cat had followed him as far as the landing, then changed its mind and returned to the courtyard.

The Hub recorded the day without assigning it a score.

**A day lived at its own pace. Work continued. Company was available. The room was ready for tomorrow.**

Emil read the entry once, closed the terminal, and went to bed.
