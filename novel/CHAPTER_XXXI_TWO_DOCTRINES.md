# CHAPTER XXXI — TWO DOCTRINES

The next morning the families finally named their disagreement.

The clearing had been cold before sunrise. Mist sat low around the redwood roots, and the canvas over the table held a fine layer of moisture that darkened the folded edges. A kettle had been set on a portable stove near the nearest trunk. Someone had brought bread from the camp, and the smell of toasted crust mixed with damp bark and the bitter steam of coffee. The two groups arrived separately, as they had the previous day, but they no longer took their places on opposite sides of the clearing by habit. A few men crossed the space to greet people they had spent the previous evening questioning. The old shirt case was back on the table, beside the printed measurements and the notebook with the two columns.

They had not slept especially well. Several men had taken turns checking the narrow line in the soil beneath the oldest redwood. It had not widened, and no second crack had appeared. Tyler had inspected the marked points at first light and found no measurable change in the ground. That did not make the event unimportant. It meant only that the observation had not yet repeated. The men had agreed to keep the record open rather than turning a single sound into a permanent rule. Their attention had shifted back toward the argument that had brought them together.

The first family called its doctrine Continuity.

The second called its doctrine Escalation.

Neither name was spoken casually. The words had been discussed in the early morning, first in conversation and then on a sheet of paper. The older men had objected to both names because each seemed to claim too much. One said that continuity without change could become a form of denial. Another argued that escalation without memory could become an excuse to discard the very men whose lives had made the process possible. The names survived because they described the emphasis each family had developed, not because they settled the argument.

Continuity meant that a man's current body had authority because it contained everything that had happened before it. A growth could be dramatic, but it had to remain legible as the same life. Old photographs mattered. Old clothing mattered. Previous measurements mattered. Memory was part of anatomy. A man who had crossed a threshold did not cease to be the person who had stood before it. The earlier body remained present as the history of the current one, and the people who had known him at each stage were not interchangeable witnesses.

Escalation meant that every stable improvement created a responsibility to discover the next stable state. A larger baseline was evidence that another threshold might already be available. They measured constantly. They compared changes. They treated adaptation as an unfinished process. To stop at a particular measurement merely because it had become familiar seemed to them like confusing comfort with completion. They wanted to know what the body could do next, what architecture might be required, and whether a limit was a true limit or only the edge of what they had tested.

The men stood opposite one another beneath the redwoods. The clearing was large enough to hold them, but the table made a centre that neither group could avoid. Luke stood between them. He had not chosen the position as a symbolic gesture. He had walked there because both sides had begun speaking at once, and he wanted to hear each without making the other repeat itself. His shoulders were broad beneath the shirt, and the fabric pulled when he folded his arms. He let his hands fall again. He did not want his body to become an argument before he had spoken.

“You are arguing over whether the past or the future has more authority,” he said.

Nobody objected.

The oldest man from the Continuity family folded his arms. His coat had been altered at the sides, and the old seams remained visible where new panels had been inserted. He looked at Luke with the patient expression of a man who had argued this point many times.

“And what do you believe?”

Luke looked at the two groups.

“Neither.”

The word landed harder than an insult. The men who had been leaning forward stopped moving. The younger representatives of the Escalation family looked at one another. The oldest man from the Continuity side unfolded his arms.

Luke continued.

“The Serum doesn't choose between continuity and escalation. It uses both.”

A breeze moved through the trees. The canvas lifted once and settled. Luke looked toward the older men.

“Without continuity, escalation becomes replacement.”

He turned toward the younger ones.

“Without escalation, continuity becomes preservation. You become the thing you were instead of becoming the person you are.”

Nobody spoke. The sentence had not been delivered as a compromise. It described a tension that both families already knew but had kept naming as the other side's mistake. The Continuity family feared that the next threshold would erase the value of the earlier life. The Escalation family feared that reverence for the earlier life would become a reason not to move. Both fears had evidence behind them. The old shirt showed what could be lost when a body advanced beyond its familiar proportions. The measurement sequence showed what could be lost when a family failed to record the changes as they happened.

One of the younger men from the second family looked down at his hands. His fingers had thickened over the previous months, and the gloves he wore for training no longer fitted properly. The original pair had been discarded. He had not thought much about it until the previous day, when the older family had opened the metal case. Now he seemed to be reconsidering the decision.

“We didn't keep anything,” he said. “Not because we wanted to forget. We just thought the old measurements were enough.”

The older man with the shirt case looked toward him. “You can still start keeping things.”

“What would you keep?”

“Something you actually used. Don't keep a shirt just because you think it's important. Keep it because it reminds you of a day you want to remember.”

The younger man looked at his hands. “I remember my first pair of gloves.”

“Then write that down.”

The exchange was brief, but it changed the mood around the table. A doctrine had become something a man could practise rather than merely defend. The older man did not tell him that he had been wrong. He gave him a way to begin.

A man from the Continuity family looked toward the measurement sheets. “And what do we do with the next baseline? We can't keep every old position as though it were still current.”

“No,” Tyler said. He had been standing near the table, listening with his notebook open. “We keep the previous readings, but we mark which one is active. The new measurement becomes the current baseline only after we confirm it. Earlier measurements remain in the history.”

The Escalation family's representative looked at him. “You make it sound administrative.”

“It is administrative,” Tyler said. “That doesn't make it unimportant.”

A few men smiled. Tyler turned the notebook so both families could see the page. He had written three headings: PRIOR BASELINE, CURRENT BASELINE, NEXT OBSERVATION. Beneath them were spaces for measurement position, instrument, date, and the reason the observation had been repeated. He added a fourth heading: **CONTINUITY CONFIRMED**. The field would not answer every question, but it would force the person recording a change to say whether the present measurement belonged to the same person and the same consistent method.

Luke leaned over the page. “And if we can't confirm it?”

“Then we say that,” Tyler replied.

The oldest man from the Continuity family nodded. “Good. A record that admits uncertainty is more useful than one that pretends it has no gaps.”

The Escalation representative looked at the headings. “We should also include what the next stable state might require.”

“Not as a prediction,” Tyler said. “As a question. What would need to change for the next state to be supported?”

Luke looked at him. “That's better.”

The two families understood that they were not going to merge their archives. They had different histories, different habits, and different reasons for keeping the records they possessed. But they could agree on the structure of a comparison. The old measurements would remain visible. The newest reading would be marked as current. A proposed next step would remain a proposal until observed. If a man changed in a way that could not be measured reliably, the uncertainty would be recorded instead of hidden.

Luke's shoulders rose with a slow breath. For one second I could see the entire argument in his posture. He was carrying both theories physically. The body that remembered and the body that advanced were not separate systems. They were the same body. His shirt held the earlier seam beside the newer one; the fabric had been altered rather than replaced, and the change remained visible whenever he moved. He could feel the present shape of his chest and remember the earlier fit without treating either as an illusion.

The two families understood it too.

The rivalry did not end. It became more precise. They would still compare measurements. They would still argue about methods. They would still keep separate records. But from that morning forward, every record would contain one additional field:

**CONTINUITY CONFIRMED: YES / NO / UNRESOLVED**

And one additional question:

**NEXT STABLE STATE: UNKNOWN — WHAT EVIDENCE WOULD CHANGE THIS?**

That was enough to change everything, though not in the way a dramatic announcement might suggest. The men had acquired a way to disagree without forcing one archive to disappear. A measurement could be challenged without insulting the man who had taken it. An old shirt could be retained without treating growth as betrayal. A new baseline could be pursued without pretending the previous one had never existed.

The younger man who had spoken about his gloves asked whether the group could inspect the storage tent later. The older man said he would show him the cedar cases. Tyler offered to bring the measurement sheets so the two records could be compared. Andrew, who had arrived carrying a small portable speaker and a notebook, asked whether they were finished talking long enough to walk the marked area by the trees. The speaker remained switched off. He had learned that the men sometimes needed a little quiet before the next experiment.

Luke glanced at Andrew. “What did you find?”

“Not sure yet.”

“Then why are you carrying it?”

“Because I might.”

The answer was so completely Andrew that several of the men laughed. The older man from the Continuity family looked at the speaker as though it might be another artifact whose importance would become clear later. Andrew set it on the table and opened his notebook to a sketch of the clearing. He had drawn the redwoods as dark vertical bars and the men as little circles at different distances. Between them he had marked the table, the old shirt case, and the narrow crack in the soil.

“What is this?” Tyler asked.

“The distances between the groups yesterday. And this morning.”

“You measured them?”

“Some of them. The rest are estimates.”

“Then mark the estimates.”

Andrew had already done so. He had used a solid line for measured distances and a broken line for approximate ones. Tyler nodded. The same discipline was beginning to travel between their different interests. Andrew's unusual attention to sound and place was not being treated as a distraction. Tyler's insistence on measurement was not being treated as an attempt to flatten the world. Each method could make the other more useful.

The morning continued. One group walked to the storage tent with the old shirt case. Another remained near the redwood, checking the soil marks. Luke moved between them, not acting as a mediator in every conversation but joining whichever exchange needed another person. At the tent, he helped the younger man open a cedar cabinet. Inside were folded garments, old shoes, two belts, and a small bundle of handwritten notes. The smell of cedar was strong, mixed with dry cotton and paper. The younger man found a pair of gloves he remembered from his first training season. He held them in both hands, examining the worn fingertips and the faded stitching.

“You don't have to keep them,” the older man said.

“I know.”

“You can keep them because you remember what they felt like.”

The younger man nodded. He placed them on the shelf rather than putting them back in the cabinet. The older man let him decide.

At the clearing, the Escalation family had assembled a different display. They placed their current instruments beside the earlier ones, showing how the measuring process had changed. One device had a worn grip where a thumb had rested for years. Another had been replaced because its calibration had drifted. They did not pretend the old readings were exact when the instrument itself was no longer reliable. Tyler examined the references and found that one sequence had been adjusted after a change in equipment. The correction had been properly recorded. That impressed him more than a perfect-looking graph would have.

By late morning, the families had found a way to work together without pretending they had always agreed. They would keep their own archives, but they would share the reference conditions for future tests. A measurement taken by one family could be compared with a measurement taken by the other if the method, position, and instrument were recorded. A garment could remain with the family that had preserved it, while an image of the garment's dimensions could be linked to the shared chronology. The arrangement did not make the records identical. It made them readable across the difference.

Luke stood beneath the trees and watched the men talk. He had not ended the rivalry; he had given it a form that could continue without making the men enemies. The group was still interested in scale. They still wanted to know how far the Serum might take them, how quickly the next threshold could arrive, and what kind of place they might need to build around it. But now the question included the person who would live inside the new body and the friends who would remember the earlier one.

In the afternoon they tried the new arrangement on an ordinary training session. The two families moved to a flat stretch of ground beyond the clearing, where the soil was firm and the trees stood far enough apart to allow a full range of movement. They brought no formal equipment beyond the measuring tape, the old gloves, and a small set of markers. The idea was to preserve the distinction between what had been observed and what the men expected to happen. One man would complete a familiar sequence of movements; another would record the current baseline; a third would write down the practical conditions that made the movement possible.

The younger man from the Escalation family began with a simple overhead reach. He stood with his feet shoulder-width apart and raised both arms until his hands were above his head. His shirt pulled slightly beneath the arms, and he paused when he felt the fabric catch. He had performed the same movement many times before, but his shoulders had broadened enough that the old range no longer felt natural. He lowered his arms and tried again more slowly, adjusting the angle of his elbows.

Tyler watched from the side. “Don't force the last part.”

“I can reach it.”

“That's not what I said.”

The younger man looked at him, then tried again without pushing. His hands reached the same height, but the movement was smoother. Tyler recorded the difference. The new baseline was not simply a larger measurement. It included a different route through the movement, a change in how the body was using the available space.

The older man from the Continuity family watched and then brought the old gloves from the cedar case. He held them out. The younger man took them, fitting the worn leather over his fingers. They were too small now. The seams pressed against his knuckles and the palm sat shallow. He turned his hand, looking at the faded stitching.

“I remember these,” he said.

The older man nodded. “You caught your first clean pass in them.”

“I dropped the first three.”

“You dropped the first six.”

The younger man laughed. “You said it was three.”

“I was being kind.”

The laughter spread through the group. The young man held the glove a moment longer, then placed it on a folded cloth beside the new measurement sheet. He did not put it away. The older man showed him how to note the original leather size and the places where the seams had worn through. Tyler added the current hand measurements beside it, not as a replacement, but as the next entry.

Andrew came over carrying a ball. “Can we use the glove?”

The younger man looked at the old one.

“Not that one,” the older man said. “It's finished.”

“Could we use the new one?”

“That's why I brought it.”

The young man from the second family had a new glove in his bag. It was larger, with a deeper pocket and a wrist strap that could be adjusted as his hand changed. He pulled it on and closed his fist. The leather creaked. He turned toward Andrew, who tossed the ball gently. The catch landed in the pocket with a low, clean sound.

“Again,” Andrew said.

The second throw came slightly to the left. The younger man shifted his feet and caught it. His stance was more open than before. Andrew moved closer and showed him how to position his glove without turning the shoulder too early. Tyler watched the line of the movement. The older man watched the younger man's expression. The drill was simple, but it brought the two doctrines into contact with ordinary activity. The old glove was preserved because it mattered. The new glove was used because the man still had to play.

Luke stood a few steps away, watching the men work. He looked at the two gloves on the cloth. One carried the memory of a first successful catch; the other allowed the next one. Neither made the other obsolete. He saw the same relationship in the altered shirt he wore. The older seam remained inside the newer stitching. It was not necessary to choose between them.

The older physician joined him.

“Is this what you meant?” he asked.

Luke nodded. “A man shouldn't have to forget what got him here to find out where he can go.”

The physician watched the practice. “Nor should he have to remain where he was.”

“No.”

They stood quietly while the younger man caught another throw. The ball struck the pocket cleanly. Andrew nodded, then changed the angle. The next throw was higher. The young man reached for it and caught it without losing his balance. A few of the others applauded, and he laughed at the attention.

“That's the current baseline,” Tyler said.

Andrew looked over. “It was a catch.”

“It's both.”

The younger man removed the glove and examined the pocket. He asked Andrew to show him the adjustment again. Andrew did, then asked him to try it on his own. They repeated the movement until the new glove fitted his hand more naturally. The old one remained on the cloth, its worn shape undisturbed.

Later, the families returned to the table and added a practical note to the two-doctrine agreement. Retained objects would be stored with their original context: who had used them, when, and what they had meant at the time. Current measurements would be kept with their method and conditions. When an object could no longer be used safely, its role would change from equipment to record. A new object could be introduced without treating the old one as a failure. The agreement did not require every man to preserve everything. It asked only that the men be deliberate about what they chose to keep and honest about what they chose to replace.

The younger man placed the old glove in the cedar case himself. He wrote a short note and folded it around the wrist strap. He did not write a number first. He wrote: **THE FIRST CATCH I KEPT.** Then he added the date and his name. The older man read the note and nodded.

“That will do.”

The two families signed the shared reference procedure separately. They kept their own archives, but the methods could now be compared. The Escalation family did not have to begin retaining every shirt, and the Continuity family did not have to abandon its garments for a digital ledger. Both had agreed that a person deserved to remain visible through the changes, and that the next state deserved to be approached with enough care to become stable.

They ate together before leaving the training ground. The meal was simple: bread, sliced meat, apples, and coffee poured from a thermos into cups that did not match. The men sat on the low timber rail, leaving the centre clear for the equipment. The younger man kept the old gloves beside him, while the new pair rested across his knees. The older physician asked whether he intended to use the new ones in the next session.

“Yes,” the younger man said. “But I'll keep these.”

“Good.”

The man from the Escalation family looked at the old glove. “I used to think keeping something meant I was afraid to move on.”

The physician shook his head. “Sometimes it means you know where you came from.”

“And sometimes?”

“Sometimes it means you have a cupboard full of things you don't need.”

The younger man laughed. “So how do I know?”

“You decide what the object means to you. Don't let the cupboard decide.”

Luke listened while he ate. He liked the way the older man had refused to turn preservation into a rule that applied to everything. A life could not be made more continuous by keeping every discarded object. Nor did a man prove that he was progressing by throwing away all evidence of the past. The value of a record depended on the person who could use it, and the value of a change depended on what it made possible.

Andrew passed a cup to Tyler, who had forgotten to take one. Tyler accepted it and shifted along the rail to make room. Their shoulders touched briefly. Neither man moved away. The two families were still distinguishable by their habits, but the men had begun to share ordinary gestures: passing food, checking equipment, asking who needed a lift back to the camp. The disagreement had not disappeared. It had stopped being the only thing they knew about one another.

Before they left, Tyler read the final shared procedure aloud. The men confirmed that each family would retain its own archive and that future comparison would preserve the source, method, date, and current status of every measurement. The previous baseline would remain visible. The current baseline would be identified. The next stable state would stay a question until evidence changed it. Any retained object would carry a note about its original use and owner. Nobody objected.

The younger man put the gloves into the cedar case himself. The older physician helped him fold the cloth around them, then let him close the lid. They carried the case back together. It was not heavy, but they shared the weight anyway.

The next stable state remained unknown. The first stable state had not been forgotten. Between them, the men had built a record that could hold both.

Because once the question was written down, somebody would eventually answer it. And Luke, already inside the Serum, knew better than anyone that the answer would not remain theoretical for long.
