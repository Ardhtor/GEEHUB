# CHAPTER XXXVIII — THE UNIVERSITY OF THE LONG TABLE

The university began with a dining table.

The architects had expected the first major decision to concern the lecture rooms, the gymnasium, or the library. Instead, the residents who had been invited to plan the institution spent most of their first meeting discussing where people would eat. They had learned from the House of Returns that a building's social life was not an accessory to its official purpose. People became colleagues in the spaces where they could remain after the work was finished.

The university was therefore planned around a central commons: a long dining room with a kitchen at one end, a library passage at the other, and several smaller rooms opening onto a sheltered courtyard. The table would be built from oak, with sections that could be joined or separated according to the gathering. There would be room for students, instructors, visiting researchers, maintenance staff, and people who had no formal reason to be there but wanted to take part in a conversation.

The name came from the table itself. The University of the Long Table was not intended to be a joke. It described the institution's first principle: knowledge should have somewhere to sit.

The academic programme developed in parallel with the buildings. The first schools were architecture and construction, physical education and movement, engineering and fabrication, history and media, and the study of living systems. The curriculum was practical without being narrow. Students learned how a structure carried weight, how a body adapted to repeated activity, how a city organised its routes, how images preserved motion, and how an account changed when another person entered it.

Dan Roman was invited to contribute to the movement programme after the architects saw his gymnasium sketches. He did not become the head of the department. He preferred to help establish the training floor, the equipment standards, and a series of practical sessions on technique, coordination, strength, and recovery. His first proposal was to make the room useful to people at different levels of experience. He wanted beginners to feel welcome without making advanced training less demanding.

The Iron Room became the university's first shared facility. Its platforms were checked, the racks were reorganised, and a wide central lane was kept clear for instruction and movement. High windows admitted soft daylight. The ventilation system was designed to cope with a full room, and the storage area was divided so that equipment could be maintained without blocking access. The building was large, but it did not feel empty. People used it throughout the day.

The dormitories opened in stages. The first residence housed thirty students in a mixture of single rooms and shared suites. Each floor had a common lounge, a laundry, a quiet study room, and a small kitchen. The ground floor connected to the dining hall through a covered passage, while the upper balconies looked toward the gardens and the distant redwoods. The design encouraged people to leave their rooms without requiring them to surrender privacy.

The first residents arrived with boxes, books, training shoes, coats, musical instruments, and the small objects that made unfamiliar rooms bearable. They learned the routes to the library, the workshops, the dining hall, and the gardens. The grey cat began visiting the lower dormitory steps. The black cat preferred the workshop court, while the ginger tom had established a reliable position beneath the long table.

The university's first week was not orderly. A delivery of books arrived at the wrong entrance. The kitchen ran out of coffee during an evening seminar. Two students disagreed about the use of a workshop bench, and a visiting instructor discovered that the lecture room had been booked for a rehearsal. The problems were ordinary, and the institution became more convincing because people had to solve them.

Luke visited on the fourth evening. The commons was full. A group of students were comparing sketches of the new fabrication shop. Near the windows, two instructors were discussing a book on architecture. Dan was showing a resident how to adjust a lifting platform, while Joseph helped a student find a quiet place to work. Bill had brought a tray of sandwiches from the kitchen and was insisting that the people who had missed dinner should eat before returning to their projects.

Luke stood at the end of the table.

Nobody stopped what they were doing to greet him. He liked that. The place had begun to function without requiring him to be its centre.

The Hub recorded the first completed university day. Its entry included the classes held, the rooms used, the meals served, the repairs requested, and the conversations that had continued after the formal sessions ended. It also recorded the spaces that had not yet found their purpose: an upper-floor studio, a small room beside the library, and a covered terrace that had become popular despite having no official designation.

At the end of the week, the university held its first open supper. The kitchen served roast vegetables, a large pot of bean stew, fresh bread, and baked apples with cream. The Long Table was extended to its full length. Students sat beside instructors, builders beside librarians, and residents beside people who had only arrived that morning.

Dan sat near the middle, listening to a discussion about the roof of the next dormitory. He made one suggestion, drew a quick diagram on a napkin, and passed it along. The drawing travelled down the table, gathering annotations as it went.

By the time it reached Luke, it had become a proposal.

The Hub saved the napkin as an architectural artifact.

The University of the Long Table had opened without a grand ceremony. Its first achievement was not a building or a programme. It was a table at which people who had not known one another could sit long enough to begin making something together.

## The table after supper

Long after the plates had been cleared, the university remained awake in small, separate pockets. A few students carried the architectural napkin to the studio and pinned it beside the elevation drawings. Two instructors continued their argument about the library's upper windows, but the argument had softened into a shared attempt to find the right angle. In the kitchen, someone was wrapping the remaining bread for the morning. The smell of baked apples lingered in the hall, joined now by coffee and the faint mineral scent of rain beginning outside.

Dan returned to the Long Table to collect his notebook. The room looked different without the crowd, though the evidence of the gathering remained: a pale ring from a glass, a crumb caught in the grain, a chair left slightly out of line. He set the notebook down and traced the route of the proposed roof beam with one finger. The drawing had become more precise because other people had touched it. He thought of the cup on the balcony, the cat beside the planter, and the way Joseph had made room for him in conversation without making a ceremony of it. A university, he realised, was not only a place where knowledge was delivered. It was a place where people could remain in one another's company long enough for an idea to change shape.
