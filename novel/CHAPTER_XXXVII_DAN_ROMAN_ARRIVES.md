# CHAPTER XXXVII — DAN ROMAN ARRIVES

Dan Roman arrived on a Thursday with a canvas holdall, a dark coat, and the composed expression of someone who had travelled far enough to be glad of a proper chair.

He was a fictional newcomer to the settlement, introduced through the Dad Convent's residential exchange. He was Romanian, broad through the shoulders, athletic without looking assembled for display, and carried himself with an easy physical confidence. His dark hair was neatly kept. His face was handsome in a direct, unstudied way, with a strong brow, clear eyes, and a smile that appeared most readily when somebody else was telling a story. He had a substantial build, a powerful back and chest, and the relaxed posture of a man who knew how to move through a room without demanding that everyone notice him.

The first person to meet him was Bill, who had been sent to collect a delivery and had mistaken Dan's holdall for a package that needed signing. The misunderstanding lasted long enough for both men to laugh about it. Bill took him to the House of Returns, where lunch was still being served.

Dan listened more than he spoke. He asked where the workshops were, whether the university had a library, and how far the nearest town lay beyond the trees. He did not ask for a tour of the most impressive buildings. He wanted to know where people actually spent their afternoons.

Joseph showed him the courtyard and the covered passage leading toward the residential wing. The black cat crossed their path, paused to inspect Dan's shoes, and continued toward the workshop. Dan watched it go.

“Does that one belong to somebody?” he asked.

“Not officially,” Joseph said. “It has chosen the place.”

Dan considered this. “A sensible arrangement.”

His room was in the eastern residence, on the second floor, overlooking the gardens. It had a broad bed, a desk, a wardrobe, and a deep windowsill. The room was not luxurious, but the timber was well finished and the window opened onto a quiet view. Dan set his holdall down, inspected the fittings, and thanked the maintenance worker who had prepared it. Before unpacking, he opened the window to let the afternoon air through.

At dinner, he met Luke and Tyler. Conversation began with the roadworks and moved toward the university district, where a new programme was being planned around physical education, engineering, architecture, and the study of movement. Dan had experience with practical training and an interest in how bodies adapted to demanding work. He spoke about the importance of technique, recovery, and a good training environment, and he was careful not to pretend that one method suited everyone.

Tyler liked him immediately.

The next morning, Dan visited the Iron Room. He took time to understand the layout before using the equipment. He checked the platforms, asked about maintenance, and noticed that the storage area had been designed for a narrower range of equipment than the room now contained. Rather than complain, he offered to help reorganise it. By midday, he and two residents had moved the racks, marked a clear circulation route, and created a separate area for lighter mobility work.

The change was small, but it made the room easier to use.

Dan's presence became familiar through repeated practical encounters. He helped carry materials at the Convent Way site, joined the gardeners in moving a stone bench, and spent an evening in the House of Returns listening to Bill explain the history of the kitchen's increasingly complicated soup recipe. He was not assigned a role immediately. The settlement allowed him to discover where his skills were useful and where he preferred to spend his time.

On Saturday, Joseph found him in the courtyard with a notebook. Dan was sketching the proportions of the new university gymnasium, particularly the roof trusses and the arrangement of the upper windows.

“You've been here three days,” Joseph said.

“I know.”

“And you're already redesigning the building?”

“Only the light.”

Joseph looked at the drawing. Dan had placed the windows high enough to bring daylight across the training floor without exposing the residents to direct glare. He had also left room for ventilation and a maintenance walkway.

“Show this to the architects,” Joseph said.

Dan closed the notebook. “I thought I might ask them first.”

The Hub created his first inhabitant record that evening. It did not reduce him to measurements or a list of physical traits. It recorded his arrival, his chosen room, his first conversations, the work he had volunteered for, the questions he had asked, and the design observation that had begun to influence the university plans.

Dan Roman was now part of the settlement's continuing account.

At dusk, he stood on the eastern balcony with a mug of coffee. The grey cat appeared on the lower steps, looked up at him, and settled beside a planter. Dan raised his mug in a small greeting.

The cat remained.

For the first time since his arrival, Dan looked entirely at home.
