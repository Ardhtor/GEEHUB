# CHAPTER XXXVI — THE ROAD TO THE CONVENT

The road to the Dad Convent began as a practical disagreement about deliveries.

The Facility had grown beyond the old service entrance. The House of Returns was receiving timber, food, furniture, books, and the occasional crate of equipment, while the western university district had begun accepting its first residents. The existing road passed too close to the quiet gardens and narrowed at a bend beneath the redwoods. Trucks could manage it, but they disturbed the residential rooms, and the gardeners had begun to complain that the turning area was destroying the roots of an old tree.

Kirk brought the problem to Luke with a map, a delivery schedule, and three possible routes. Tyler had already walked the ground. Joseph had spoken with the residents whose windows faced the road. Bill had arrived with coffee and a list of meals that would need to be moved if the kitchen entrance changed.

Nobody treated the road as a line on a map. It was a daily habit that had become inconvenient.

The proposed answer came from the Dad Convent, whose builders had been extending their wooded estate through a sequence of workshops, halls, residential wings, and service buildings. Their approach was different from the Facility's. They did not begin by asking which road would solve the immediate problem. They asked what kind of settlement the road was supposed to serve ten years from now.

A delegation arrived on a clear morning. Luke recognised the familiar manner of men who had spent years making large plans survive contact with actual ground. They inspected the slope, the drainage, the mature trees, and the existing foundations. They asked where the deliveries were coming from, where the residents walked, where the cats crossed, and which buildings were likely to be added next.

The last question changed the meeting.

The western district was not going to remain a university and two dormitories. There were proposals for a dining hall, a library, a fabrication school, a covered training court, and a row of small residences for visiting instructors. The Dad Convent builders recommended a service road that curved behind the workshops, a pedestrian avenue that followed the higher ground, and a planted buffer between the two. The plan required more work at the beginning, but it would not force the settlement to rebuild its circulation every time another building appeared.

Tyler studied the cross-section. “The drainage is doing half the design.”

“More than half,” one of the builders replied. “If the water has nowhere to go, the road will tell you what you forgot.”

They spent the afternoon marking levels. The crew set temporary stakes, checked the fall, and moved the proposed footpath away from a cluster of roots. Joseph walked the route with two residents from the dormitory wing. They wanted a sheltered section where people could meet without standing in the delivery lane, and a place to sit where the evening sun reached the trees.

The revised plan included both.

The road was named the Convent Way, though nobody could agree whether the name referred to the people who had designed it or the settlement it would eventually connect. The Dad Convent builders did not mind. They were more interested in the first foundations.

Work began with drainage channels and a retaining wall. The stone arrived in separate loads, sorted by size. The carpenters built forms for the culverts, and the surveyors checked the levels before the concrete was poured. The work was not spectacular. It was the sort of construction that made later construction possible.

At the end of the first week, the grey cat appeared beside the new pedestrian route. It sat on a stack of paving stones while the crew cleared the work area. One of the builders placed a small board nearby so the animal would not have to cross the wet ground.

“Good omen?” Kirk asked.

“Good inspector,” Tyler said.

The Hub recorded the exchange under local customs, construction safety, and the developing relationship between the Facility and the Dad Convent. It also added a new map layer: routes intended for people, routes intended for vehicles, and routes that belonged to neither.

The Convent Way was still unfinished when the first residents began using it. They walked the compacted surface in the evening, carrying coffee from the House of Returns and watching the lights come on in the workshops. Beyond the trees, the Dad Convent's Great Hall was nearing completion. Its heavy oak beams were visible above the roofline, and a row of windows caught the last light.

Luke stood at the junction and looked from one settlement to the other.

“We're building a place that can keep growing,” he said.

The oldest builder nodded. “Then build the road as if you mean to come back.”

That sentence became the first inscription in the Convent Way ledger.
