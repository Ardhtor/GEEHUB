# CHAPTER XXXV — THE CITY WITH CATS

The first cat appeared on the western steps at sunrise.

It was a small grey animal with a white chest and a torn edge to one ear. It sat beneath the stone balustrade and watched the gardeners unload their tools. Nobody knew where it had come from. The grounds were enclosed, but the gates were not sealed, and the old service roads connected the Facility to several settlements beyond the trees.

A kitchen porter placed a saucer of water near the steps. The cat approached after the porter had gone inside, drank, and settled beneath the bench as though it had been assigned the place.

By the end of the week there were three cats.

The second was black and moved through the gardens with the confidence of an animal that had already inspected every route. The third was a large ginger tom who appeared at the House of Returns during lunch and took up residence beneath the oak table. He did not beg. He simply waited until the room had accepted him.

The inhabitants began to treat the cats as a sign of good luck. It was an old custom in some of the towns beyond the Facility, and the idea travelled quickly. A cat sleeping on a windowsill meant that the room was settled. A cat crossing the courtyard meant that the day had not yet decided what it would become. A cat choosing a workshop was taken as an endorsement of the work being done there.

The Hub attempted to classify the phenomenon. Its first record was practical: three animals, distinct appearances, repeated locations, observed feeding behaviour. Its second record was social: inhabitants had begun leaving water in sheltered places and keeping the courtyard doors open when weather permitted. Its third record was more difficult to quantify. The presence of the cats changed the pace of the buildings. Men paused before closing doors. Conversations softened when an animal settled nearby. The gardeners began to take the longer route because it passed the warm stones where the grey cat liked to sleep.

The House of Returns acquired a small shelf near the kitchen entrance for food and supplies. The kitchen staff established a rule that no animal would be fed unsuitable scraps, and the gardeners made a sheltered place beneath the eastern wall. Nobody claimed ownership of the cats. Their routes remained their own.

The Other Side sent an account of its own animals. In that town, cats were said to appear before a new building became properly inhabited. They were not treated as supernatural messengers, but as sensitive occupants who noticed changes in a place before the people did. When a cat chose a doorstep, the residents took it as a reason to look more carefully at the building: perhaps the threshold was warm, perhaps the light was good, perhaps the street had become quiet enough to rest.

The two communities compared their records and found that their customs were similar without being identical. The Mexiancas added a note to the Hall of Accounts: a repeated sign did not need a single origin to become meaningful.

Luke found the grey cat in the archive corridor one afternoon. It had climbed onto a low shelf and curled itself beside a box of old photographs. He moved the photographs to a higher shelf and left the animal undisturbed.

Tyler arrived with coffee. “It has chosen the archive.”

“Apparently.”

“Does that mean we're lucky?”

Luke looked at the cat, then at the rows of records. “It means we should keep the place comfortable.”

The answer was entered into the Hub's local customs file.

As the season changed, the cats became familiar inhabitants of the grounds. The ginger tom preferred the dining room. The black cat took the long route around the workshops. The grey one moved between the archive and the western steps, occasionally disappearing for a day and returning without explanation. New cats appeared in the lower gardens, and the residents learned to distinguish them by their habits rather than by assigning them names too quickly.

The city beyond the Facility began to notice. Shopkeepers left shallow bowls near their entrances. The university dormitories built sheltered ledges beneath the windows. The construction crews designed a few protected corners into the new residential blocks, not as ornamental features but as places where animals could pass through without being trapped.

The cats did not make the city good. They made its existing kindness visible.

One evening, as the lamps came on along the western road, the Hub received a new image from the Other Side: a cat asleep on a chair beside a window. The room behind it was unfamiliar. The chair was unmistakably the second chair.

The accompanying note read:

THE HOUSE IS LEARNING WHO MAY REST IN IT.

Luke saved the image beside the first account. The second chair remained warm, though no one was sitting there. Outside, the grey cat crossed the steps and disappeared into the gardens.

For the first time, the Facility's map included a route marked not for people, deliveries, or machinery, but for cats.

It was the smallest route on the map.

It was also the one nobody proposed removing.
