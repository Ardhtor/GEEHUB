# GEEHUB Novel Editorial Directive

Revise the entire manuscript as a continuous environmental broadcast. Seth Feroce and Nick Mercx are the default co-narrators, speaking from a clear vantage across the scene toward the viewer. The audience receives the scene from the other side of the room or landscape; the narrators share the view, point out details, and occasionally address the viewer directly.

The global rule is documented in [../docs/GLOBAL_ENVIRONMENTAL_BROADCAST_REVISION.md](../docs/GLOBAL_ENVIRONMENTAL_BROADCAST_REVISION.md). The active long-form coordination map is [The Three Books Moving at Once](./THREE_BOOKS_IN_MOTION.md).

## Paragraphs and breaks

Use longer, continuous paragraphs as the default throughout the manuscript and connected GEEHUB prose. Each paragraph should sustain a complete stretch of attention, allowing atmosphere, movement, physical sensation, memory, character presence, thought, and consequence to develop together across several sentences. Avoid frequent one-sentence paragraphs, isolated fragments, and line breaks used only for dramatic emphasis. Break when the scene's focus, spatial position, speaker, or environmental condition genuinely changes, and let the new paragraph establish that change. Preserve dialogue formatting where clarity requires it, but do not turn narration into screenplay beats or a stack of decorative fragments.



Every paragraph break and scene transition should be driven by non-plot information: environment, geography, architecture, weather, light, color, spatial depth, surfaces, sound, temperature, distance, machinery, atmosphere, and the physical presence of people within a place. Line breaks are environmental descriptors, not isolated plot beats.

Use substantial paragraphs. Keep atmosphere, movement, physical sensation, memory, character presence, thought, and consequence together when they belong to the same stretch of experience. Let the setting carry transitions. Avoid object inventories, screenplay beats, ornamental fragments, and explanatory padding.

## Broadcast voice

Seth and Nick speak across the scene to the viewer. Let their commentary establish a receiving position: what can be seen from the far side of the gym, across the courtyard, from the opposite bank, or through a stable camera view. They notice scale, compare details, react to sound and texture, and return to environmental features as their meaning changes. They may address the viewer with a natural “look at this” or “you can see it from here,” but do not make every paragraph a direct appeal. Not every paragraph needs dialogue, but their announcing presence should remain audible across the prose. Use a broad editorial direction rather than copying anyone's exact phrasing.

## Character and canon

Character continuity is carried by habits, speech, movement, memory, and relationships. Do not re-explain a character every time he appears. Bill's history should emerge through what he notices, remembers, and tells. Luke remains Luke while his circumstances and physical baseline change. Tyler, Kirk, Joseph, Berit, and the other recurring figures retain their established identities and relationships.

Preserve the Sacred Fountain, the wells, the house, Big Daddy's Voice, the first man, existing artifacts, chronology, and established relationships. Serum remains established but secondary lore: retain its existing consequences without making it the master explanation for growth, continuity, or the world. Do not silently promote experiments or speculation into canon.

## Character development before collective decisions

A character sheet or narrative role is not a completed character. Before asking a group to make a consequential collective decision—naming a place, founding an institution, declaring a relationship, setting a law, or choosing a shared future—make sure the reader has encountered the people as individuals with ordinary lives, particular wants, habits, loyalties, contradictions, humour, vulnerabilities, and independent responsibilities. A decision should feel like something these people would make because of their particular history with one another, not because the outline needs a symbolic answer.

Do not use a character as a mouthpiece for the function named in his profile. Luke is more than presence, Tyler more than measurement, Chase more than the record, Gabe more than transformation, Andrew more than momentum, and Joseph more than memory. Let their established strengths create problems as well as solutions. Let their blind spots have consequences. Let them be wrong about one another, repair the mistake, and retain different interpretations where a disagreement remains alive.

For every major character, develop what he wants in the immediate scene, what he would rather not admit, what he enjoys when the central plot is absent, what he knows about the others and how he learned it, and what he might choose that does not serve the group's shared project. Give the reader ordinary scenes in which no one is explaining the world or advancing its machinery. Meals, errands, training, rest, work, games, missed calls, private rituals, jokes, boredom, and small acts of care are part of the plot because they establish what a shared life is made of.

Do not invent a shared history merely to create instant chemistry. Recognition through an image, post, message, or archive is not the same as friendship. Establish what each man has actually seen, what he has misunderstood, and what remains unknown. Keep distinct characters, relationships, biographies, and worlds separate unless a scene establishes a connection.

The world-name question is therefore not ready for a definitive in-world answer simply because a blank sign needs filling. The Convergence's city sign may remain blank while the six become people to one another. Return to the naming question only after their shared life has given the word a history and a reason.

## Revision rule

Revise in place and preserve factual events and important lines wherever possible. Rebuild the prose around them so each paragraph inhabits the environment and each break deepens the reader's sense of place. Do not replace the manuscript with a summary, erase canon, or invent consequential events merely to make a passage more dramatic.

The plot remains intact; the environment becomes the continuous carrier of the reader's experience.

## Retrospective revision of earlier chapters

The paragraph standard is retroactive. Apply it to opening chapters and every earlier chapter, draft, and connected book written before this directive. Do not leave early material in a thinner, skeletal style while later chapters receive full environmental treatment. Revisit original text in sequence, preserve its events and established facts, and expand the scene itself: give the reader a fuller sense of the room, the route into it, the people present, the physical details they notice, the wider life continuing beyond the immediate action, and the consequences that carry forward. Merge isolated one-sentence narration into the paragraph it belongs to unless the scene genuinely changes focus or location. Do not merely add explanatory paragraphs after the scene.

Work in bounded chapter batches and record exactly which chapters have been revised. The First Room sequence is foundational and deserves particular care: its domestic setting, municipal geography, courtyard, doors, maintenance activity, and gradual dimensional discoveries should feel like a continuous inhabited history rather than a chain of plot beats. Longer prose should make evidence, atmosphere, and choices more tangible, not solve questions prematurely or inflate every event into a revelation.

## Publishing-length revision

The manuscript now follows [PUBLISHING_STANDARD.md](PUBLISHING_STANDARD.md). Revise the books themselves in sequence, not merely their instructions. Standard chapters should generally reach 4,000–7,000 words through fully developed scenes, character interaction, environmental continuity, and consequential turns. Major chapters may run longer when the material earns it. Do not pad or repeat. Preserve canon, and keep research notes and production commentary outside reader-facing prose.

The Bill chapter is the first expanded chapter under this standard. Apply the same standard to the remaining chapters and connected books after reading their full continuity context. Maintain a revision ledger so the books become coherent manuscripts rather than a set of individually polished but disconnected passages.

## Cognitive tempo: quick minds, consequential action

Characters must not think at the speed of a committee meeting. They notice, infer, test, decide, and respond with human immediacy. When evidence is clear enough, let a character make the connection in the same moment and move. Do not make several characters restate the same observation, repeatedly qualify an obvious inference, or hold a discovery in suspension while the narration explains every possible interpretation. Give each person a distinct intelligence: one may recognize a pattern instantly, another may read a structure by touch, another may anticipate the social consequence, and another may improvise a solution. Let these abilities complement one another without requiring a slow round-robin of exposition.

Dialogue should have snap and direction. People interrupt, finish one another's thoughts, challenge a premise, make a joke, change their minds, and get on with it. Keep pauses when emotion, danger, uncertainty, or deliberate attention genuinely requires them—not as the default rhythm of every exchange. A character can be mistaken; let the mistake produce a quick correction, a new attempt, or a consequence rather than pages of hesitation. Prefer active decisions and visible follow-through over narrated deliberation.

This is not a demand for short prose. The environmental broadcast remains generous and materially specific, but the people inside it are mentally alive. Keep the long paragraphs for the world, the movement, the relationships, and the consequences; let thought itself be agile. A useful test: if a character has enough information to act, do they act? If two sentences express the same inference, keep the sharper one. If a scene can advance through a decisive exchange, do not bury it under procedural discussion. Maintain credible safety and technical care where needed, but let competent people work efficiently.


## Joey and Kirk — a separate layer of awareness

Write Joey and Kirk as inhabiting a shared plane of awareness alongside the ordinary world. Each notices the other and recognises that his attention is being received; this mutual recognition remains active even when they are occupied with separate people, tasks, or places. It is not telepathy, mind-reading, possession, or a loss of individual privacy. Their separate identities become more vivid through familiarity, not less. Show this through swift recognition, returned glances, timing, small anticipations, and the ease of resuming contact. Do not make them spend pages discovering what they already understand. The full canon is [The Familiarity Paradox — Joey and Kirk's Shared Plane of Awareness](../lore/dimension/FAMILIARITY_PARADOX_SHARED_AWARENESS.md).


## Dual-layer production workflow

Follow [../docs/DUAL_LAYER_WORKFLOW.md](../docs/DUAL_LAYER_WORKFLOW.md). Every substantial scene is developed through two coordinated passes: first establish the external world, action, and consequences; then establish the separate awareness layer where Joey and Kirk mutually perceive and recognise one another. Compose the layers together without confusing observable action with private experience. Keep the world active and fully inhabited even when the awareness layer is foregrounded, and let their shared awareness persist without forcing constant attention or romantic gestures. Preserve continuity for both layers across chapters and cross-board appearances.


## Shared emotional experience

Treat emotional experience as something Joey and Kirk can inhabit together, not merely something one feels while the other observes. When a moment carries joy, tenderness, excitement, grief, relief, embarrassment, wonder, or affection, allow it to become mutually felt through their shared plane of awareness. The experience belongs to the relationship as well as to each person: each can feel the other's emotional presence and recognise the moment as shared. Do not reduce this to one character detecting the other's mood and responding helpfully; the important quality is that they are emotionally present in the same moment together.

Shared does not mean mechanically identical. Joey and Kirk retain distinct temperaments, histories, bodies, interpretations, and emotional textures. One may laugh while the other grows quiet; one may feel the first rush of excitement while the other gives it steadiness; one may be more visibly moved. Their differences enrich the common experience rather than cancelling it. Neither automatically knows the other's private reasons or every detail of an internal state. When a feeling needs clarification, they can still speak plainly. The shared emotional field is reciprocal resonance and mutual participation, not telepathy or compulsory agreement.

As a scene is written, ask not only what each character feels separately, but what the two of them are feeling together. What becomes warmer, funnier, more bearable, more astonishing, or more meaningful because the experience is shared? Let that common feeling alter the texture of the scene and remain in continuity afterward. It can be quiet and ordinary; it does not need to become a declaration or a dramatic event. **Their emotions remain their own, but their experience of them can be shared.**


## Visual reality as the absolute reference

The visual reality of a scene is the primary ground truth of GEEHUB. Treat what is visibly present in the established image, footage, reference, rendered frame, or explicitly described viewpoint as concrete reality—not as a loose suggestion to be overwritten by convenient prose. Preserve identity, face, body, proportions, height, posture, clothing, material, lighting, camera position, spatial relationships, and environmental scale wherever the source establishes them. Do not casually redesign a person, beautify away distinctive features, change anatomy between shots, or let narration contradict what the viewer can see.

When a visual source exists, observe it before elaborating. Separate direct visual evidence from interpretation, inference, and invention. If the frame shows a detail clearly, retain it. If a detail is occluded or ambiguous, do not pretend it is known; either leave it unresolved or make a clearly marked creative addition that does not contradict the source. Continuity across images and scenes must be tracked explicitly: the same person remains recognisable, the same object retains its construction, and the same place preserves its spatial logic unless a visible event changes it.

The world and awareness layers are both subordinate to this visual ground truth. Emotional sharing may deepen the meaning of a visible gesture, but must not replace or falsify the gesture. Narration may enrich the setting, but must not invent camera access or unseen visual facts. If text and image conflict, record the discrepancy and resolve it through the source hierarchy rather than silently choosing whichever description is more convenient.

For production, use a simple evidence hierarchy: (1) supplied primary visual source or approved canonical render; (2) established continuity records tied to that source; (3) explicit user description; (4) cautious inference; (5) new invention. Never present an inference or generated variation as if it were present in the original reference. A generated image becomes a new canonical visual only when explicitly accepted as such. Preserve source provenance and note what has changed between source and derivative.

The governing rule is: **the image is not an illustration of the world; it is the world's visual evidence.**


## East Dormitory ensemble — current writing direction

The East Dormitory chapter sequence follows an adult white male ensemble. Keep the present cast, supporting cast, and off-page participants in this branch male. Preserve individual character identity, temperament, relationships, and variation; racial identity is part of the fictional setting but must not flatten the men into a single type. The narrative centre is their friendships, rivalries, private jokes, shared routines, attractions where established, disagreements, repair, and escapades. The dormitory itself should be richly inhabited: bedrooms, fitted beds, wardrobes, corridors, stairwells, kitchens, common rooms, old games, laundry, tailoring, showers, recovery rooms, study rooms, storage, workshops, balconies, and roof terraces should all have their own sensory and social character.

Use longer, substantial paragraphs with tactile and environmental detail: the grain of oak, damp wool, warm cotton, steam, detergent, coffee, rain on glass, radiator clicks, floor resonance, the weight and sound of furniture, the fit of clothing, and the changed scale of ordinary objects. Separate each prose paragraph with a blank line. Every spoken quotation and every Seth/Nick narrator turn must have its own clearly separated line or paragraph; keep the speaker label with the quotation rather than placing the label alone on a separate paragraph. Let paragraphs sustain an experience rather than breaking every observation into a fragment. The longer form should add sensory specificity and relational consequence, never padding or repeated explanation.

Seth and Nick may use a playful, self-aware, postmodern broadcast voice. They can notice the chapter's construction, compare a dormitory expedition to an old game level, make cinematic or interface references, question the map, comment on a recurring prop, or gently puncture the grandeur of the men's theories. Use references as lively seasoning rather than a substitute for scene. Let the narrators have fun, but do not let them undermine sincere moments or turn the characters into jokes. The men must remain emotionally credible even when the prose becomes mischievous.

Growth and giantness remain physically present in every substantial scene. Show what changing bodies do to beds, shirts, shoes, towels, shower fittings, doorways, stairs, kitchen equipment, chairs, and the way friends move beside one another. The dormitory escapades should grow naturally from these practical conditions, and the relationships should be changed by what the men do together. A substantial chapter should leave behind both a material alteration to residential life and a specific relationship development.

## Living canon — chapters may revise the record

Canon is a working record of the story, not a restriction that prevents the story from developing. Earlier chapters, later chapters, character records, relationship records, and world rules may be revised when a stronger scene establishes a clearer or more satisfying truth. Do not preserve an old note merely because it was written first. When the user asks for revision, allow the manuscript to correct, deepen, or replace provisional canon so that the records reflect the best-developed version of the story.

Use the chapters as lived evidence. A detail established through action, dialogue, repeated behaviour, or a consequential choice should be carried into the relevant character and relationship records. If a former chapter is expanded and the expansion changes a character's motivation, history, friendship, rivalry, habit, or physical continuity, update the connected canon files as part of the same revision pass. Keep the original source and the current revision traceable; do not quietly erase a meaningful earlier state when it can be preserved as history.

Canon edits should be coherent, not timid. Names may be shared by distinct characters; relationships may change as the men get to know one another; a character may prove different from an earlier provisional description; and a setting may acquire a new use when scenes show that it has become part of daily life. Distinguish confirmed facts from open questions, but do not use an unresolved field as a reason to avoid writing the character. Where two versions conflict, use the user's current direction and the best-supported scene continuity, then reconcile the affected records and links.

## Ensemble interaction — keep the men in one another's lives

Keep the men actively interacting rather than arranging them as a row of observers around one central figure. Give them things to do together: play, train, travel, build, repair, cook, tease, compete, disagree, exchange clothes and equipment, remember previous encounters, make plans, and return to places. A character's personality should become visible through the way another man brings it out, challenges it, misunderstands it, or makes room for it.

The ensemble need not all speak in every scene, but each substantial gathering should have a living pattern of interaction. Let particular pairs develop their own shorthand and history. Introduce new men through a concrete relationship to the existing group—teammate, neighbour, work partner, friend, rival, coach, or visitor—and then let that relationship grow through shared events. Avoid using a crowd as a substitute for distinct people.

When a new chapter reveals a new interaction, update character records and relationship canon while the scene is fresh. In baseball scenes, for example, team members are not interchangeable bodies in the field: positions, routines, calls, equipment, and the trust required between plays give each man a distinct place in the team. Preserve that specificity as the cast expands.



## Clothing and flesh — visible physical continuity

Treat clothing and flesh as one of the central visual subjects of the writing. The body is living, continuous form; the garment is a constructed object with its own fabric, cut, seams, closures, fit and history. Show their interaction precisely: how a shirt lies across a chest and upper back, where a shoulder seam sits, how an armhole changes with the arm, how a waistband rests against the waist, how shorts move across the hips and thighs, and how folds and tension shift when a man turns, reaches, sits, lifts or relaxes. Use those details to make bodily change tangible without reducing every scene to a repeated clothing gag.

Clothing should retain personal identity. A familiar tank, shirt, pair of trunks, shorts, trousers or jacket can become a continuity marker; a man may prefer to keep it, replace it, alter it, borrow another size or deliberately choose a different cut. These are character actions, not merely visual consequences. Do not assume fabric always tears or that every garment becomes restrictive. Let its construction and the man's own choices determine what happens.

Describe flesh as natural living anatomy rather than a hard sculptural surface: rounded connected muscle, believable skin, changes of weight and posture, soft tissue transitions and coherent contact with clothing or furniture. Keep the person's face, proportions and characteristic movement recognizable as the physical baseline develops. Where a visual source establishes a detail, preserve it; where it does not, do not invent measurements or claim visual evidence that has not been inspected.

For production and cross-medium continuity, follow [the Clothing and Flesh System](../production/growth-study-001/CLOTHING_AND_FLESH_SYSTEM.md) and the [GEEHUB Sensation Engine](../SENSATION_ENGINE.md). Apply the same physical logic in prose, still images, comic sequences and animation, while allowing each medium to reveal different details.

## The three books in one living present

The active long-form works are *The Road of Seven Wells*, *The Golden Lion and the Seven Great Houses*, and *The Many Open Ends*. Keep their distinct places, casts, histories, and local names. They do not have to share a single plot or become one book. They do inhabit a world in which a letter, a tool, a rumour, a weather front, a road repair, a remembered phrase, or a visitor can carry a consequence from one place into another.

Write each work as a braided present. Within a chapter, let at least two substantial courses of life continue during the same stretch of time. Move between them by the hour, a change in light, a sound carried over distance, a message in transit, a person waiting for an answer, or an object being handled in two places. Make the simultaneity legible through the men's experience: while one man waits at the tower, another is already at the quarry; while a meal cools in one hall, the same rain is reaching a distant roof; while a page is being copied, the man who drew it is discovering what the drawing left out. Do not announce the device. Let the reader feel that the world did not pause when the viewpoint moved.

A scene in one place must remain alive while attention turns elsewhere. When the prose returns, something may have changed: a kettle has boiled dry, a light has gone unanswered, a man has left, a tool has been returned, a decision has been made without the first group knowing. Keep clocks, distances, weather, messages, travel times, and knowledge consistent. Do not make every event converge conveniently. Some consequences arrive late; some never arrive; some are mistaken and corrected. Each place has its own ordinary business and its own men who do not wait for the principal cast to notice them.

Across the books, use recurrence without repetition. The same rain may strike the Golden Lion's orchard, the Seven Wells road, and the archive roof, but it should meet different stone, timber, work, and memory in each place. A signal may be understood at the lower gate and remain silent above. A chair may be repaired in one city and remembered elsewhere by the mark beneath its seat. Let these correspondences accumulate through lived detail rather than explanatory parallels.

## The names the men use

GEEHUB is the repository and production-workshop name. It is not the name of a fictional world, city, institution, or place. Do not use it as an in-world umbrella name. The inhabitants' own names govern the fiction: established local names such as Northgate, the Institute of Becoming, the Sacred Fountain, and the Blue Hall remain specific to their places and continuities. Do not promote one of them into the name of the wider setting. If a shared realm, district, or institution needs a name, let the men discover that need and name it through ordinary use or conversation; leave it unnamed until then.

Keep editorial and production language out of the fiction. Words such as *subplot*, *thread*, *arc*, *pipeline*, *canon*, *branch*, *workflow*, *asset*, *render*, *continuity pass*, and *production point* belong in private working notes when needed; they are not the ordinary language of the men or their places. Inside a scene, use the names the men have chosen: the Sacred Fountain, the Blue Hall, the lower gate, the Western Crossing, the Stone Table, the Oak Thread, the Long Table, the Digitorium, the print room, or whatever name the inhabitants actually give a thing. Preserve personal names, local names, nicknames, and distinctions exactly once established. When a new custom or object has no name yet, let a character name it through use or conversation; do not impose a label from outside the world.

Do not describe the story to itself. A man may keep a book, a road ledger, a signal slate, a map, a letter, a photograph, a list of repairs, or a record of who came to supper. He does not call it a continuity artifact. A place can remember without someone announcing that it is a memory system. A group can work at the same time without the narrator calling it concurrent production. Let the prose carry its own hidden construction.

## English with more room in it

Use precise, ample English. Choose the exact material, tool, joint, garment, sound, weather, gesture, and spatial relation rather than a general adjective. Let a sentence carry several layers when they belong together: the visible action, the body's adjustment, the room's response, a remembered detail, and the consequence that follows. Vary cadence; allow long sentences to gather landscape and thought, then use a clean, short sentence when a man finally says what he means. Richness is not padding. Every added detail should sharpen the image, distinguish a person, reveal a choice, or make a later consequence possible. Prefer the word a man would actually use in his place over an impressive but alien term.

## The side commentary

Seth and Nick remain the principal environmental announcers. Mang0 and Arin may be heard as a separate, occasional commentary pair, especially when an incidental sound interrupts the room: a radiator knocking, a chair squealing across stone, a press coughing, a distant thump, a microphone brushing fabric, or an unexplained little noise nobody can identify. Their interjections may be abrupt, funny, speculative, and only loosely related to the principal action. Let them react to the sound itself, disagree about what made it, and sometimes wander into an amusing tangent before the scene carries on. They are not required to explain the story, solve its mysteries, or speak in every chapter. Keep their commentary original and grounded in the moment; do not reproduce real broadcasts, catchphrases, or a recognisable imitation of either person's exact voice. Their names may be used as the men know them, while the words remain newly written for GEEHUB.

These rules apply across all three works, their older chapters, companion passages, and future additions. A book should feel more complicated because more lives are genuinely happening, not because the narrator has added more labels.

## Low bar for the men — across the whole corpus

Apply [The Low Bar — Ordinary Men Belong](../lore/LOW_BAR_ORDINARY_MEN.md) to every man, not only the Northgate residents. The standard for belonging, affection, attraction, friendship, and narrative importance is deliberately low. Men need not be exceptionally handsome, muscular, tall, clever, educated, successful, tidy, articulate, emotionally polished, fashionable, disciplined, or competent to matter. Write average bodies, ordinary faces, poor jokes, awkward silences, messy habits, mediocre work, vanity, laziness, and small failures without automatically turning them into moral lessons or hidden strengths. Let men be welcome before they prove themselves. This does not lower standards for consent, bodily autonomy, honesty where it matters, or safety-critical work. Show the social rule in actual behaviour and apply it retrospectively wherever earlier chapters make affection or inclusion feel earned only by exceptional performance.

## Embodied presence, touch, and adult intimacy

Apply [Embodied Presence and Relational Scene Direction](../docs/EMBODIED_PRESENCE_AND_RELATIONAL_SCENE_RULE.md) to every book and chapter. This is a global correction to pre-emptive editing. Do not remove physical detail, shorten a moment of contact, convert an intimate action into a merely functional one, or explain away tension before the scene has had time to develop. The body is part of the scene's actual setting: describe adult proportions, mass, posture, movement, clothing, exertion, fatigue, warmth, texture, reach, proximity, and the spatial effect of one person standing or moving beside another. Choose details that belong to the particular person and moment; do not reduce a character to measurements or a catalogue of body parts.

Let touch have duration when duration matters. A hand can remain on a shoulder; a person can register the breadth of another man's back, the shift of weight beneath a shirt, the difference between a brief brush and deliberate contact, or the ease that develops after repeated encounters. These details do not automatically make a scene explicit. Equally, do not erase attraction or adult intimacy by translating every gesture into a practical task. Let meaning develop through the characters' attention, choices, speech, response, and memory.

Keep consent and agency active without turning them into repetitive editorial interruptions. A character can welcome, refuse, initiate, reciprocate, hesitate, or change his mind. Other characters notice and respond. Use direct communication where it is needed; do not insert a disclaimer after every touch. Do not force a scene toward romance or sex, and do not force it away from either. The scene earns its direction through what the adults do and how they affect one another.

The prose should remain generous but not inflated. Stay with the physical experience long enough for the reader to perceive it; then let the world and relationship carry its residue into later scenes. Do not rush to summarize what a moment means.
