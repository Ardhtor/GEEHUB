# THE ROAD OF SEVEN WELLS
## GEEHUB — REWRITTEN OPENING
*A revised manuscript opening*

## Chapter I: The Road of Seven Wells

**SETH:** North of the last city, the road gets quiet. Buildings thin out, fields widen, and the hills take up more of the view than the settlements do. On an ordinary map, it looks like a short trip into the country. Out here, a short line can mean days on foot.

**NICK:** Rain is coming across the open ground. The amber forest sits on the horizon like a wall, and the old survey marks are getting harder to trust. The northern works are somewhere behind the travelers now. They can't see them anymore, even though they haven't gone very far.

Seventeen people left the works: soldiers, porters, two scribes, an old chief physician, and an apprentice carrying a glass vessel beneath his coat. He had been told the vessel was empty. It was warm against his ribs, and its weight seemed to change whenever he paid attention to it. The physician walked at the front with the settled manner of someone whose instructions had been followed for so long that people no longer asked why.

**SETH:** There's no gate closing behind them, no marked border. Just a wet bridge, the sound of rain on the road, and the last tower slipping out of sight.

The apprentice stopped to look back. The northern works had vanished behind the weather.

“Don't measure the distance by what you can see,” the physician told him.

“Then how?”

“By what you have already left behind.”

They continued north. The road crossed three bridges, each one set in a different stretch of country. At the first, a horse seemed a little taller. At the second, its harness no longer sat properly. By the third, the leather hung loose around its body. The soldiers wanted to leave the animal behind. The apprentice argued that it had carried them through the works and belonged with the expedition.

The physician considered him, then looked along the wet road.

“Then walk slower.”

The group slowed down. The road dropped into a wide basin of grass and low stone ridges. Trees stood far apart, and clouds had room to build whole landscapes of their own. A mountain could remain the same size in the view for an entire afternoon. The apprentice began to understand why the old maps left so much blank paper between their marks. The blank areas were not empty. They were places whose scale the surveyors had never managed to describe.

**NICK:** The map isn't necessarily wrong. It's just too small for the job. A line tells you where the road is; it doesn't tell you what it costs to cross the land around it.

The vessel beneath the apprentice's coat grew heavier as they approached the wells. At the first, water ran uphill along a stone channel. At the second, raindrops touched the surface without making ripples. At the third, footsteps sounded below the paving. At the fourth, the travelers saw their own tracks approaching from the opposite direction. The scribes tried to record every detail. The physician ordered them to stop. They carried on writing in secret.

The disagreement stayed with them longer than any single marvel. The physician feared that recording everything without judgment could make dangerous knowledge easy to repeat. The scribes believed that leaving it unrecorded would only guarantee that someone else made the same mistakes. The apprentice listened to both sides and began to think of memory as more than storage: it was a way to keep people and places connected after they had moved apart.

By evening, the expedition had become a row of small figures against an enormous stretch of country. Rain blurred the road behind them. Ahead, the amber forest ran across the horizon, darkening as the light fell. The seventh well lay somewhere beyond the hills.

**SETH:** From here, you can see how far they've come, but not where they're going. The land is doing most of the talking: the open basin, the wet stone, the forest edge, the road narrowing in the distance.

The seventh well had no water. A staircase descended through its stone rim into a chamber damp with condensation. Names covered the walls. Some belonged to members of the expedition. Others belonged to people who had died before the northern works were built. The apprentice found his own name beside a date that had not yet arrived.

He reached toward it. The physician caught his wrist.

“Not yet.”

They climbed out at dawn. The rain had passed, leaving clear bands of sunlight across the northern plain. Valleys and ridges appeared in the distance, and the road split, disappeared, and returned farther away. The travelers had no reliable map for what lay beyond the last surveyed boundary, but they set out anyway. Before noon, a brass key was in the apprentice's hand. He could not remember picking it up.

Days later, they reached a house standing alone in a field. Forest stretched behind it; open country extended on every other side. The building was modest, with a low roof and weathered walls. It looked too small for the vastness around it, but it stood with the quiet confidence of something that had been there long before the roads arrived.

Luke, Tyler, Kirk, and Joseph would later remember the journey differently. Luke remembered three days. Tyler remembered one afternoon. Kirk remembered sleeping beneath a tree that nobody else could recall. Joseph remembered little except that they had walked together. Their accounts did not match, but each man was certain of his own version.

**NICK:** Same road, same group, four different memories. We don't need to explain that away yet. It's simply part of what happened to them.

Inside the house, four chairs stood around a wooden table. A fifth appeared while the men were looking elsewhere. Luke moved toward it, but Tyler stopped him.

“Don't.”

“Why?”

Tyler kept his eyes on the chair. “Because somebody was sitting there.”

The seat was still warm. A cup beside it held water. In the water, Kirk saw a ceiling that did not belong to the room. Joseph rested his hand on the table and heard footsteps overhead.

Upstairs, four beds stood beneath a sloping ceiling. A narrow wardrobe occupied the far wall. Each bed held an object: a brass key, a black stone, a folded map, and a sheet of blank paper. Luke carried the key toward the door, and the hallway seemed to lengthen. When Tyler lifted the stone, the walls appeared deeper. Kirk unfolded the map and found roads around the house that none of them had walked. Joseph watched words appear on the paper and fade before he could read them.

One sentence remained on the back: WAIT UNTIL THEY ARE READY.

When they returned downstairs, a man was sitting in the fifth chair. He did not look surprised to see them. The room seemed to fit him, though he was not especially large. He gestured to the table.

“Sit.”

They did. Through the windows, the field ran toward a distant line of trees. The road they had followed was barely visible now. Beyond the forest lay hills and more country than any of their maps could name. A kettle began to heat on the table, although there was no fire beneath it.

“Who are you?” Joseph asked.

The man glanced toward the staircase.

“I am the part of the story that waited.”

Seven lights appeared across the land beyond the windows. They were small and steady, separated by long stretches of darkening field.

**SETH:** A house in open country. Seven lights in the distance. Four men who don't yet agree on what brought them here.

**NICK:** The place is bigger than the explanation. We'll learn more as they do.

## Revision rule for the remaining manuscript

The rest of the book should follow this treatment chapter by chapter: preserve the people, events, artifacts, and continuity that matter, but make the prose legible on first reading. Let the reader see and hear the place before asking them to interpret it. Keep the uncanny details concrete and restrained. Remove repeated explanations, grand claims, and jargon when an ordinary description can carry the same meaning. Serum remains a minor strand of lore, not the world's master key. Paragraphs should turn with changes in setting, sound, light, distance, architecture, or physical atmosphere. Seth and Nick act as conversational environmental announcers; their presence should clarify the scene rather than make it more obscure.
