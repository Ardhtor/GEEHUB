# The Director of Standards
## The Grand Meridian — an interlude in the second cycle
### Sole Devil Wears Prada carryover: a fictionalized Stanley Tucci

The Grand Meridian had an office devoted to the appearance of perfection. It was not listed on the guest directory, and no porter could give directions to it without first checking whether the person asking had a reason to enter. The office stood behind the east gallery, between the ballroom's wardrobe rooms and the corridor leading to the private dining suites. Its door was finished in dark teal lacquer, with a small brass plaque that read DIRECTOR OF STANDARDS. Inside, everything was arranged with the precision of a room that had never been used carelessly: paper aligned at the edge of the desk, a pair of gloves laid beside a leather folder, a silver pen placed parallel to the grain of the wood, and a vase containing a single white flower. The shelves held guest registers, tailoring books, architectural samples, menu proofs, photographs of the hotel in different seasons, and a series of plans showing the building before it had acquired its present size. The office smelled faintly of cedar, starch, and expensive cologne. It was the one room in the Grand Meridian where the changing geometry seemed to have been anticipated and given a proper filing system.

The man who occupied it introduced himself as Stanley Tucci. He was the hotel's Director of Standards, responsible for the presentation of its public rooms, the appearance of its staff, the quality of its guest experience, and the invisible coordination by which an elaborate establishment could seem effortless. He wore a beautifully cut suit, carried himself with relaxed authority, and spoke with a dry precision that made even a simple question sound as though it ought to have been prepared in advance. He did not hurry, did not raise his voice, and did not appear impressed by the six men who had come to see him. When Luke asked whether he managed the hotel, Tucci corrected him: he managed the standard to which the hotel held itself. The distinction, he said, was important. A building could be managed by schedules and maintenance; a standard required judgment. A lobby could be clean and still be vulgar. A dining room could be expensive and still be badly arranged. A guest could be welcomed and still be made to feel that he had not been expected. The Grand Meridian had survived because its details were consistent, even when the building itself was not.

Tyler asked whether the Director of Standards knew about the weekly recurrence. Tucci removed a page from a folder and set it on the desk. It was a room-service order, dated for a Monday that had not yet occurred. The handwriting was his own, but the order had been completed and signed by a guest whose name did not appear anywhere in the register. Tucci said that he knew the hotel repeated certain arrangements. He knew that some guests returned to the same rooms, that staff occasionally remembered a conversation without remembering when it had taken place, and that the public spaces could acquire the atmosphere of an event that had not yet happened. He did not pretend to know why. His responsibility was to ensure that the hotel continued to function without allowing its uncertainty to become an excuse for poor service. Andrew asked whether he considered the time loop an operational inconvenience. Tucci looked at him for a moment and replied that it was an inconvenience only when someone failed to dress for the occasion.

That answer should have seemed absurd, but the men found themselves looking around the office and noticing how much of the hotel's luxury depended on decisions that appeared trivial until they were missing. The spacing between chairs allowed a man to pass without turning sideways. The table lamps produced warm pools of light without leaving the corners in darkness. The carpets softened footsteps but did not conceal the sound of a door opening. The staff uniforms had been designed to look formal without making movement difficult. Even the flowers in the entrance court were changed before they began to wilt. Tucci showed them a series of photographs documenting the same lobby across several cycles. In each image the furniture was arranged in precisely the same way, but the shadows fell at different angles. In one photograph, the marble floor reflected a much larger room than the one visible above it. In another, the central chandelier appeared to hang thirty feet higher than its chain allowed. Tucci said that he had retained both photographs because the hotel had instructed him, through an unsigned note, not to correct the discrepancy. He had learned that some irregularities were not defects. They were disclosures waiting for the right occasion.

Chase asked whether he had seen Joey. Tucci closed the folder. "I have seen him at breakfast, in the library, at the east gallery, and once in a room that was not technically occupied," he said. "I have also seen what the hotel does when the men turn away from him." The room became quiet. Tucci did not smile, but the expression in his eyes suggested that the question had finally reached a subject he considered worthy of his attention. He explained that the Grand Meridian had a presentation problem. It showed Joey to the guests in a form they could comfortably accommodate, and it allowed the building to absorb the consequences of a much greater presence without making that presence visible. Every time the men looked directly at Joey, the hotel offered them an ordinary-sized man. Every time they looked elsewhere, the true body asserted itself through weight, distortion, sound, and space. The staff had learned not to comment. Some were frightened; others treated it as a feature of the building. Tucci considered both responses insufficient. A standard, he said, was not the same as a denial. One could arrange a room around a difficult truth without pretending the truth was not there.

Luke asked whether Tucci knew why Joey kept them inside the week. Tucci straightened the paper on his desk. "I know that the hotel is very good at making a man feel that an unpleasant fact can be postponed until tomorrow," he said. "It provides excellent beds, excellent food, beautiful rooms, a full programme of recreation, and a concierge who can arrange almost anything. Tomorrow is always available here. That is the trap. The men are not held only by locked doors. They are held by the comfort of believing that they will face the matter later." Gabe said that this sounded less like a time loop and more like a habit. Tucci replied that the finest prisons rarely announce themselves as prisons. Andrew asked whether Joey was the owner. Tucci said that ownership was a legal term and therefore not especially useful. Joey was the authority to whom the hotel's deepest arrangements answered. The Director of Standards could keep the rooms immaculate, the dining service punctual, and the staff composed; he could not overrule the person for whom the hotel had become a sentence.

Before they left, Tucci took a small card from the top drawer and wrote six names on it, followed by a seventh. He placed the card in a silver frame and turned it toward the men. The six names were theirs. The seventh was Joey. Beneath the names, Tucci had written a single instruction: DO NOT REDUCE THE GUEST TO THE FORM IN WHICH YOU FIND HIM. Luke read it once. Tyler asked whether the wording had come from Joey. Tucci said that it had come from the hotel's oldest register, which contained an entry older than the building. Chase asked whether he could photograph it. Tucci permitted one photograph, then returned the card to the drawer. As the men reached the door, he called after them. "One more thing. When the hotel finally shows you what it has been concealing, do not confuse astonishment with recognition. Astonishment lasts a moment. Recognition requires you to change the way you behave afterward." He closed the office door gently. The six men stood in the gallery, surrounded by polished wood, deep carpet, quiet light, and the almost imperceptible sound of a building preparing for another Monday.

## Continuity note

Stanley Tucci is the sole *Devil Wears Prada* carryover, represented as a fictionalized character within the Grand Meridian. No other characters, cast members, plotlines, or workplace relationships from that film are imported. Tucci is the Director of Standards, responsible for the hotel's immaculate luxury and presentation. He is not the devil behind the hotel. Joey remains the controlling authority, the Teal Titan incarnate, and the architect of the men's sentence. This fictional appearance does not make claims about the real Stanley Tucci's private personality or life.
