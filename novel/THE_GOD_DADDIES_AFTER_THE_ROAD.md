# THE GOD DADDIES — AFTER THE ROAD

## GEEHUB — POST-BOOK CYCLE

*Quotidian scenes after Book I*

### I — The Morning After the Ending

     The morning after the book ended was ordinary enough to be almost disappointing. There was rain on the roof, not the supernatural kind, not rain that carried voices or opened wells or remembered the names of men who had been dead for centuries, but plain rain that had fallen during the night and left the yard dark and soft beneath the windows. Luke was the first one awake. He stood barefoot in the kitchen wearing an old shirt and looked through the window for several minutes before deciding that the world was still there. Tyler came in behind him, opened the refrigerator, stared into it, and said, “There's nothing to eat.”

     Luke did not turn around. “There's eggs.”

     “I don't want eggs.”

     “Then don't eat.”

     “That's not helpful.”

     Luke laughed quietly. “You woke up and came downstairs specifically to complain about eggs.”

     “I came downstairs because I was hungry.”

     “Same thing.”

     Tyler shut the refrigerator with his hip and leaned against the counter. Outside, the country went on farther than either of them could see. The old road crossed the field beyond the house and disappeared into the wet trees, and beyond those trees were hills, and beyond those hills were the amber forests, and beyond those forests were places none of them had visited yet. For once, however, nobody was waiting for them there.

     Kirk came downstairs carrying two mugs of coffee. He handed one to Tyler without asking whether he wanted it and kept the other for himself. Joseph followed a few minutes later, still half asleep, and stood in the doorway until Kirk looked over at him.

     “You want coffee?”

     Joseph nodded.

     Kirk poured another cup.

     “You're making too much,” Luke said.

     “Then drink it.”

     Luke took the cup.

     Nobody mentioned the ending again that morning.

     That became the first pleasure of the new country: nothing had to mean anything immediately.

### II — The Walk

     By afternoon the rain had stopped. The four men walked down the road without a destination, which at first felt strangely irresponsible. They had spent so long traveling toward wells, houses, doors, fountains, battles, strangers, and answers that walking merely because the weather was good seemed almost rebellious. Luke carried a stone he had picked up beside the ditch. Tyler kept taking it from him and throwing it farther down the road. Kirk complained that this was making the walk unnecessarily long. Joseph said that was the point.

     They walked for hours.

     Sometimes two of them went ahead and two fell behind. Sometimes Luke and Joseph walked together while Tyler and Kirk argued about something nobody else could hear. Once they stopped beside an enormous cedar because Luke wanted to see whether he could climb it. Tyler told him he would fall. Luke climbed it anyway. He got halfway up, looked down, and said, “It's higher than I thought.”

     “That's what I said.”

     “No, you said I'd fall.”

     “You still might.”

     Luke stayed there for another ten minutes just to make Tyler wait.

     When he finally came down, Joseph was laughing.

     “What?”

     “Nothing.”

     “You're laughing.”

     “Because you climbed a tree to prove Tyler wrong and then discovered Tyler was right.”

     Luke looked at Tyler.

     Tyler smiled.

     “Don't.”

     “I didn't say anything.”

     “Your face did.”

     They kept walking.

### III — Bill Makes Lunch

     Bill had decided that the great problem with the new age was that nobody respected lunch. He announced this while standing in the kitchen with his sleeves rolled up, making sandwiches for everyone and explaining, at considerable length, that civilization had always depended upon people pretending ordinary meals were unimportant while secretly arranging their entire lives around them.

     “You can tell what kind of people they are,” Bill said, “by what they do when somebody says they're hungry.”

     Joseph was sitting at the table.

     “What kind of people are we?”

     “Undisciplined.”

     “Because we're hungry?”

     “Because you're asking when lunch isn't ready.”

     Kirk looked over from the window. “How long?”

     Bill pointed the knife at him.

     “You see? Exactly.”

     Tyler laughed.

     Bill continued preparing lunch while the others drifted in and out of the kitchen. Luke came in twice, stole pieces of bread, and was sent back outside. Joseph washed the cups. Kirk opened the back door because the room was getting warm. Tyler sat on the counter until Bill told him to get down.

     None of this entered the official history.

     Bill considered that a mistake.

     “This,” he said, looking around at the men, “is precisely the sort of thing that disappears first.”

     Nobody answered.

     Bill smiled.

     “So we'll keep it.”

### IV — The Afternoon Game

     The game began because Luke threw an apple at Tyler.

     Tyler caught it.

     “Again.”

     Luke threw another.

     Tyler caught that one too.

     Kirk looked up from the porch.

     “What are you doing?”

     “Playing.”

     “That's not a game.”

     “It is now.”

     Joseph came outside carrying a chair and sat beside Kirk. Bill watched from the doorway with the expression of a man who had already understood that the afternoon was about to become much louder than necessary.

     Within an hour the entire yard had become a contest. The men discovered that they could throw things much farther than ordinary men ought to be able to throw them, and that the landscape itself seemed willing to accommodate whatever foolishness they attempted. A stone thrown by Luke landed on the far side of the field. Tyler threw one farther. Kirk tried and accidentally knocked a dead branch from a tree three hundred yards away.

     Joseph looked at him.

     “Show-off.”

     Kirk shrugged.

     “You did it.”

     “I didn't mean to.”

     “That's worse.”

     Luke began laughing so hard he had to sit down.

     The game eventually became less about distance and more about making one another laugh. Tyler attempted to imitate Luke's serious expression. Luke imitated Bill. Bill imitated Tyler. Joseph discovered that Kirk could not keep a straight face when somebody exaggerated his posture.

     By sunset they were exhausted.

     They lay across the grass in a loose circle, looking up at the enormous sky.

     Nobody had saved the world.

     Nobody needed to.

### V — Evening Clothes

     The next morning Luke complained that everybody was wearing the same three shirts.

     “We need clothes,” he said.

     Bill looked at him.

     “We have clothes.”

     “We have old clothes.”

     “They're clean.”

     “That's not the point.”

     Tyler looked down at his shirt.

     “What's wrong with this?”

     Luke pointed at him.

     “Exactly.”

     “What's exactly?”

     “You don't even know.”

     Kirk laughed from the doorway.

     Joseph was folding laundry on the table. He held up one of Kirk's shirts.

     “This yours?”

     Kirk looked at it.

     “Yeah.”

     “It's inside out.”

     “I know.”

     “Why?”

     “Because I like it that way.”

     Joseph smiled and folded it anyway.

     The afternoon passed in the strange domestic confusion of men who had spent years becoming legendary and now had to decide whose turn it was to wash the towels.

     It turned out that godhood did not eliminate laundry.

     Bill considered this proof that the universe had retained its sense of humor.

### VI — The Long Porch

     Weeks passed without a chapter.

     The men settled into habits. Luke disappeared for hours and came back with stories that were never quite believable. Tyler began repairing things around the house even though nobody had asked him to. Kirk became the one who knew where everyone had left their shoes. Joseph started walking in the mornings and usually returned with somebody else's coat because he had found it somewhere along the road. Bill read, cooked, argued, remembered, and occasionally disappeared into the country for a whole day without telling anyone where he was going.

     They became familiar with one another in the slow way that only ordinary life allows.

     A man learns how another man takes his coffee. He learns which jokes make him laugh and which ones make him quiet. He learns how long silence can last before either of them feels obliged to break it. He learns which chair the other one always chooses, which side of the road he walks on, how he looks when he's tired, how he sounds when he's trying not to laugh.

     These things were more intimate than any prophecy they had survived.

     One evening they sat together on the porch while the enormous country darkened beyond them.

     Luke had his feet on the railing.

     Tyler told him to move them.

     Luke didn't.

     Kirk was reading.

     Joseph leaned against the porch post.

     Bill watched the sky.

     “You know,” Bill said eventually, “we could go anywhere.”

     Nobody moved.

     “I know,” Joseph said.

     Bill looked at them.

     “That's the strange part.”

     Luke lowered his feet.

     Tyler looked toward the road.

     Kirk closed his book.

     The country stretched out before them, enormous and quiet.

     They had finally reached the place where a story could become a life.

     So they stayed for dinner.


### VII — Luke Runs Production

     By the following week Luke had quietly taken over production. Nobody had elected him. There had been no meeting and no announcement. He simply began carrying a notebook, asking where things were, deciding which road they would take, and telling everyone what time they needed to leave. The others discovered that this was how Luke worked: he made the next thing feel obvious. If somebody wanted to go somewhere, Luke knew which car to take. If a scene needed a place, he found one. If the afternoon was empty, he filled it without making the emptiness disappear.

     “What are we making?” Tyler asked one morning.

     Luke looked at him from across the kitchen.

     “The day.”

     Tyler stared.

     “That's not an answer.”

     “Sure it is.”

     Kirk came in carrying coffee. Joseph followed him, still buttoning his shirt. Bill was already at the table reading something old. Luke opened the notebook and showed them a page covered with times, roads, names, and little marks that none of them understood.

     “We're going to town,” Luke said. “Then we're getting food. Then we're fixing the Mustang. Then we're going to the river. Then we'll see.”

     Joseph smiled.

     “You planned all that?”

     Luke shrugged.

     “Somebody's gotta drive production.”

     Outside, the Mustang waited near the fence.

     That became Luke's particular power in the days that followed. He did not control the world by commanding it. He kept things moving. He found the next road. He noticed when everyone had been sitting too long. He decided when lunch was late and when the weather was good enough to go outside. He made little productions out of ordinary life, then acted as though he had done nothing at all.

     Bill eventually noticed.

     “You're directing us,” he said.

     Luke looked offended.

     “Nah.”

     “You have a schedule.”

     “So?”

     “You have assigned seating in the car.”

     “That's because Tyler complains about the window.”

     Tyler looked up.

     “I do not.”

     Luke pointed at him.

     “See?”

     Bill laughed.

     Luke closed the notebook and stood.

     “Come on. We're wasting daylight.”

     They followed him outside.

     The Mustang started on the first try.

     Luke smiled.

     “Good car.”

     And production began.
