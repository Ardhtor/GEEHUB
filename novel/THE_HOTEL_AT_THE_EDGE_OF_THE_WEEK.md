# The Hotel at the Edge of the Week
## A GEEHUB novel
### Book I — The Sevenfold Guest
### Canon status: active development
### Setting: the Grand Meridian, an enclosed all-male hotel
### Governing mechanism: weekly recurrence and metaxic residue

The hotel had no name on its exterior, although every document inside it referred to the building as the Grand Meridian. It stood beyond a long avenue of dark trees, at the end of a road that seemed to approach it from every direction without ever quite reaching the surrounding countryside. Its architecture was difficult to date. The entrance was Victorian, with enormous wooden doors and brass fittings worn smooth by generations of hands, while the central atrium rose through several floors of glass and steel. There were old-fashioned lifts, a modern gymnasium, a swimming pool beneath a vaulted ceiling, a private baseball field, a library with a fireplace that never appeared to consume its logs, and a ballroom whose chandeliers reflected rooms that did not exist elsewhere in the building. The upper floors contained hundreds of bedrooms, each furnished according to the preferences of its occupant, though none of the men could remember having supplied those preferences. Somewhere beneath the hotel were service passages, storage chambers, an underground dining hall, and a locked floor that appeared on the directory only when viewed in a mirror. The Grand Meridian was inhabited entirely by men. Its guests, porters, cooks, engineers, groundskeepers, trainers, receptionists, and administrators all belonged to the same enclosed society, although the men rarely agreed on how long they had been there or whether the distinction between guest and employee meant anything at all.

The week began on Monday, though the men would eventually discover that this was a statement about the hotel's arrangement rather than the passage of time. Monday morning always arrived with the same cool light in the entrance court, the same faint smell of coffee from the dining room, and the same low mechanical hum behind the reception desk. The newspapers carried the same date. The breakfast menu listed the same dishes. The porter polished the same brass rail, and the clock above the lifts showed 7:16 for several minutes before advancing. Yet the building was never quite identical. A chair might be turned toward a window instead of away from it; a book might be open to a different page; a key might be found in a coat pocket where it had not been the previous Monday. These small discrepancies were not remembered by the men as memories. They appeared as impressions, habits, inexplicable preferences, or sudden certainties without a source. The hotel repeated the visible week while something beneath the week retained the pressure of what had happened.

## Monday — The Arrival That Had Already Happened

Luke woke in Room 214 with the peculiar conviction that he had already experienced the day. He could not identify a particular memory, and there was nothing immediately unusual about the room. His clothes were arranged neatly over a chair, his gym bag stood beside the wardrobe, and a glass of water had been placed on the bedside table. The hotel directory lay open to the page describing the swimming pool, although he had no recollection of reading it. He looked at the clock, which showed 7:16, and then at the window. The entrance court was visible below, with its circular fountain and the two stone benches beside the main doors. A large man in a dark jacket was crossing the courtyard, carrying a folded newspaper. Luke watched him disappear beneath the entrance canopy, then noticed something written on the inside of the window, faintly visible where the condensation had gathered. It was only three words: *Don't take the lift.* He wiped the glass with his sleeve. The writing disappeared, leaving no mark behind. A moment later the telephone rang, and a calm male voice informed him that breakfast would be served until ten. When Luke asked who had written on the window, the man on the other end of the line paused before answering, "You haven't asked me that yet."

At breakfast, Luke found Tyler sitting near the windows overlooking the gardens. Tyler seemed entirely comfortable with the hotel, which irritated Luke slightly because the building itself felt increasingly unfamiliar. He had a coffee, a newspaper, and a small collection of keys arranged beside his plate. Chase was seated farther down the room, examining the printed breakfast menu as though it contained an inconsistency. Gabe had taken a table near the fireplace, where he was watching the other guests enter. Andrew was standing at the buffet, a physically imposing Italian man with broad shoulders, muscular arms, and bright blue eyes, holding a plate while studying the arrangement of the fruit. Joseph arrived shortly afterward and greeted Luke with an ease that suggested they had known each other for years, although Luke could not remember their last meeting. Andrew eventually sat down and announced that the hotel's clocks were wrong. Tyler asked which clocks. Andrew said all of them, but particularly the one above the reception desk, which appeared to gain approximately eleven seconds whenever someone entered the lobby carrying luggage. Chase asked whether he had measured this. Andrew opened his notebook and showed them a page of observations, complete with small diagrams of the entrance court and a peculiar drawing of a bell. Nobody understood why he had been watching the clocks, but the notebook contained enough detail to make it difficult to dismiss him. When Luke asked how long Andrew had been staying, Andrew looked at him for several seconds and replied, "That's the question I'm trying to stop asking."

The first attempt to leave occurred shortly before noon. Luke, Tyler, Chase, Gabe, Andrew, and Joseph assembled in the entrance hall, where a porter in a dark green uniform stood beside the revolving doors. They had decided to walk down the avenue and find the nearest town. The porter did not prevent them from leaving, nor did he suggest that they needed permission. He simply asked whether they had checked the weather. Outside, the afternoon was bright and the road was dry. The six men crossed the courtyard, passed beneath the stone archway, and continued toward the trees. After approximately twenty minutes, they reached a small stone bridge crossing a narrow stream. Andrew stopped to examine the water. Chase checked the map on his telephone. Tyler suggested that they had taken the wrong turn, although the avenue had remained perfectly straight. They continued walking. Another twenty minutes passed, and the same bridge appeared ahead of them. Luke recognized the broken stone at its western edge. Gabe recognized a tree whose trunk divided into two branches at shoulder height. Joseph recognized a discarded newspaper lying beside the path. Andrew opened his notebook and found that he had already drawn the bridge, including the exact position of the newspaper. They turned around and walked in the opposite direction. The hotel appeared beyond the trees within minutes, its entrance court visible through the avenue. They tried a different route the following morning, then a service road, then a path that descended behind the gardens. Every route returned them to the hotel. The building was not preventing them from leaving in any ordinary sense. It was arranging the geography so that every departure became another entrance.

That afternoon, the men discovered that the hotel contained an extraordinary number of facilities, each apparently intended for a different kind of life. The gymnasium was large enough to accommodate several training groups, with a raised platform, a row of benches, a free-weight room, and a glass wall overlooking the indoor courts. The swimming pool was quieter, its vaulted ceiling giving every movement a soft echo. The library held books in several languages, including volumes whose publication dates had not yet arrived. The cinema displayed a programme of films that changed when nobody was looking directly at it. The baseball field behind the west gardens had a scoreboard with no team names, only seven columns of numbers. Andrew was immediately drawn to it. He walked the perimeter, inspected the fence, and began recording the way the field returned the sound of a ball thrown against different sections of the backstop. Luke found him there near sunset, writing in his notebook. Andrew explained that the field had a different acoustic character at each hour, and that the scoreboard appeared to record games that had not yet taken place. Luke asked whether he thought the hotel was haunted. Andrew considered the question and said that haunting was an imprecise word for a building that seemed to be keeping its own statistics.

## Tuesday — The First Refusal

By Tuesday morning, the men had begun to suspect that the hotel was doing something to their memories. They could remember their lives before arrival, but those recollections were uneven. Some remembered homes, work, training, friendships, and cities with extraordinary clarity, while others found that the details became vague whenever they attempted to establish a precise chronology. The most disturbing feature was that they all remembered the previous Monday differently, and none of them could agree on what had happened during the night. Luke remembered a conversation with Tyler near the swimming pool, but Tyler insisted that he had spent the entire evening in the library. Chase recalled seeing Joseph enter the ballroom carrying a wet coat, while Joseph said he had never been inside the ballroom. Andrew claimed that they had all met in the basement dining hall and that Gabe had explained the hotel was repeating the week. Gabe did not deny having said it. He simply could not remember. That night, each man returned to his room and discovered something that should not have been there: a small physical remnant of an earlier action. Luke found a key beneath his pillow. Tyler found a damp page torn from a notebook. Chase found a photograph of the six men standing together in the ballroom, although he could not remember the photograph being taken. Gabe found a book open to a passage he had underlined. Joseph found a handwritten note in his own handwriting that read, *You promised to come back.* Andrew found a baseball in his wardrobe, marked with a small blue line. None of the objects explained anything. They were simply evidence that the week had already happened.

The key beneath Luke's pillow opened no door he could find. It was old, made of dark brass, and carried a small circular stamp on its bow. Tyler's damp page contained a rough floor plan of the hotel, but the drawing did not match the official directory. There were two additional corridors behind the ballroom and a staircase descending from the library to a level marked only with a small horizontal line. Chase's photograph showed the six men seated at a long table. They looked tired, and Joseph's chair was empty. In the background, a seventh man stood with his back to the camera, facing a wall. Gabe's book was a volume on medieval architecture, though the marked passage concerned thresholds, doors, and the symbolic function of a passage between one condition and another. Joseph's note had been written in his own hand, but the handwriting was unusually hurried. Andrew's baseball had a blue line running from one seam to another, and when he rolled it across his desk it stopped with the marked line facing upward. He repeated the test six times. It stopped the same way each time. He put the ball in his bag and wrote: *Object remembers orientation.*

The men began to disagree about what they should do. Luke wanted to search the building and establish a practical route out. Tyler wanted to map every floor and compare the physical layout with the directory. Chase wanted to document every anomaly before anything was moved. Gabe believed that the hotel was communicating through indirect impressions and that they should not force an interpretation too early. Andrew wanted to investigate the field and the clocks. Joseph wanted to know who had written the note and why he had promised to return. None of these approaches was unreasonable, but they were not the same project. Their first argument took place in the library, where Tyler accused Chase of treating every object as evidence without considering what it might mean. Chase replied that meaning was exactly what they were trying to establish, and that careless interpretation would make the record useless. Luke told them to stop arguing and choose a corridor. Gabe quietly observed that they were already repeating a conversation. Nobody knew whether he meant that they had said those exact words before or that the disagreement itself had become familiar.

## Wednesday — The Metaxy

On Wednesday, the metaxy first became visible to Luke as a place rather than a feeling. He had gone down to the swimming pool shortly after midnight, unable to sleep. The pool was empty, the water completely still, and the overhead lights had been dimmed. At the far end of the room, where the tiled wall met the glass doors, he noticed a second reflection of himself. It was not delayed in the ordinary way that reflections sometimes appear to be delayed in dreams. It was standing in a different posture, with one hand resting against the glass. Luke turned around, but the room behind him was empty. When he looked back, the reflected figure had moved closer. Behind it, the glass revealed another version of the pool: the same room, but with the lights on and several men sitting around its edge. He recognized Tyler, Chase, Gabe, Andrew, and Joseph. They were speaking to one another, though he could not hear their voices. One of them stood and approached the glass. It was Joseph. He placed his hand against the other side, exactly where Luke's reflection had placed its own. Then the image disappeared. The pool returned to its ordinary appearance. Luke found a small wet handprint on the glass, although the surface on his side was dry. He placed his palm against it and felt, very briefly, the sensation of another hand pressing back.

The metaxy was not another world in the ordinary sense. It was the interval through which the hotel allowed two versions of an event to touch without becoming one event. A reflection could preserve a gesture; a threshold could retain the pressure of a man who had crossed it; a dream could carry a sentence spoken by someone whose waking memory had been reset. The men began to notice that certain places were more permeable than others. The pool's glass wall, the mirrored lift doors, the ballroom curtains, the narrow passage between the library and the west stairwell, and the dark windows of Room 214 all seemed capable of showing something that did not belong to the current Monday. These were not reliable visions. They could not be summoned at will, and they did not provide complete explanations. Sometimes a man saw himself standing in a corridor he had never entered. Sometimes he heard a conversation through a wall and recognized his own voice. Sometimes an object appeared in a room for only a moment before returning to its proper place. The metaxy offered clues, not answers, and the clues often contradicted one another.

The next morning Luke told Tyler what he had seen. Tyler listened without interrupting, then removed the damp page from his desk drawer. It contained a drawing of the swimming pool, with the glass wall divided into two halves. On one side were six figures. On the other side was a single figure, identified by a small arrow as Luke. Beneath the drawing was a sentence: *The room remembers us from the other side.* Tyler had no recollection of writing it. Chase compared the paper with the photograph he had found, and noticed that both carried the same faint watermark: a circular symbol resembling a door viewed from above. Gabe suggested that the hotel had two simultaneous histories, one in which the men experienced the week and another in which the consequences of their actions remained visible. Andrew proposed that the building was not repeating the same week exactly, but repeating its external arrangement while allowing something beneath the arrangement to continue changing. Joseph read the sentence several times and said that the distinction mattered. If the hotel merely repeated time, then nothing they did could survive. If something accumulated beneath the repetition, then the men were not starting again. They were being made to forget the progress they had already achieved. For the first time, the six of them considered the possibility that they were not alone in the building's memory.

## Thursday — The Seventh Door

That evening they returned to the ballroom together. The room had been closed during the day, but its doors were unlocked. The chandeliers were lit, and the polished floor reflected the long windows overlooking the gardens. A table had been arranged in the centre of the room with six place settings. Each plate bore a different name. The men stood around the table without sitting. Andrew inspected the cutlery and found a small mark on the underside of his glass. Chase turned his plate over and discovered a date that had not yet occurred. Tyler found a folded card beneath his napkin. Luke recognized the handwriting before he opened it. It was his own. The card contained only one sentence: *Do not let Andrew open the seventh door.* Andrew looked toward the far wall. There were six visible doors in the ballroom, each leading to a different part of the hotel. At the end of the room, between two heavy curtains, there was a narrow vertical seam in the wall. It had not been there when they entered. Andrew walked toward it. Luke called after him, but Andrew did not stop. He placed his hand against the seam and felt a mechanical vibration beneath the plaster. Then the chandeliers went dark. Somewhere inside the building, a bell rang seven times. When the lights returned, the table was empty, the cards had disappeared, and the men were standing in the entrance hall. It was Monday morning.

## The Recurrence Rule

The first week establishes the visible structure of the recurrence. Monday through Sunday repeat in the same order, with the same public schedules, meals, weather patterns, hotel announcements, and appointments. At the end of Sunday night, the men return to their Monday rooms and lose ordinary episodic memory of the preceding cycle. Their bodies retain fatigue and familiarity only imperfectly; their conscious recollections reset. The hotel, however, does not reset cleanly. The metaxy carries residue across the boundary: altered reflections, repeated phrases, misplaced objects, marks on surfaces, sounds, dreams, and impossible recognition. These traces may be incomplete, distorted, or out of sequence. They are evidence of prior action, not omniscient narration.

No character should acquire full memory of previous weeks through a convenient explanation. Recognition must be earned through repeated contact with metaxic traces, comparison, trust, and the gradual construction of a shared record. The hotel itself remains ambiguous. It may be a mechanism, an organism, a prison, a test, a damaged threshold, or a place caught between possible histories. Its staff are men and are subject to the same uncertainty as its guests. No outside rescue or explanatory authority should resolve the mystery prematurely.

## The Metaxy Ledger

The men begin a ledger in the library, recording every anomaly with its date, location, witness, and physical consequence. The ledger is not immune to recurrence. Some pages vanish, others acquire writing that none of them remembers making, and occasionally an entry appears in a handwriting belonging to a man who has not yet joined the investigation. They decide to distinguish direct observation from interpretation. A wet handprint on the pool glass is an observation; the claim that Joseph made it from another week is an interpretation. A key that opens no known door is an observation; the claim that it belongs to the seventh door remains a hypothesis. This discipline becomes essential as the hotel begins to exploit their desire for certainty.

The six men do not become a unified team immediately. Luke's instinct is to act and protect, but he must learn that action without listening can destroy evidence. Tyler is drawn to systems and measurements, but he must accept that the hotel changes its geometry according to conditions that cannot yet be measured. Chase preserves details and wants every claim supported, but the record can become another enclosure if he refuses to act until certainty arrives. Gabe is unusually receptive to the intervals between events, but his intuition must remain answerable to what the others can verify. Andrew's eccentric observations repeatedly uncover patterns that conventional investigation overlooks, although he sometimes follows a compelling detail too far. Joseph carries the emotional weight of promises and recognitions that have survived the reset, and must decide which memories to trust when the evidence is incomplete. Their differences are not defects to be erased. They are the instruments through which the hotel can be studied.

## Continuity Questions

The seventh door remains unopened. The identity of the seventh man in Chase's photograph is unknown. The porter who appears in the entrance court may be the same man who answers the telephone, but this has not been established. The blue line on Andrew's baseball may be a marker, a warning, or a record of a particular game. Joseph's note may refer to a promise made to one of the other men, to the hotel itself, or to someone who has not yet appeared in the current cycle. The mirrored floor beneath the ballroom has not been located by ordinary means. The date on Chase's plate is still in the future. The Grand Meridian's management has not explained the recurrence, and the men have not established whether the staff know more than the guests.

The novel proceeds by allowing each repeated week to change the men's understanding without granting them ordinary memory. Every cycle should produce new metaxic evidence and a consequential choice. The same Monday can contain a different relationship; the same dinner can reveal an earlier betrayal; the same corridor can become passable because a man remembers its shape without knowing why. The hotel is a closed world, but it is not static. Its central contradiction is that the week repeats while the men continue to change.
