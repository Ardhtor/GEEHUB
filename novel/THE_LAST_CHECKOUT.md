# The Last Checkout
## The Grand Meridian — Joey's sentence and the Teal Titan revealed
### Book I — The Sevenfold Guest
### Climax sequence; all characters and crimes are fictional

## The Fine Print

The men had begun to understand that the hotel could show them almost anything except the fact they needed most. They had walked through rooms that did not exist on the plans, heard conversations that had not yet happened, and found objects that seemed to remember the hands that had once held them. They had looked into mirrors and seen another arrangement of the hotel, and they had found the extra corridor, the seventh chair, the blank room register, and the map room behind the library glass. Yet their ordinary experience of Joey remained stubbornly simple. He was a man of ordinary height who carried a recorder, drew the building, listened to its sounds, and sometimes sat with them at breakfast. The clues around him were too large for that explanation, but each man had developed a private way to keep the contradiction manageable. Tyler treated it as a measurement problem. Chase treated it as a problem of photographic interpretation. Gabe called it a presence. Andrew called it an extraordinary structural anomaly. Luke regarded it as a practical danger that would eventually require a physical solution. Joseph waited for Joey to say what the others were unable to ask. Their methods were different, but the result was the same: the men continued to look directly at Joey, see the ordinary presentation he allowed them, and conclude that they had not yet found the answer.

The Director of Standards had warned them that astonishment was not recognition. The sentence had seemed elegant and slightly theatrical when Stanley Tucci delivered it in the quiet office behind the east gallery. Now the six men understood that it had been exact. They had been astonished by the shadows, the pressure marks, the sound beneath the floors, and the strange proportions of the hotel's reflections. Astonishment had allowed them to keep every clue separate. Recognition would require them to change the way they behaved toward the man who stood among them. It would require them to accept that Joey had been choosing what they could see, that the hotel had been arranged around that choice, and that their own conduct had helped preserve the arrangement. They were not merely missing a large man. They were refusing the fact that he was large because the ordinary presentation let them continue speaking to him as though nothing else had to be acknowledged.

That afternoon the hotel sent each man a formal notice. The envelopes were thick, cream-coloured, and sealed with the Grand Meridian's circular emblem. Each carried the same heading: FINAL ACCOUNT. The notices listed a room number, a date, a charge, and a name. Beneath those details was a list of incidents, each described in the language of a hotel complaint: damage to property, falsification of a record, concealment of evidence, failure to report an accident, unauthorized access, breach of a guest's trust, and abandonment of an agreed duty of care. The descriptions were exact enough to be recognized and incomplete enough to be contested. Luke's notice concerned an act of strength used to silence rather than protect. Tyler's concerned a measurement he had altered because the unaltered result implicated someone he wished to shield. Chase's concerned a photograph he had kept but never entered into the official record. Gabe's concerned a truth he had sensed and then diluted until nobody had to act on it. Andrew's concerned an experiment that had become a spectacle at another man's expense. Joseph's concerned a promise he had treated as fulfilled merely because he had waited in the place where he had made it. The incidents were fictional and belonged to the men's history in the hotel. None of the men could recall committing them, but each knew enough to understand that the notices were not random accusations.

At the bottom of each notice was the same line: *The Director accepts no appeals concerning the principal.* Tucci's signature appeared beneath it, in a hand as neat as the rest of the hotel's paperwork. Luke took the notice to the Director's office. Tucci read the page without asking him to sit. He explained that the notices were not a verdict and that the hotel did not pretend to be a court. They were an accounting, prepared so that a man could no longer claim that an act had disappeared simply because its memory had been removed. Luke asked who had prepared them. Tucci replied that the incidents had been entered by the hotel, but the sentence had been set by Joey. Tyler arrived with his own notice and asked whether the records were accurate. Tucci said that the descriptions were accurate in their essential details, but that no record could contain every reason, pressure, fear, or consequence. Chase asked whether the men had chosen freely. Tucci looked at him and said that the devil's talent was to make a choice feel like the only available option. It did not follow that the choice had never been made.

## The Principal

Joey was waiting in the ballroom when the six men arrived. The chandeliers were lit, the polished floor had been cleaned, and the six place settings had been arranged exactly as they had been in the first cycle. The narrow seam between the curtains was visible again. The seventh chair stood apart from the others, facing the wall. Joey sat in an ordinary chair near the table, dressed as he had been in the library, his recorder placed beside a glass of water. He looked like an ordinary-sized man. The men could see the chair beneath him, the height of his shoulders, the shape of his hands, and the quiet expression with which he watched them enter. The hotel had given them the familiar presentation once more, perhaps to see whether they would mistake it again for the whole of him. Luke stopped a few paces away. Tyler held the folded notice. Chase carried the ledger. Gabe had brought the recording of the forty-seven-second gap. Andrew held the baseball with the blue seam. Joseph carried the blank seventh name plate.

Joey greeted them as though they were expected. He asked whether they had enjoyed the hotel. He asked whether the breakfast service had been satisfactory, whether they had made use of the pool, and whether the gymnasium equipment had been maintained to a good standard. His questions were courteous and almost affectionate. Luke told him that the week was a prison. Joey replied that the men had been free to enjoy nearly every facility in the building. Tyler said that the road always returned them to the entrance court. Joey answered that a road could be travelled without being understood. Chase asked whether the weekly reset was his doing. Joey said yes. Gabe asked whether the metaxy traces were also deliberate. Joey said that some were, and some were consequences of the men trying to remember what they had refused to see. Andrew asked why Joey had not simply shown them his full size at the beginning. Joey looked down at the baseball in Andrew's hand and said, "I could have. I chose not to." Joseph finally asked why. Joey's expression softened, but only slightly. "Because you would have treated it as another spectacle. You would have looked, measured, talked about it, and then gone back to yourselves. I wanted to know whether you could recognize me after the shock had passed."

The men understood that Joey had been choosing his apparent size all along. When they looked directly at him, he could present as ordinary. When they looked away, he could unfold into the monumental figure that had been pressing against the architecture. The shadows and reflections were not accidental leaks from a body he could not control. They were glimpses he permitted. The overloaded lift, the depressed floor, the altered scoreboard, and the low note in the service passages were consequences of his full presence, not malfunctions that had happened to reveal it. Joey had allowed the men to discover enough evidence to become suspicious, then restrained the final recognition whenever they tried to reduce him to an ordinary explanation. He had been their host, their jailer, their tempter, and the authority behind the repeating week. The hotel did not confine Joey. It was built to serve his sentence.

## What They Had Done

Joey walked around the table and examined the six notices. He did not deny that he had arranged the circumstances of the crimes. He had presented Luke with an opportunity to use strength as a shortcut, given Tyler a reason to alter a measurement, placed Chase in possession of evidence that would damage a relationship if disclosed, encouraged Gabe to keep a private truth to himself, made Andrew's experiment attractive as entertainment, and offered Joseph the comfort of waiting instead of returning. The exact details had been recorded in the notices, but the pattern was now visible. Each man had been given an easier action and a harder one. Each had chosen the easier action. Joey had made those choices tempting, and then he had made the consequences permanent. He had not created their capacity for wrongdoing; he had learned how to direct it. The hotel was the architecture of that direction, and the week was its repetition.

Luke asked whether Joey took pleasure in making them fail. Joey said that he took pleasure in being recognized, and that the men had made recognition very difficult. Tyler answered that this was not the same as justice. Joey did not dispute him. Chase asked whether the notices included Joey's part in the crimes. Joey said that the notices concerned the guests. Joseph asked who was keeping the account of the host. For the first time, Joey did not answer immediately. The ballroom's reflections darkened. In the mirrors behind the curtains, the ordinary-sized man sitting at the table was accompanied by a second figure whose shoulders extended beyond the upper edge of the glass. The figure's arms were enormous, its torso filled the reflected room, and its head rose into a darkness that seemed to continue above the ballroom itself. Joey could have shown them the whole truth at that moment. Instead he let the reflection remain incomplete.

Gabe said that Joey was asking them to accept a truth he had arranged while refusing to answer for the arrangement. Joey looked toward him. Gabe did not lower his eyes. He said that the men had done wrong, and that the notices contained events they could no longer deny. But Joey had also built the room in which those decisions were made. He had made himself the tempter, the witness, the judge, and the jailer, then repeated the punishment until the men could no longer tell the difference between accountability and captivity. Joseph added that a sentence could be true and still be administered unjustly. The hotel could make them confront what they had done, but it could not make Joey innocent. Luke said that if Joey wanted to be recognized, then he had to accept the same standard. He could not ask the men to stop hiding behind excuses while hiding behind the role of devil.

Joey stood. The ordinary chair moved back from the table. The sound was small, but the ballroom's floor answered with a much deeper vibration. He looked at the six men and said that he had never claimed to be innocent. The men had wanted a host who would provide food, warmth, entertainment, status, and a daily routine without asking what those comforts cost. He had given them exactly that. He had offered them a beautiful place to avoid themselves. He had kept them there because they had mistaken the offer for freedom. "You wanted a world where tomorrow could always take responsibility for today," he said. "I gave you a week that never ended." The words were not a confession of remorse. They were an admission of authorship. Joey had engineered the sentence and was proud of the completeness of its design.

## The Measure

Andrew placed the baseball on the table. The blue seam aligned with the circular symbol engraved in the wood. Tyler opened his measurements. Chase opened the ledger. Gabe played the recording. Luke stood beside the nearest column. Joseph placed the seventh name plate in the empty chair. They were not trying to force Joey to reveal himself. They were showing that they had finally gathered the pieces without reducing them to separate anomalies. The measurements agreed across the hotel. The weight readings, the floor impressions, the acoustic intervals, the reflected dimensions, and the architectural plans described a single body at a single scale. The body had been present through every cycle. The men had been allowed to look away from it, then permitted to see an ordinary-sized man whenever they looked back. Joey had controlled the concealment, but the men had participated in the denial because the ordinary version allowed them to postpone what recognition required.

Joey looked at the evidence and then at the six men. He told them that he could show them at any time. He had always been able to do so. The last door did not require a key, a code, a special day, or a correctly aligned set of mirrors. It required him to choose. He had withheld that choice because the men still believed the revelation itself would solve everything. They thought that if they saw how large he was, they would have completed the task. But the true test was what they would do after seeing him. Would they make him smaller in their explanations? Would they call the body a trick, a projection, an instrument, or a punishment detached from the man? Would they return to ordinary conversation and let the event become a story they could dismiss? Joey wanted them to remain in the room with the fact. He wanted them to recognize his full presence and the full responsibility that accompanied it.

The lights in the ballroom softened. The curtains drew back. The roof above the chandeliers did not open; the space behind it simply became visible. The room unfolded upward into the same immense chamber the men had glimpsed in their reflections, and Joey's body became visible at its full scale. He stood thirty feet tall. His Stage Four muscle development was complete and unmistakable: the width of his shoulders, the depth of his chest and back, the immense volume through his arms, the muscular weight of his torso, and the development of his legs all belonged to one coherent, human body. His teal colouring held against the warm ballroom lights. His head, neck, and shoulders remained recognizably human. His gray shorts were the same simple clothing anchor the men had seen in the incomplete images. Nothing about the figure was an optical illusion. The enormous girth and physical heft were not exaggerations made by the hotel's camera angles. The body was real within the world of the novel, and it had been there all along.

The six men looked up. Joey did not make himself ordinary again. This time he chose to remain fully visible while they watched. Tyler checked the height against the architectural grid and confirmed thirty feet. Chase recorded the front, side, and three-quarter views with fixed references. Pavel, standing at the ballroom entrance, checked the structural response and found that the load had settled across the hidden supports beneath the floor. Andrew looked from the scoreboard motif to the enormous body and finally understood why the seventh position had sounded different. Luke saw the floor compress beneath Joey's feet and understood the source of every pressure mark. Gabe heard the low note resolve into the sound of a single immense presence. Joseph looked at Joey without mistaking him for anyone else. The Teal Titan incarnate stood before them, thirty feet tall at the fourth stage of muscle growth, and the men no longer needed to infer the whole from its fragments.

## The Week Breaks

The hotel clock advanced from 10:59 to 11:00. The seven bells sounded, but this time there was no displacement to the lobby and no return to Monday. The corridor remained open. The ballroom remained in its expanded form. Joey remained thirty feet tall. The men did not become innocent by recognizing him, and Joey did not become innocent by showing them the truth. The sentence ended because the act that sustained it had ended: the men stopped denying Joey's actual size, and Joey stopped using that denial as the instrument of their confinement. He could have broken the week earlier, but he had chosen to hold them inside it. He could have shown his true body earlier, but he had chosen to restrain their perception. The recognition was therefore both their reckoning and his admission of power.

The entrance doors opened onto the avenue. For the first time, the road continued beyond the dark trees instead of bending back toward the hotel. The Grand Meridian remained behind them, beautiful and immense, its public rooms illuminated and its staff standing in their usual places. Stanley Tucci waited beside the east gallery, immaculate as ever. He looked from the six men to Joey, then adjusted the cuff of his jacket. "Well," he said, "that is considerably more difficult to fit into the guest directory." It was the first time any of them laughed without the sound being swallowed by the hotel. Joey looked down at Tucci and gave a small nod. The Director of Standards returned it, then turned toward the entrance court to see that the men had a clear way through.

Luke stepped outside first, followed by Tyler, Chase, Gabe, Andrew, and Joseph. They did not run. They had not been released from responsibility; they had been released from the convenience of repetition. Joey followed them at his true scale, carefully adjusting his movement to the open grounds. Beyond the trees lay a road that had not appeared on any map. The men understood that the world after the hotel would not erase their offences or the part Joey had played in them. It would give them something the loop had withheld: a future in which they could make different decisions without pretending the previous ones had never occurred.

The Grand Meridian had been a beautiful prison. Its luxury had allowed the men to mistake comfort for freedom, and its weekly routine had turned postponement into eternity. Joey had designed that sentence, had tempted the men into their crimes, and had kept them there as the Teal Titan hidden in plain sight. The body had never been absent. The devil had never been powerless. The week had never been an accident. It ended when the men recognized the full measure and Joey chose to let the road continue.

## Canon rule established by the climax

Joey is the controlling devil behind the Grand Meridian. He is responsible for engineering the circumstances of the men's fictional crimes and for maintaining their repeating week as a sentence. He can reveal his thirty-foot Stage Four Teal Titan form whenever he wishes. When they look directly at him, he can restrain their perception so that he appears ordinary-sized; when they look away, he may unfold into his actual scale. He deliberately uses this effect to keep the men from recognizing the whole truth. The loop continues through their repeated denial and Joey's decision to maintain the punishment. It breaks when they acknowledge Joey's full physical presence and the mutual responsibility involved: their choices and his manipulation. Recognition ends the loop but does not erase guilt or absolve the devil.
