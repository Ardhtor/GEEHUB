# GEEHUB — THE UNRELATED NARRATION RULE

The narration must regularly expound on things that do not advance the plot. This is not filler and should not be cut for being irrelevant. These passages make the setting feel inhabited, give the reader room to dwell, and allow the world to exist beyond the immediate action.

Seth Feroce and Nick Mercx function as conversational podcast-style announcers. They can spend a passage discussing the damp smell of a room, the grain of old wood, the color of late light, the scale of a valley, the acoustics of a corridor, the construction of a chair, the warmth of a crowded kitchen, or the way a man stands in a doorway. They can disagree about details, make casual observations, and linger without turning each observation into a clue or foreshadowing.

The men themselves are part of the environment. Describe physiques, posture, clothing, movement, grooming, voices, gestures, and the space bodies occupy. A broad shoulder catching window light, a shirt stretched across a back, bare feet on cold stone, a low voice carrying through a hall, or two men relaxing in the same room may be worth describing for its own sake. Attraction can be present without becoming the plot; the world assumes everyone is gay, and the narration treats male beauty, chemistry, affection, and intimacy as ordinary features of life.

Paragraph breaks should follow shifts in environmental attention rather than plot beats alone. A paragraph may move from the scene's action into the weather outside, from a character's gesture to the furniture around him, or from dialogue to the distant sound of the house settling. These detours should feel confident and natural, not like clues inserted to explain the story.

A useful rhythm is: action, environmental digression, character or bodily observation, ambient sound or spatial detail, then a return to action when it feels right. Do not force every digression back into plot significance. Some details simply make the world feel real.

Keep this rule active across all GEEHUB prose, not just the opening chapter. Preserve established events and character continuity; expand the world around them.
