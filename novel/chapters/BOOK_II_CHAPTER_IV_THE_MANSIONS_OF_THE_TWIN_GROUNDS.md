# Book II — Chapter IV: The Mansions of the Twin Grounds

The second map of the lower country was still drying when Oren brought it into the Measure House. He laid it beside the first, aligned the cedar marks, and placed a smooth stone at each corner so the sheets would not curl. The old route remained visible beneath the new one: the Long Fold before the floor rose, the Warm Table before it tilted, and the dark opening that had appeared behind it. The fresh map showed the upper gallery, the northern bypass, and a narrow route that had not been present in the earlier survey. None of the lines had been drawn through the dark opening.

Senn stood at the far side of the table with a ruler in one hand. “We can reach the shelf without crossing the seam,” he said. “The northern route has enough width for the party if we move the equipment in two loads.”

Vale tapped the upper gallery with his walking staff. “It is not the old way. We should not call it safe because the floor looks smoother.”

“No one has called it safe,” Oren replied. “We have called it the only route we can test without entering the opening.”

The expedition leader looked at the two maps. The previous party had been right to turn back. The current party had been right to wait. That did not mean the next party should stay away indefinitely. It meant that the route needed a fresh inspection, a second return path, and Kirk's account of what he had felt during the movement.

Kirk joined the discussion through the receiver. He could hear the names and the measurements, but he could not see the maps. The leader described the northern bypass and the proposed two-load approach. Kirk listened, then said that the pressure beneath the Warm Table had eased since the previous evening. There had been another small movement at the edge of the lower country, but it had not travelled toward the opening. He could not promise that the passage would remain unchanged. He could agree to the inspection, provided the party did not cross the seam and stopped if the ground tightened again.

Andrew Sim was standing near the receiver. He had arrived early with his baseball and a bundle of chalk. “We'll go as far as the shelf, mark the route, and come back,” he said. “No heroics.”

Kirk laughed. “You say that as if heroics are the only thing you have to watch.”

“I can be careful.”

“You can. You can also get excited and forget that other people are still carrying the equipment.”

Andrew glanced at the men beside him. “That's fair.”

The reply was brief, but it pleased him. Kirk had remembered the way he moved through a plan, and had spoken to him as someone who knew him well enough to correct him. Andrew wanted to continue the conversation. He wanted to ask whether Kirk had slept, whether the movement had changed the way he felt, whether the sound of the receiver was clearer that morning. He held the questions for the moment and listened while Oren set the conditions for the inspection.

They went in two groups. Vale led the first with the surveyor and Senn's assistant; Andrew went with the second, carrying the chalk and the ball. Oren stayed at the Measure House to keep the map table open and receive the reports. The first group passed through the upper gallery, reached the northern bypass, and checked the floor at measured intervals. At each change they marked the surface with a small cedar strip. They did not put a permanent stake into the ground. Kirk had asked them to avoid unnecessary fixing until they understood how the country was moving.

The second group followed after the first had returned a signal. Andrew rolled the baseball along the new route, but only where Vale had cleared the surface for a test. It moved along the middle of the passage, slowed at a shallow bend, and came to rest against a low rise. They measured the point and repeated the test from a slightly different position. The ball took another path. Andrew marked both results, then added a small drawing of the bend.

“Your map is becoming a baseball field,” the surveyor said.

“Every country needs a field.”

“This one needs a return route.”

“Then we'll make a field with two exits.”

The surveyor shook his head, but he wrote the result into the margin. Andrew's jokes often arrived with a useful idea concealed inside them. He liked that people could laugh and still use what he had noticed.

The northern bypass continued beyond the old Warm Table. It did not cross the dark seam. Instead, it curved around the shelf and opened onto a broad terrace with two rising grounds separated by a sheltered valley. The view was larger than anything they had yet seen in the lower country. A natural span crossed the valley; beyond it, the ground opened toward two separate slopes, each broad enough to hold a settlement. The left-hand rise had a long, sheltered terrace. The right-hand rise widened into a shallow garden-like basin. Both surfaces bore traces of previous use: shallow grooves, small worn hollows, and the remains of markers whose material had softened with time.

The men stopped before crossing the natural span. The surveyor recorded its surface and the relation between the two grounds. Vale tested the first approach with his staff. Andrew watched the ball roll a short distance and come to rest near the centre. He could see that the bridge was not perfectly level, but the difference was consistent. The structure seemed to have been used and maintained before.

“Someone has crossed here a lot,” Chase said.

He had come with the second group after helping at the Measure House. His attention had settled on the small marks beneath the span. He crouched, examined their spacing, and traced the shape of one with a pencil. The cuts were not decorative. Some had been renewed at different times; others were partly concealed by a later layer of smooth material. Chase wrote down the position of each mark and kept the original drawing beside the new one.

“Not a monument,” he said. “A route.”

They named the span the Cedar Bridge after the pale brown lines that ran through its surface. The name was provisional, but it was useful. The two rises became the Twin Grounds. The men did not yet know their full boundaries. The name described what they could see, not what they believed lay beyond it.

Kirk asked for a report. The expedition leader described the terrace, the bridge, and the two slopes. Andrew took the receiver and explained the view. He told Kirk that there was a broad western shelf, a sheltered valley, and an eastern basin where the light gathered differently. He described the bridge as a route rather than a monument, and said that the old marks suggested earlier travellers had used it.

Kirk listened carefully. “Are you comfortable there?”

“Yes.”

“Is everyone?”

The leader answered for the group. The men were safe and had not crossed the bridge yet. Kirk asked them to wait until the next inspection, then said he would like to hear the measurements before they decided whether to build anything.

Andrew was not ready to end the call. “The view is extraordinary,” he said. “You'd like it.”

“I can't see it from where I am.”

“I know. I'll describe it properly.”

“Another time. Send the map first.”

Andrew looked at the receiver. “All right.”

He handed it back, but the conversation stayed with him. The new country was exciting, and he wanted to share every part of it with Kirk. He found himself imagining the call he would make when the bridge was finished, the one he would make when the first house was standing, the one he would make when the garden had been planted. Each future event seemed to contain another reason to hear Kirk's voice.

The settlement was not built in a day. The first return journey took them back by the northern bypass and the upper gallery to the Measure House. Oren compared their report with the older maps and asked Chase to preserve the marks on a separate sheet. The next inspection took place after a full rest interval. Kirk reported no discomfort and agreed that the natural span could be reinforced without changing its underlying shape. The residents would use a timber deck and removable handrails, leaving the older surface visible beneath the new work. The bridge would have two approach routes and a clearly marked turning point if the ground changed.

They returned to the Twin Grounds with timber, tools, lamps, and the first materials for a shared hall. The construction crew established a neutral worksite near the bridge. No house was to be built across the main path, and no resident would have to pass through another person's home to speak with Kirk or reach the shared spaces. The receiver would remain in a separate alcove in the Common Hall. It would carry voices when Kirk and the residents chose to use it, and it would be closed when no conversation was wanted.

Robert Laskin chose the upper western terrace. He had already spoken about the kind of house he wanted, and the site suited him: a long view over the Cedar Bridge, a sheltered porch, and enough level ground for a library wing. He arrived with drawings for an observatory and a gallery, but he did not insist that the first plan had to be the final one. Chase asked him to include a second stair so visitors could reach the bridge without walking through the house. Robert considered the request, then added it to the drawing.

“I want people to visit,” Robert said. “I don't want the house to become the only way through.”

The builders raised the Western Mansion in cedar and dark timber. A pale stone base followed the surface of the terrace without cutting into it. The porch was deep enough for several chairs; the library wing held books, maps, and a long table; the observatory faced the Long Fold and the upper route. A broad gallery looked down toward the bridge. Robert added a small room where visitors could leave wet coats and boots, because the approach from the Measure House could carry moisture after a change in the lower passages. It was an ungrand detail, but it made the house more hospitable.

Chase chose the northern rise for his own mansion. He wanted to be near enough to the bridge to see the movement of the settlement, but not so close that he would spend every morning watching it. His house grew around a square archive court with a cedar tree at its centre and four covered walks leading to different rooms. The map room held the successive states of the Long Fold and Warm Table, with the oldest version on the left and the newest on the right. A library rose above it. The house also had a large kitchen, a reading room, guest bedrooms, and a terrace overlooking the valley.

Chase rejected the idea of a signal balcony. “I don't need to watch everyone,” he said. “I need to know what happened, and then I need to live somewhere.”

He designed the archive to keep conflicting accounts side by side. If one surveyor said the floor had moved east and another said it had turned beneath him, both reports would remain attributed to their source. The map could show a best current interpretation, but it would not erase the route by which the interpretation had been reached. Chase knew that people often repeated a simple explanation until it replaced the event itself. His house would make that harder.

Gabe chose the eastern terrace, beside the sheltered basin. He wanted a mansion that was part home and part workshop, with a great timber-and-glass hall where ordinary work could happen in good light. The lower rooms included a kitchen, a training room, a workshop, and a common room with doors opening toward the garden. Upstairs were plant rooms, balconies, and a gallery of portraits and measurement cards. Gabe insisted that earlier images should remain visible. He had no interest in building a house that celebrated only the latest body or the largest achievement. The record should show that a person had lived through more than one form and still belonged to all of them.

The upper floor included a room with no assigned purpose. The builders suggested a second training room, a display gallery, and an enclosed terrace. Gabe left the room empty. He wanted to learn the house's daily rhythm before telling it what it must become. He planted a line of small trees along the garden path and asked the crew to widen the paving enough for two people to walk side by side. He liked the idea that someone could come to the house and spend an afternoon without having to explain why.

Joseph chose the quieter site at the eastern end of the Cedar Bridge. His mansion had a wide veranda, two wings joined by a bright central room, and two garden courts protected from the strongest movement of air through the valley. One wing held books, familiar objects, and a sitting room; the other had a domestic kitchen, dining room, and bedrooms that looked toward the gardens. He requested openable windows in every main room. He wanted to hear the settlement rather than shut it out. At the back of the house, a narrow tower gave him a view of the bridge and the western terrace, but the tower was not meant to become a watch post. He wanted a view he could choose to use, not an obligation to keep guard.

Joseph took more time over the house than the others. He walked the site in the morning and again after dusk. He listened to the bridge, asked how far the common hall could be heard, and checked whether the garden would remain sheltered if the ground changed. He chose a kitchen with a large worktable and a veranda bench facing the valley. He imagined ordinary evenings: someone bringing a book, someone coming by for food, the windows open, the lamps low. He wanted a house that would remain a home even when the expedition was quiet.

The four mansions became distinct parts of the same settlement. Robert's Western Mansion was hospitable and outward-looking. Chase's Northern Archive House preserved the history of the place without making it a command centre. Gabe's Eastern Garden Mansion kept making, training, and continuity close together. Joseph's East Bridge Mansion was quieter, open to the weather and the sound of the valley. None of the houses was the principal residence. The residents were free to visit one another, close their doors, share meals, or spend the evening alone.

The Cedar Bridge was strengthened with a timber deck and removable handrails. The original surface remained visible at the edges. The Common Hall was built beside the bridge, on a shelf that did not belong to any private residence. It had a dining room, a covered recreation court, an assembly room, washrooms, and the receiver alcove. Two paths approached from either side of the valley. A person could cross from one ground to the other without entering a mansion or asking its owner for permission.

Daily life settled into the new geography. Robert opened his porch in the morning and made coffee for whoever arrived. Chase kept the first maps beside the revised ones and cooked dinner when the survey crew returned late. Gabe spent long hours in the workshop, then came out to water the garden and walk the new path. Joseph read on the veranda, made meals in the large kitchen, and listened to the bridge lamps clicking on at dusk. The residents started to recognise the sound of one another's routines. The place grew familiar through use rather than through a name on a map.

Kirk remained present without becoming constantly available. The receiver gave the residents a way to speak with him, and he sometimes called the Common Hall himself to ask about the bridges or to hear how the houses were settling. He could ask for quiet, postpone a report, or say that a movement made him uncomfortable. The residents could report a shift, request a pause, and move away from any route that had become uncertain. No one treated the receiver as a window into his thoughts. It carried words he chose to speak.

Andrew did not build a mansion. He stayed between the First Shelter, the Measure House, and the Common Hall, often helping with whatever needed doing. He was energetic and popular with the construction crew. He could carry a plan from one side of the valley to the other, invent a quick test, make a joke that eased an argument, and remember a detail that saved someone a second trip. But he increasingly arranged his day around Kirk's calls.

At first there were practical reasons. Andrew wanted to report the bridge's new sound, ask about a change in the ground, or compare a movement he had observed with Kirk's account. Then he began calling to share smaller things: a strange pattern in the garden, the way a lamp reflected in the glass hall, the shape of a cloud-like shadow across the western slope. These were not meaningless details. Andrew liked sharing the world with Kirk. The difficulty was that the calls became more frequent, and the practical reason often ended well before Andrew was ready to say goodbye.

He began arriving at the receiver alcove before breakfast. If Kirk answered, Andrew would ask whether he had slept, whether he had eaten, whether the night had been comfortable, and whether he had noticed anything new. After the report was complete, Andrew might find another question. If Kirk did not answer, Andrew returned later. Sometimes he called again after lunch, then once more in the evening. He was not demanding a favour in any formal sense. He was seeking the familiar pleasure of being answered.

Kirk enjoyed Andrew's company. Their chemistry had not been invented by the expedition; it had been re-established in the First Shelter and strengthened by shared work. Andrew's peculiar observations made Kirk laugh, and Kirk's exact questions gave Andrew's ideas a direction. But the growing frequency changed the balance. Kirk had other conversations, other obligations, and periods when he simply wanted to rest. He began to tell Andrew when he could speak and when he needed to leave the receiver quiet.

Andrew accepted the limits when they were stated. The trouble was that he kept looking for another opening. A thought would arrive and he would want to share it immediately. A small event would occur and he would imagine Kirk's reaction. He began to feel that the day was incomplete if he had not spoken to him. The old promises returned to his mind, especially the promise to come back. He had not considered that coming back could mean waiting until the other person was ready.

Joseph noticed the change before Andrew did. His own conversations with Kirk had often happened in the quiet intervals after the expedition's reports, when there was no immediate need to measure or explain anything. They could talk about the garden, a book, an old memory, or a question that did not need an answer that evening. Now the receiver was often occupied. Andrew's calls filled the alcove, and even when he finished, he stayed nearby as though he expected another conversation to begin.

One night Joseph came to the Common Hall with a book under his arm. Kirk had said he might call after dinner, and Joseph wanted to ask him about a passage he had been reading. Andrew was already in the alcove, describing the bridge's low wooden note. Kirk was listening. Andrew paused when Joseph entered, but only long enough to ask Kirk whether the sound changed when the bridge was crossed from the eastern side. Kirk asked him to repeat the test.

Joseph waited. When Andrew had finished, he greeted Kirk and began to tell him about the book. Andrew remembered another detail and added it before Joseph could finish. He was not trying to silence Joseph. He simply did not want to lose his own thought, and he had become accustomed to treating the receiver as the place where his thoughts should go as soon as they arrived.

Kirk asked Andrew to let Joseph finish. Andrew stepped back, but remained in the alcove. Joseph looked at the receiver and then at Andrew. “It can wait,” he said.

Kirk answered at once. “It doesn't have to.”

Joseph gave a small shake of his head. “Not tonight.”

He left the Common Hall and crossed the Cedar Bridge alone. The lights laid a broken reflection along the valley. Joseph could have gone to Robert's porch or Gabe's garden, but he returned to his own veranda and opened the windows. He sat for a while with the book closed on his lap, listening to the settlement carry on around him.

The next morning Kirk called Joseph directly. They spoke in the bright central room of the East Bridge Mansion. Kirk asked whether Joseph had left the previous night because he wanted privacy or because Andrew had made it difficult to stay. Joseph said that he wanted privacy, but that the two things had become connected. He did not accuse Andrew of malice. He said that he was tired of waiting for an interval that never seemed to arrive.

Kirk listened. “You shouldn't have to compete for a conversation with me.”

“I know that.”

“Do you feel that you are?”

Joseph looked out at the garden. “Sometimes. I don't want to make it a contest, so I've been stepping back. But stepping back has started to feel like disappearing.”

Kirk was quiet for a moment. “You aren't disappearing to me.”

Joseph accepted the answer, though it did not solve the problem. He told Kirk that he did not want Andrew banished or humiliated. He wanted the room to remain large enough for more than one relationship. Kirk said he would speak with Andrew and make the call arrangements clearer. Joseph agreed, then asked whether Kirk had been able to rest. The conversation turned to ordinary things. They talked about the open windows, the garden, the light on the bridge, and a book that Joseph had left on the table. It was not a dramatic reconciliation. It was a return to the kind of conversation they had been missing.

Andrew did not know the whole of that conversation. He knew only that Joseph had stopped coming to the receiver alcove and that Kirk had begun ending calls with a clear goodbye. The change unsettled him. He wanted Kirk's attention more than he had admitted, and the new boundaries made the want visible. When he tried to explain it to Gabe, he did so while helping fit a shelf in the glass hall.

“I keep thinking of things to tell him,” Andrew said.

Gabe held one end of the shelf while Andrew checked the bracket. “Then some of them can wait.”

“I know that.”

“You keep saying you know.”

Andrew looked at him. Gabe tightened the screw. “Do you want to talk to Kirk because something happened, or because you want him to answer?”

Andrew considered the question. “Both.”

“Those are different reasons.”

“I like talking to him.”

“I know.”

“I don't want to become a nuisance.”

Gabe measured the shelf again. “Then let him have a day that isn't organised around answering you.”

Andrew looked toward the receiver alcove across the bridge. He disliked the idea of waiting. Waiting made him feel that the conversation might be lost, that someone else might arrive first, that the space would close before he could say what he wanted. He had not thought of his need in those terms before. He had thought of it as enthusiasm, as affection, as wanting to share. It was all of those things, but it was also fear of missing the moment.

Gabe did not tell him to stop caring. He said that Kirk could enjoy his company and still need a quiet day. Andrew nodded, but the nod did not mean that the desire had gone away.

Chase saw the social change from another angle. He kept a record of the calls, not as a tally of affection but as a sequence that could be compared with the times Joseph used the hall. He did not write that Andrew was jealous or that Joseph had withdrawn because of him. Those were interpretations. He recorded that Andrew had begun calling outside the agreed report windows, that Kirk had asked for clearer intervals, and that Joseph had started using his own veranda for conversations he had once held in the common room. The facts were enough to show that the settlement had changed.

Robert noticed the practical consequence. The receiver alcove had become a place where people hesitated before entering. He suggested that they put a second bench outside it, so someone waiting could sit without standing in the doorway. Chase agreed, but added that the bench would not solve the underlying problem. The receiver needed a simple schedule for reports, alongside the freedom for Kirk or any resident to initiate a conversation when they chose. The schedule would protect quiet without turning communication into a rigid appointment system.

Kirk agreed to try it. The ordinary report windows would be morning and early evening; outside those times, calls would be made only when something had changed or when both parties wanted to talk. The rule applied to everyone, including Kirk. He could call whenever he wished, and anyone could ask for a conversation. No one could assume an answer was owed.

Andrew found the arrangement difficult for the first several days. He had to let small observations remain with him until evening. Some were less interesting by then; others became better because he had time to understand them. He started carrying a notebook. When he wanted to call, he wrote down the thing he wanted to say and went back to his work. Sometimes the note was enough. Sometimes it became a proper report. Sometimes he still wanted Kirk to hear the joke, and he waited until the next window.

Joseph began returning to the Common Hall, though not every evening. He did not rush to reclaim the time he had lost. He spoke with Kirk when they chose to speak, and he kept his own life active in the mansion. He cooked, read, worked in the garden, and invited Gabe over for a meal. The house became a place he wanted to inhabit in its own right, not merely a retreat from Andrew's attention.

One evening, after the second report window had closed, Kirk called the Common Hall himself. Andrew was there with the notebook in his pocket. Joseph had come from the eastern house to return a borrowed book. Kirk asked first about the garden path. Joseph described a new shallow dip near the lower stair and the way the paving had warmed during the afternoon. Andrew waited until he had finished, then reported that the Cedar Bridge had made its low note again, but this time only when crossed from east to west.

Kirk asked a question about each report. He listened to the answers. When Andrew had finished, the receiver went quiet for a moment. Andrew wanted to fill the gap. He did not. Kirk said that he was glad to have heard both accounts and that he would ask for the next survey in the morning. Then he said goodnight.

It was an ordinary call. Andrew felt the pull to keep it going, but he let it end. Joseph took his book and went back toward the East Bridge Mansion. Andrew remained by the bench, looking at the receiver. He had not received less of Kirk because he had waited. He had received a conversation in which the others had room as well.

He would not learn the habit all at once. The next morning he reached the alcove before the report window and stood there with his hand on the receiver. He looked at the notebook, considered the small joke he had written down, and decided it could wait. He carried a timber brace to Gabe's garden instead. The bridge was quiet. The houses were open. The ground moved beneath them, slowly enough that no one had to stop what they were doing.

The Twin Grounds had become a settlement. Four mansions stood around the Cedar Bridge, the Common Hall served as neutral ground, and the old routes remained visible on the map beside their new forms. The place had a daily rhythm now: meals, repairs, gardens, records, visits, quiet evenings, and the occasional call from Kirk. It contained more than one kind of home and more than one kind of attachment.

Andrew still wanted Kirk's attention. The want had become part of the story, not an error that could be crossed out. Joseph still had a place with Kirk, and the two men had not settled every difficult feeling that Andrew's need had stirred. Kirk would have to keep speaking clearly, and Andrew would have to learn that closeness was not made safer by holding on more tightly. The next promises would depend on what they did, not only on what they said.

At the Measure House, Chase placed the first map beside the latest one. The old Long Fold, the raised shelf, the Warm Table, the northern bypass, and the Twin Grounds all remained visible as successive states. He added a note that the Cedar Bridge was now in regular use and that the four residences had become active homes. Beneath it he recorded the new receiver schedule, with the words **contact by choice; silence is also permitted**.

The outer edges of the Twin Grounds remained unmeasured. Beyond the northern bypass, a further passage had appeared in the latest survey, but no one had entered it. The dark opening behind the Warm Table remained closed to the expedition. The men had gained a place to live, but not a complete map of the country.

That was enough for now. The next discovery would have to find them living there.
