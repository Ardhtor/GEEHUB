# Book II — Chapter IV: The Promises That Keep

After the party returned from the Long Fold, the Measure House had begun to feel less like a place they had found and more like a place that had agreed to keep them. The cedar post still stood beside the entrance, though its base had shifted again during the night. The pale line along the far wall had lengthened into a narrow passage, and the floor had settled into a shallow curve that made the eastern side of the chamber easier to cross. The men recorded each change before breakfast. They had learned not to call a place stable merely because it had been quiet for a few hours.

Andrew Sim arrived while the surveyor was drawing the new measurements. He came down from the upper route with a field bag over one shoulder, his large athletic frame carrying the easy balance of a man accustomed to moving at speed without wasting it. His blue eyes went first to the altered passage, then to the surveyor's paper, and finally to the receiver resting beside the cedar post. He looked as though he had several questions and had already decided that the first one should be a joke.

“You've all been measuring the same wall again,” he said. “That's how it gets you. You measure it, it gets self-conscious, and then it changes.”

Marcus looked up. “The wall changed before we measured it.”

“Then it was waiting for an audience.”

The surveyor gave him a tired look. Andrew grinned, set down his bag, and crouched to study the marks at the base of the post. There was nothing random about his attention. He noticed that the post's new angle did not match the direction of the floor's movement; he noticed that a small stone had been turned over, exposing a pale face that had been hidden the previous evening; and he noticed that the cord tied around the post had been knotted twice, although he remembered tying it only once. He could make a game out of almost anything, but the game usually began with a real observation.

Kirk's voice came through the receiver before Andrew had finished speaking. “You're back.”

Andrew picked it up. “You sound surprised.”

“I was told you were taking the western route.”

“I did. The western route was taking too long, so I came back by the upper ridge.”

“That sounds like you.”

“It's a compliment when you say it.”

Kirk paused, and Andrew's grin softened. Their exchanges had always moved this way: a quick turn toward mischief, a little resistance, then the ordinary warmth underneath. Neither had to announce that the other was familiar. It appeared in the speed with which they found the right tone, in the jokes that required no explanation, and in the quiet confidence that a serious answer would still be there when they needed it.

The history between them was older than this expedition. It was not a single event that could be named as the beginning, nor a perfect sequence in which every promise had been kept without effort. They had accumulated one another through returns, challenges, small acts of attention, and the repeated discovery that the other man was more interesting than the first explanation suggested. They had learned how to disagree without making every difference a verdict. They had also learned that their best moments were rarely the ones either had planned. Andrew could make Kirk laugh by taking an absurd idea completely seriously; Kirk could make Andrew slow down by asking one exact question that revealed the real subject hidden underneath three jokes.

“Did you sleep?” Kirk asked.

“Enough.”

“That isn't an answer.”

“It's an Andrew answer.”

“Then I'll ask it again.”

Andrew looked down at the receiver. “Yes. I slept. The upper ledge was cold, but I had the blanket. Nobody fell, nobody got lost, and I didn't try to race the sunrise.”

“You were going to race it?”

“I was considering it.”

Kirk laughed. The sound arrived a little late, softened by the receiver and the distance between them, but Andrew recognised it. He had missed that particular laugh: not because it was rare, but because it belonged to the unguarded part of Kirk that appeared when the conversation stopped being a test.

The others returned to their work. Andrew remained by the post, listening as Kirk described what he had felt during the night. There had been a small tightening, followed by a change in the direction of pressure. It had not hurt him, but it had disturbed his sleep. The ridge had moved after the tightening, though not in the same direction as the previous day's shift. Kirk was unsure whether the movement had caused the change in the country or whether both had followed some third event that neither could yet identify.

Andrew asked him to describe the order again. Kirk did. Andrew listened without interrupting, then asked whether the sensation had started near the lower passage or farther away. Kirk corrected himself: it had begun farther away, and the pressure had travelled toward the lower passage. Andrew repeated the sequence back to him, not to prove that he had understood but to give Kirk a chance to change it. Kirk corrected one small detail. Andrew accepted the correction.

“Good,” Andrew said. “Now we have something to work with.”

“We?”

“You thought I was going to let you have all the interesting problems?”

“I thought you might find one of your own.”

“I already have. Your landscape keeps changing the rules.”

Kirk's reply came gently. “It isn't doing it to annoy you.”

“I know. That's what makes it interesting.”

There was the chemistry again, not as a mysterious force that excused them from understanding one another, but as a working ease. Kirk did not mistake Andrew's playfulness for a failure to care. Andrew did not mistake Kirk's deliberation for a lack of appetite for adventure. One could turn a serious problem into a game without denying its seriousness; the other could insist on careful limits without making the game feel smaller. They were able to move between those positions because neither needed to win every exchange.

Andrew took a baseball from the side pocket of his bag. He had carried it through the western route, more from habit than from any plan to use it. The ball looked almost absurdly ordinary beside the chamber's curved walls and the immense body of country beyond them. He set it on the floor near the cedar post and watched it roll. It travelled several feet, slowed, and came to rest against a shallow rise.

“The floor is lower on that side,” the surveyor said.

“Not quite,” Andrew replied. “The floor is turning.”

He retrieved the ball and set it down again, this time a little farther to the left. It followed a different path. Andrew adjusted the starting point, marked the two positions with small pieces of chalk, and tried a third time. The ball traced a curve that looked at first like an accident, then like a route.

Kirk asked what he was doing.

“Trying to find out whether your country has a favourite direction.”

“My country?”

“You heard me.”

“It doesn't belong to me in the way you're suggesting.”

“I know. I'm teasing you. But it is your body, and you know things about its movements that the rest of us don't. I can watch the ball. You can tell me what you felt. We can see whether the two accounts meet.”

Kirk agreed. Andrew ran the test again. This time Kirk described the faint movement he felt under the corresponding region. The ball shifted slightly before the receiver carried Kirk's words to the shelter. The delay made the comparison imperfect, but it gave them a useful question: whether a change in the floor could be detected before Kirk consciously noticed the movement, or whether the receiver's delay merely made the sequence appear that way. Andrew wrote the question on the edge of the map. He drew a small baseball beside it.

“That's the mark for a question?” Kirk asked.

“It's the mark for a question that deserves another pitch.”

“You have a symbol for everything.”

“Not everything. I haven't decided what the symbol for you should be.”

“Don't.”

Andrew laughed. “You see? That's why I keep you around. You save me from my worst ideas.”

Kirk said that Andrew had never needed much help producing those. Andrew answered that Kirk had a remarkable talent for making a criticism sound like an invitation. Their teasing moved on, but neither missed the truth underneath it. Kirk liked that Andrew could approach him without making his unusual scale the only subject in the room. Andrew liked that Kirk could be imposing without requiring everyone around him to become solemn. They could talk about a changing ridge, a crooked post, a baseball, lunch, or an old joke with the same familiar attention. Their affection was not a ceremony reserved for exceptional moments. It had a place in ordinary ones.

After the measurements were finished, Andrew carried the map to the far wall. He found a clear stretch of cedar-coloured surface and fixed the paper there with two small pegs. The surveyor had drawn the ridge, the shelter, the lower passage, and the route of the previous day's movement. Andrew added the three ball paths, then a line showing where Kirk had felt the pressure travel. He left a gap between the two sets of marks.

“Why leave a space?” Kirk asked through the receiver.

“Because they aren't the same kind of evidence. I don't want the map pretending they are.”

Kirk was quiet for a moment. “You've learned to do that.”

“I learned from someone.”

“You learned by arguing with me.”

“That's still learning.”

It was one of the things they gave one another: permission to change without having to deny the earlier version. Andrew had once rushed from an idea to its conclusion and expected everyone else to keep up. Kirk had learned to name what he felt without waiting for another person to certify it. Their closeness had not made either man into the other's copy. It had given each of them a companion who could recognise the change and remember what had preceded it.

They spoke for a long time after the others had gone to prepare the next route. The conversation wandered through the expedition, the strange trail, the possibility of another shelter farther below, and the ordinary pleasures they meant to return to when the day's work was over. Andrew promised to bring the ball back to the ridge. Kirk promised to keep the receiver open during the next movement, provided the connection remained clear. They made smaller promises too: to correct each other when the map became misleading, to say when they were tired, to let the other man finish an explanation, and to keep a place for a joke even when the problem was difficult.

At some point Andrew said, “We have made a ridiculous number of promises to each other.”

“We have.”

“And we keep making more.”

“Do you want to stop?”

“No. I want to know how many there are.”

Kirk considered it. “More than we can count.”

“Good.”

“That isn't a very careful answer.”

“It isn't meant to be. I'm making you an infinite promise.”

Kirk laughed again, and this time the sound seemed to arrive without delay. “You can't promise infinity.”

“I can promise the next one. Then the one after that. Then another when we need it. That's how you get there.”

Kirk understood. The promise was not that nothing would change, that neither would become tired, or that every future choice had already been made. It was that there would be room to choose again. They could return without pretending they had never been apart. They could make a new promise without using it to erase an old disappointment. They could be close without turning closeness into ownership, and they could give one another room without treating distance as abandonment.

“I promise to tell you when something changes,” Kirk said. “Even when I don't understand it yet.”

Andrew nodded. “I promise to listen to the whole thing before I start inventing explanations.”

“And when you invent them?”

“I'll label them as inventions.”

“That's a start.”

“I promise to come back when I say I will. And if I can't, I'll tell you why.”

Kirk answered, “I promise not to make your return a test you have to pass.”

Andrew looked at the map. The little baseball marks were still separate from the measured lines. “And I promise to leave you room to decide what you want. Even when I'm excited.”

“That one matters.”

“I know.”

The exchange did not end with a grand declaration. It ended with the receiver resting between them, the map held flat against the wall, and a meal being prepared on the other side of the shelter. Their promises were not a chain around the future. They were a way of keeping it open. Each one had a practical shape, and each could be renewed, revised, or joined by another. The infinite part was not that either man could control every day to come. It was that neither had to pretend the present was their last opportunity to choose one another.

Before the next expedition set out, Andrew took the ball and walked to the mouth of the lower passage. He placed it on the ground and waited for the small movement he and Kirk had been discussing. The ball rolled a short distance, paused against the curve, and then travelled toward the passage. Andrew watched it disappear around the bend.

“Your country has answered,” he said into the receiver.

“Has it?”

“Not in words.”

“Then don't tell me what it means yet.”

“I won't. I'll bring you the map.”

Kirk's voice grew warm. “Bring yourself back with it.”

Andrew looked over his shoulder at the cedar post, the shelter, and the men gathering their equipment. “That's already in the plan.”

The lower route waited below. The old trail was now familiar, but the dark opening beyond the Warm Table remained unentered. The new maps held both the known route and its latest alteration. But the expedition now had another kind of route: not a line across the map, but a relationship that could travel with them. Andrew and Kirk had not returned to an earlier version of themselves. They had found that the old chemistry still worked in the changed country, and that it could hold more than it had held before.

The next promise was already taking shape.
