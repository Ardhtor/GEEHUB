# Book II — Chapter IX: The Narrow Turn

The proposed challenge had been written beneath the last entry on the Evermeasure Board in Andrew's largest handwriting: **A LONGER PASSAGE. A NARROWER TURN. NO SCRAPES.**

Chase had added a smaller line below it: *Route and apparatus to be surveyed before any attempt.*

Andrew had returned the next morning to find that the smaller line had acquired a second sentence. *A successful passage must be repeatable by more than one pair of carriers.* He read it twice, then looked across the pavilion at Chase, who was arranging his measuring tools in a row.

“You've made my challenge sound like a public examination.”

“I've made it sound like a test,” Chase said.

“Those are usually the same thing.”

“Only if you haven't prepared.”

Andrew took the pencil from the ledge and added a third line: *The test must also be worth watching.*

Chase considered the addition. “That is a separate requirement.”

“It's an important one.”

The morning had begun cool and clear. The pavilion's open sides admitted the smell of cedar from the covered walk and the faint mineral scent of the fountain. Beyond the common green, the four houses were waking at different speeds. A lamp still burned in the upper gallery of Laskin Mansion. The Record House had its shutters open, and Chase's map room was already lit. Gabe's training court was quiet except for the sound of a mat being unrolled. At the House of Returns, Joseph had opened the sheltered door and set a small tray on the outer table. The country did not wake as one household. It woke as four homes that had chosen to share a morning.

Robert arrived carrying a rolled drawing. He had spent part of the previous evening considering the display frame's route and had sketched a temporary test arrangement that could be assembled inside the pavilion without altering the Cedar Span or the ground beyond it. Two adjustable uprights would establish the approach; a pair of movable crosspieces would define the turn; and a broad floor covering would protect the timber frame if the carriers needed to lower it. Nothing would be fixed to the country itself. If the test proved useful, the residents could decide later whether a permanent feature was needed.

Andrew unrolled the drawing. “That looks disappointingly sensible.”

“It is a mock-up,” Robert said. “It should be sensible.”

“Can we make it more difficult?”

“We can make it more exact. Difficulty is not a substitute for knowing what we're testing.”

Chase placed his measuring rod beside the drawing. “We need the frame's actual envelope, the widest point during the turn, the hand positions, and the space required by the carriers. The frame passed the Cedar Span once. That does not tell us whether it can pass through a narrower approach without changing the way it is held.”

Gabe came in from the training court. “And we need to know who is carrying it. Two men can be perfectly capable and still have different reaches, grips, and preferred pace. If the arrangement only works for one pair, it isn't a shared challenge.”

Andrew looked from one to the other. “You have all managed to make a simple carry into a discipline.”

Joseph arrived with a small oak object tucked beneath one arm. “It was already a discipline. You just hadn't noticed.”

The object was a corner cradle, shaped to support the lower rail of the display frame while the carriers changed their grips. It had two shallow channels and a rounded outer edge. Joseph had made it in the workshop beneath the House of Returns, using a removable pin so that it could be fitted without drilling into the frame. He set it on the table and turned it so the others could see the underside.

Robert picked it up. “This is very good.”

“It needs a trial,” Joseph said.

“Of course it does,” Andrew replied. “But it is already better than the thing I was going to suggest.”

“What were you going to suggest?”

“A rope.”

Joseph looked at the cradle again. “Then it is fortunate you didn't.”

The joke passed, and the morning settled into work. Michael was not expected that day; his visit had been for the previous exhibition, and nobody treated his absence as a gap that had to be filled. The frame had been built for the pavilion, the residents had their own hands and ideas, and the next attempt belonged to whoever chose to take part.

The first task was to establish the mock-up. Robert and Chase set the uprights on the pavilion floor, leaving enough room for the frame to approach from the western opening. The crosspieces could be moved inward or outward and raised or lowered. A length of pale cloth marked the floor beneath the turn. The arrangement looked less like a gate than a piece of theatre scenery, which pleased Robert. He wanted the test to be visible from the benches, but he also wanted the structure to disappear when it was not in use.

Chase checked the frame at rest. The lower rail had been fitted since Michael's carry, and the central pin was now secure. He measured the outer corners, then watched Robert rotate the frame by hand. The upper edge described a wider arc than the lower one. The turn would have to account for the whole object, not merely the point nearest the carrier.

“That's the first thing,” Chase said. “The frame does not turn around the hands. It turns around its centre, and the corners travel farther than either man expects.”

Andrew took hold of one end. “Then I'll expect it.”

“You can expect it all you like. The corner still moves.”

Gabe stepped beside him. “Let's do the first pass without trying to make it impressive.”

“That's an odd way to begin an exhibition.”

“It's how you get to the impressive part.”

They lifted the frame and approached the mock-up. Andrew took the western end; Gabe took the eastern. Joseph stood near the lower rail, ready to guide it, while Robert watched the upper corner. Chase remained outside the marked area with his notebook. The frame entered cleanly. At the turn, Andrew moved first, eager to bring his end around. Gabe felt the change through the timber and held his position for half a beat. The upper corner came close to the crosspiece. Andrew stopped before it touched.

“Nearly,” he said.

“Nearly is not a passage,” Chase replied.

“Nothing touched.”

“Because you stopped.”

“Which was the correct decision,” Gabe said. “We can learn from it.”

They lowered the frame onto the protected floor. Robert moved the crosspiece outward by a small amount, and Chase recorded the adjustment. The next attempt passed, but the carriers had to alter their hand positions twice. Joseph examined the lower rail and showed them where the cradle might keep the frame stable during the change. He fitted it, checked the removable pin, and asked Andrew to lift the end again.

The cradle did not make the object lighter. It made the change of grip more deliberate. Andrew could rest the lower rail for a moment without allowing the frame to roll, while Gabe moved his hand to the new position. The upper corner remained clear. The frame came through the turn and settled on the far side without a scrape.

Andrew looked at Joseph. “That's a win.”

“It's a useful result.”

“Those can be the same thing.”

“They can,” Joseph said. “But we haven't repeated it.”

Chase wrote the result as a first successful passage with the cradle, not as a record. Robert asked for the crosspiece to be returned to its earlier position so that the group could establish whether the change had truly mattered. They repeated the attempt. This time Andrew and Gabe passed through without the cradle, but the movement required a slower rotation and a longer pause. The difference was not dramatic. It was visible to anyone who watched the corners rather than the men.

Andrew wanted to claim that the cradle had made the passage elegant. Chase wanted another pair to test it. Joseph suggested that the next carriers should be Robert and Andrew, because Robert knew the frame's construction and Andrew had already learned the turn. Gabe agreed to supervise the warm-up and watch the hand positions. The challenge was becoming less about who could carry the frame and more about whether the arrangement could teach a new pair to do it well.

Robert removed his jacket and examined the grip. “I can take the lower end.”

Andrew grinned. “You have been waiting to compete.”

“I have been waiting for you to stop treating the frame as an opponent.”

“Everything is an opponent if you give it enough time.”

“Then you should be able to beat this piece of timber.”

The second pair began. Robert moved carefully, but his first instinct was to protect the frame's upper edge by keeping his end too high. Andrew adjusted in response and the lower rail tilted. Joseph called for them to stop before the turn. They set the frame down, and Robert studied the position of his hands.

“I was carrying the corner rather than the weight,” he said.

Gabe nodded. “You were trying to keep the top clear by lifting the whole end. That made Andrew compensate.”

Robert tried again with the cradle fitted. This time he let the lower rail rest while he changed his grip. Andrew waited. The frame moved through the turn with a small, even rotation. They passed the crosspiece without contact.

Andrew turned to the benches. “There. A second pair.”

Chase checked the record. “A second pair has passed the mock-up. We still need a third attempt on another day before we call it repeatable.”

“Why another day?”

“Because today you know where everything is. Tomorrow you may arrive tired, distracted, or carrying a different expectation. A result that only exists while the designer is standing beside it is not yet a reliable method.”

Andrew looked as if he might argue, then smiled. “Fine. Tomorrow, then.”

Robert returned from the theatre with a small wooden model under one arm. He had made it from the measurements of the display frame and the adjustable uprights, reducing the whole arrangement to something that could be turned beneath the theatre's lamps. The model was not a miniature of the pavilion; it was a working study of the corner that kept disappearing from view.

“Come and look,” he said. “I think the audience has been watching the wrong part.”

The miniature theatre was cool and dark compared with the open pavilion. Robert set the model on the low stage and brought up a broad wash of light from the left. A second light came from above, leaving the far corner in shadow as the model turned. From the seats, the movement seemed smooth. From the side aisle, the corner appeared to pass dangerously close to the upright.

Andrew stood and moved down the aisle. “That's the part I couldn't see.”

“You could see the frame,” Robert said. “You couldn't see the clearance.”

Chase came closer. “The view from the front conceals the distance between the corner and the crosspiece. The side view makes it visible, but it hides the carriers' hands.”

“Then show both,” Andrew said.

Robert changed the lighting and brought a second image onto the small screen beside the stage. One view followed the hands and the other followed the far corner. The two images did not agree in every instant because they were looking at different parts of the same movement. That was the point. The audience could see the whole passage only by allowing the views to remain distinct.

Joseph examined the model's lower edge. “The cradle is missing.”

“I haven't added it yet.”

“Then the model is showing the old method.”

Robert looked at him, then smiled. “You're right. Give me the revised piece.”

Joseph brought the cradle from the worktable. Robert fitted a small wooden copy to the model and repeated the turn. The far corner cleared the upright after the lower rail had settled into its channel. The difference was slight, but the second view made it unmistakable.

Andrew watched the two images again. He was still thinking about the speed record, but he could see a different kind of display taking shape: not a man carrying a frame as quickly as possible, but the movement made legible from the hands to the farthest corner. He wanted the audience to see the exact instant when the carriers stopped fighting the weight and began moving together.

“That's the exhibition,” he said. “Show the first attempt, then the cradle, then the two views. Let people see what changed.”

“Only if the record says which view is a model,” Chase replied. “It isn't footage of the actual passage.”

“Of course.”

Robert made a note to label the theatre study as a reconstruction. The actual attempts would remain in the record with their own observations. The model could explain the geometry, but it could not replace the event. Andrew accepted the distinction. He had wanted the display to be impressive; now he wanted it to be convincing.

When they returned to the pavilion, the frame was still resting in its cradle. The morning's work had acquired another form: a physical test, a revised support, a comparison drawing, and a small visual explanation that made the hidden corner visible. Robert carried the model back to the theatre, where it would remain available for another lighting test. The two images were not yet a finished presentation. They were a better way to ask the next question.

The men took a break while Robert adjusted the gallery lights. The frame was placed in its cradle beside the pavilion wall, and Joseph carried the corner support to a small worktable. He wanted to soften one edge and add a shallow mark showing which side faced outward. It was a modest improvement, but it would make the piece easier to fit correctly when someone else used it. Chase recorded the change as a revision to the cradle, not a new object. The first version remained in the workshop drawer.

Gabe prepared a simple recovery circuit for anyone who wanted to repeat the carry. It began with shoulder movement, wrist and hand preparation, and a short walk along the pavilion's open side. It was not compulsory, and he did not present it as a test. Andrew completed it while making exaggerated claims about how much stronger he felt. Robert joined him and discovered that the sequence was more useful than he expected. Joseph declined, saying he had spent enough time at the bench that morning. Nobody tried to persuade him otherwise.

When they returned, Chase had arranged the day's observations on a board. One column showed the original attempt, one showed the cradle-assisted turn, and one showed the second pair's passage. The record made the improvement plain without exaggerating it. The first attempt had required a stop. The revised grip had allowed the frame to turn without contact. The second pair had repeated the movement after instruction. The next test would establish whether the method remained usable after a night away from the apparatus.

Andrew studied the board. “I still think there should be a time.”

“There can be,” Chase said, “once we know the passage is safe and repeatable.”

“Then I want the fastest clean passage.”

“That's a different category.”

“It's the category I want.”

Gabe looked at him. “You can have it. But the first category should be the cleanest passage with the fewest corrections. Speed comes after control.”

Andrew considered the distinction. “All right. Two records. One for the cleanest method, one for the fastest clean run.”

“And no points for touching the frame to the crosspiece,” Chase said.

“Obviously.”

“And a stop remains a stop, even if the frame doesn't fall.”

Andrew sighed. “You're going to make the rules unbearable.”

“Only for people who want the record to say something.”

Robert laughed. “Put that on the board.”

They settled on two separate marks. The first would reward a controlled passage with no contact and no unplanned correction. The second would record the fastest passage that met the same conditions. A run that touched the mock-up or required an emergency stop would remain in the archive as a failed attempt, useful for understanding the method but ineligible for either mark. No one would be required to compete, and no one would be timed without agreeing to take part.

The distinction changed the mood. Andrew had wanted a race, but he could see that the two records gave him something more interesting. A man could first master the movement, then return to improve the time. Another could refuse the timed category and still earn a place in the exhibition by finding a more graceful method. The Evermeasure had acquired another branch without becoming a single ladder.

At midday, Joseph invited the group to eat at the House of Returns. The table was set with thick slices of bread, a bowl of lentils, roasted carrots, greens dressed with lemon, and a dish of chicken that had been cooked slowly with garlic and herbs. Robert brought a jar of pickled onions from his kitchen. Gabe arrived with a basket of fruit. Andrew carried the folded test drawing, which Joseph took from him and placed on a side table before anyone sat down.

“No drawings at lunch,” Joseph said.

“It's only one drawing.”

“Then it can wait.”

Andrew laughed and left it where it was. They ate with the doors open to the sheltered walk. The conversation wandered from the test to the miniature theatre, from the theatre to a film Robert wanted to show, and from the film to the question of whether the next exhibition should include work that had nothing to do with carrying or strength. Gabe suggested a demonstration of the recovery circuit. Joseph proposed a small display of workshop objects that had been improved through use. Robert wanted a lighting test. Andrew wanted a contest in which the men had to make something impressive from a limited set of materials.

“That's not a bad idea,” Joseph said.

Andrew looked pleased. “You admit it.”

“I said it wasn't bad.”

“That's practically a trophy from you.”

Joseph passed him the bread. “Don't spend it all at once.”

After lunch, the residents separated. Robert returned to Laskin Mansion to revise the miniature-theatre layout. Chase took the measurement sheets to the Record House and prepared a clean comparison drawing. Gabe returned to the Canvas House, where he had a private session to complete and a recovery note to write. Joseph went down to his workshop to finish the cradle. Andrew stayed in the pavilion, not because he had been assigned to watch it, but because he wanted to try the movement once more without an audience.

He set the uprights to the morning's final position and checked the crosspieces. He lifted the frame alone only far enough to understand the balance, then lowered it again. The full passage required two people. He knew that. Still, he wanted to understand the point at which his own impatience entered the movement. He had been so intent on making the turn look effortless that he had moved before the other carrier was ready. The frame had told him immediately.

Joseph came back carrying the revised cradle. He stopped at the edge of the marked floor.

“You're still here.”

“I wanted another look.”

“Do you want company?”

Andrew considered the question. “Yes, but not another attempt. I want you to tell me whether the drawing makes sense.”

Joseph put the cradle on the table and looked at the sketch. He pointed to the upper corner and traced the path it would follow. Andrew had drawn the turn as though the frame rotated around the near hand. Joseph showed him how the centre moved in relation to the floor marker and why the far corner needed more room than the drawing allowed. Andrew listened without interrupting.

“You see it now?” Joseph asked.

“Yes.”

“Good.”

Andrew looked at him. “I wanted you to see the first pass this morning. I kept thinking you'd say it was good.”

“I did see it.”

“I know. But I wanted you to say it.”

Joseph leaned against the table. “You can ask me what I think. You don't have to turn every thing into a contest to make me look.”

Andrew nodded. The remark did not sting as much as it might have earlier. He had asked for a technical opinion and received one. The rest was an admission he had not planned to make.

“I like it when you notice what I've done,” he said.

“I know.”

“And I like it when Kirk notices.”

“I know that too.”

Andrew looked down at the drawing. “I don't want to make you compete for a place beside me.”

“Then don't.”

The answer was plain. Joseph did not make it into a lesson, and Andrew did not try to turn it into a promise that would solve everything. He folded the drawing carefully and placed it on the table.

“I'll leave the next attempt for tomorrow,” he said.

“That's sensible.”

“Don't tell Chase.”

“I expect he already knows.”

They both laughed. Joseph set the revised cradle beside the frame. The new outward mark was small, cut into the oak rather than painted across it. It would remain visible after handling and could not be mistaken for a measurement. Andrew turned it over in his hands.

“That's better,” he said.

“It should be.”

The afternoon light moved through the pavilion as the residents returned to their own work. The test structure remained assembled, but its crosspieces were loosened and the frame was secured in its cradle. The arrangement was temporary. It had not altered the Cedar Span, the eastern hollow, or any part of Kirk's country. It had produced a method that could be tested again, and a new question about how a pair of carriers might move together without relying on one man's strength to compensate for the other's timing.

Near evening, Andrew received a message from Kirk. The note did not mention the challenge. Kirk asked whether Andrew still wanted their conversation the following morning. Andrew read it, then answered that he did. He did not send the day's measurements, ask for a movement, or turn the conversation into another invitation to watch. He said he would be ready at the agreed time.

Kirk replied with a simple confirmation.

At the Record House, Chase placed the morning's failed and successful attempts side by side. Robert adjusted the theatre lighting; Gabe left the training court ready for the next resident; Joseph kept the revised cradle beside his tools. Each house carried a different part of the day's work.

The pavilion stood open beneath the darkening trees. Tomorrow the residents would try the passage again. The Evermeasure would preserve what happened, whether they improved the method or found another fault. The Narrow Turn had become a test of shared control as much as strength, and the next attempt would show what they had learned.
