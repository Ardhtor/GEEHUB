# Book II — Chapter I: The Lost Country

The first mistake was assuming that Kirk's body had an edge.

The men had been following the ridge for nearly an hour. It rose ahead of them in a long, warm curve, broad enough to carry a road, a line of trees, and the remains of the little observation platform they had built that morning. Behind them, the familiar landmarks had dwindled into a distant arrangement of shadows. Ahead, the country narrowed and rose again, opening into a scale that made their maps look like notes written on a hand.

They had come to explore Kirk's enormous cock. That fact had been obvious from the beginning, and nobody in the expedition had mistaken the destination. What they had not understood was how completely the place would exceed the language they had brought with them. From a distance, the anatomy had seemed like one immense landmark, a single astonishing feature of a man whose proportions had become difficult to reconcile with ordinary rooms and ordinary measurements. Once they were there, it became a whole country: ridges, valleys, sheltered hollows, long slopes, and passages that turned out of sight. The men had entered with the confidence of visitors approaching a monument. They were now learning the habits of travellers.

The morning had begun with a simple arrangement. The expedition leader had wanted a fixed observation point from which the surveyor could take a first set of bearings. They had chosen a section of the ridge that appeared broad and level, then used timber braces to raise a small platform above the uneven surface. The platform was not intended as a permanent building. It was a place from which the men could compare one view with another without allowing a change in their own position to confuse the record. They had driven a cedar post at the western edge and marked the route back to the lower path with a line of pale stones. The work had taken longer than expected because every measurement depended on a reference that could be found again.

Marcus had been the first to object to the surveyor's method.

“Stop marking the distance in steps,” he said. “It isn't helping.”

The surveyor lowered his measuring staff. “It helped yesterday.”

“Yesterday the ridge was straight.”

“It is still straight.”

“It has curved around us.”

“That isn't the same thing.”

Marcus looked back along the route. The cedar post was visible, but the platform had shifted in the perspective of the ridge. It seemed lower than it had when they began, although the surveyor's notes showed that its height had not changed. A shallow valley that had appeared to lie behind them was now visible on their left. The line of trees had become a broken line, interrupted by a fold in the surface that none of them remembered crossing.

The expedition leader came over and examined the map. He did not tell either man that he was wrong. He asked the surveyor to take another bearing and Marcus to mark the new direction of the ridge. They did so from the same place. The numbers disagreed with the first reading, though not by enough to prove whether the country had moved or the instruments had been misaligned. The leader entered both results and left a space between them.

“We can argue about the cause later,” he said. “For now, we need to know where we are.”

The argument was interrupted by a low movement beneath the ground. It travelled through the ridge, lifted the loose dust from the stones, and passed away toward the distant rise. The men stood still. Their instruments trembled in their hands. Far behind them, the observation platform shifted several yards from the position recorded on the morning map.

Nobody called it an earthquake. They had already learned that the ordinary names were inadequate. Kirk had moved, perhaps only slightly, and the country had moved with him.

The first concern was not the map. It was whether anyone had been hurt. The leader counted the men, checked that the platform had not struck anyone, and sent two of them to examine the line of stones. One had been pushed into the dust; another had disappeared beneath a fresh fold in the surface. The cedar post remained visible, though it had tilted. The timber supports had not broken, but the eastern feet no longer met the ground. The platform was suspended over a shallow hollow that had not been there when they built it.

They recovered the instruments first. The surveyor wrapped the most delicate pieces in cloth and placed them in the carrying case. Marcus and another man steadied the frame while the leader removed the loose boards. No one tried to force the platform back into its old position. The surface beneath it had changed, and they had no reason to trust the ground simply because it had once held their weight. They set the cedar post upright again, then placed a second marker beside it to show where it had stood after the movement.

The surveyor measured the separation between the two markers. The new distance was entered beside the old one, with the time and the direction of the displacement. It was the first record of a place changing while they watched it.

That was when they discovered the old trail.

It descended along the far side of the ridge, concealed behind a fold in the terrain. The trail was unmistakably made by people. Its surface had been cleared, and small stones had been placed along the margins. At intervals, narrow posts carried symbols cut into the wood. The markings were not theirs. Some were weathered almost smooth; others looked recent. One post bore a simple drawing of a broad-shouldered man with a line beneath his feet. Another showed a door, though there was no door anywhere in sight.

The expedition leader crouched beside it. “Someone has been here before us.”

“Or someone is still here,” Marcus said.

The surveyor examined the post without touching the cuts. The wood had darkened in the shallow grooves, and dust had settled along the lower edge. A small branch had grown around one side of the marker, holding it in place. The trail was not a fresh construction made in response to the morning's movement. It had been here long enough to become part of the country.

They followed it only as far as the next turn. The leader wanted to know whether it led toward a safe place to stop or into a passage that could close behind them. The surveyor marked every bend. Marcus watched the ground and looked for signs that the trail had been maintained. The stones were fitted together with a care that suggested repeated use. In one place, a section had been repaired with a different kind of stone. In another, a post had been replaced, but the old base remained beside it.

The descent took them through a sheltered region where the light changed from the open brightness of the ridge to a deep amber glow. The air was warmer. The walls curved inward, creating a long natural corridor whose surface carried faint patterns like the grain of polished cedar. The men found places where earlier travellers had rested: a flat stone arranged as a seat, a small fire ring, a length of cord tied around a post. There were no remains of a camp, no abandoned provisions, and no sign of panic. Whoever had made the trail had expected to return.

The leader called another halt at the fire ring. He asked the surveyor to check the air and the floor, then sent a man back to the ridge to confirm that the return route remained visible. The man returned with the agreed signal: a reflector flash from the upper path, followed by a second flash from the platform. The signal took longer than expected to arrive. The corridor bent in a way that made the reflector hard to see, and the men adjusted the route mark rather than trying to make the signal reach through the stone.

Marcus examined the cord tied around the post. It was old but not brittle. Someone had knotted it at shoulder height, then wrapped the loose end around the wood so that it would not trail underfoot.

“Not a marker,” he said. “A handhold.”

The surveyor looked at it. “Could be.”

They recorded the possibility without assigning it a settled meaning. The cord remained where it was. A little farther along, the corridor widened, and a second length of cord had been fixed across a shallow drop. The men used it one at a time. It held under their weight, though the leader asked them not to lean on it more than necessary. They had not made the trail, and the fact that it had survived earlier journeys did not guarantee that it was still safe.

At the end of the corridor, the men found a chamber large enough to hold their entire company. Its floor descended gently toward a dark opening. A line of pale light travelled along the far wall, brightened, and disappeared. They could hear a slow rhythm beneath the stone. It was not quite a heartbeat, although it seemed to belong to the same living country.

They stayed near the entrance and took their first readings. The chamber's dimensions were greater than the corridor had suggested. The roof rose beyond the reach of their lamps, and the far wall curved away before turning out of sight. The dark opening sat low in the floor, beyond a shallow shelf of smooth stone. Nothing about it indicated whether it was a passage, a hollow, or a space that had been exposed by the recent movement.

The surveyor set a measuring line between two points near the entrance. He checked the distance, moved the instrument, and repeated the reading. The numbers agreed. When he returned to the first point, the distance had changed.

He looked again at the staff.

“Don't reset it yet,” the leader said. “Mark the time.”

The surveyor entered the second reading below the first. Marcus went to the edge of the shelf and examined the dark opening from a safe distance. The air coming from it was warmer than the air in the chamber. The vibration under the floor seemed stronger near the lip, but the men could not tell whether it was coming from the opening or travelling through the chamber from somewhere beyond it.

The leader called a halt. They recorded the chamber's dimensions, checked the air, and marked the route back to the ridge. Their instruments showed that the corridor had shifted since their first measurements. The distance between two posts had increased by nearly a third. The change was too consistent to be a simple error.

Marcus looked at the map. “We aren't walking across a fixed place.”

“No,” the surveyor said. “We're walking across Kirk.”

The sentence settled over the company. It changed the way they regarded the chamber, the trail, and the distant rhythm. They were not explorers crossing an unfamiliar landscape that happened to resemble a body. They were explorers inside a living man's extraordinary anatomy, and the distinction imposed responsibilities. Kirk could move. Kirk could feel. The country could change without warning, and the men had to remain attentive to the person whose body made their journey possible.

The leader asked everyone to step back from the dark opening. No one objected. It was tempting to treat the chamber as a discovery that belonged to them, especially after the long descent and the first glimpse of a route that had not appeared on their own map. But the place was not theirs. Their instruments could tell them where the floor had shifted; they could not tell them what the movement meant to Kirk. A measurement did not give them permission to proceed.

They established a temporary camp in the chamber, well away from the dark opening. The cedar post from the ridge was brought down and set beside the entrance. It became the first marker of the lower route. They named the chamber the First Shelter, not because they believed it was the first place of its kind, but because it was the first place where the expedition could stop without losing sight of its way back.

The camp was arranged along the higher side of the chamber. The men kept the central floor clear, leaving a broad route between the entrance and the instruments. One man checked the lamps while another sorted the provisions. They ate a small meal standing near the wall, then made a second round of measurements before allowing themselves to rest. The leader did not want anyone sleeping beside the dark opening. He asked two men to keep watch in shifts, not because he expected an attack, but because the chamber itself might change before morning.

The first map had been drawn on a single large sheet. It was already too simple. The surveyor divided the new page into layers: the ridge as it had been at the start of the morning, the ridge after the movement, the old trail, the corridor, and the chamber. He used a lighter line for anything that had not been checked twice. The dark opening was marked with a small outline and left blank inside.

Marcus watched him work.

“You've drawn the same place twice.”

“I've drawn two states of the place.”

“Someone will think we've made a mistake.”

“Then they'll have to read the dates.”

Marcus considered the map. “Keep both, then.”

The surveyor looked up. “I was going to.”

That evening, a message arrived from Kirk.

It came through the small receiver they had been carrying since the expedition began. His voice was calm, though the sound arrived with a slight delay. The leader answered and gave their position. Kirk asked whether the men were safe, and whether the movement on the ridge had caused any injuries.

The leader described the shifted platform, the old trail, and the chamber. He told Kirk that the men were safe and had established a temporary camp. He did not claim to understand the movement or the purpose of the dark opening. He described the warmth and the slow vibration, then read out the measurements that had changed.

“I felt the movement,” Kirk said. “Are you all right?”

“No one has been hurt.”

“Good. Don't go farther tonight.”

The leader looked toward the dark opening. “We found a trail. Someone has used it before.”

There was a pause. The men could hear the faint electrical sound of the receiver and, behind it, the soft movement of their own camp.

“Then record it,” Kirk said. “Don't assume you know where it goes.”

The connection remained open for another moment. The leader asked whether Kirk had felt the movement as a single shift or as several smaller changes. Kirk said he could describe what he had noticed, but he could not see the expedition's measurements and did not want to guess at the route. He had felt a tightening and a gradual release. The leader told him that the instruments had recorded a change in the ridge and another in the chamber. Kirk listened, then asked the men to send the readings when they had prepared them.

The connection ended. The men sat quietly for a while. Kirk had not ordered them to abandon the expedition. He had asked them to be careful, and the difference mattered. Their exploration depended on his permission, but it also depended on their own judgment. They could not treat his body as an inert landscape, nor could they treat every movement as a message intended for them.

The leader wrote the report while the surveyor checked the figures. Marcus read the description of the old trail and asked that the symbols be copied exactly, including the weathered marks that were difficult to see. They sent the message through the receiver and waited for confirmation. Kirk acknowledged it and said he would tell them if he noticed another movement. He did not promise that he could predict one.

Before sleeping, they drew the chamber again. The new map was larger than the first, with the ridge, the shifted platform, the concealed trail, and the First Shelter marked in separate layers. They left the dark opening blank. Beyond it, the country continued.

During the night, the men on watch heard the slow rhythm beneath the chamber floor. It rose and faded, never quite becoming a sound that could be separated from the movement of the walls. The pale line along the far side appeared twice. On the second occasion it remained visible long enough for the surveyor to make a note of its direction. He did not wake the whole camp. He marked the time and waited for the next observation.

By morning, the cedar post at the entrance had moved several inches. The chamber had widened, and the pale line along the far wall had become a narrow passage. The men measured the change, checked one another's equipment, and prepared a second expedition. The old opening was still present, but its relation to the floor had altered. The new passage offered another possible route, though none of the men assumed that it was safe merely because it had appeared.

They entered the day's measurements twice: once to describe the chamber as they had found it, and again to describe the chamber as it was now. The first page was not discarded. The second did not replace it. Between them lay the record of what had happened while the men were trying to understand where they stood.

The first expedition had not found its way out. It had found the beginning of the world.

## Continuity record

- **Book:** II — the Kirk expedition.
- **Premise:** A company of adult men becomes lost while exploring the impossible scale of Kirk's enormous penis, with Kirk's knowledge and permission.
- **Opening location:** The ridge, the concealed old trail, and the First Shelter.
- **Growth rule:** Changes in Kirk's body alter the geography; each return must inherit and extend the previous state rather than repeat it.
- **Agency and safety:** Kirk remains a person, not an inert setting. The expedition communicates with him, records uncertainty, and makes its own careful decisions.
- **New evidence practice:** The expedition records the first state and the changed state separately. A new measurement does not erase its predecessor.
- **Open questions:** Who made the old trail? Where does the dark opening lead? How many earlier travellers have passed through this country?
