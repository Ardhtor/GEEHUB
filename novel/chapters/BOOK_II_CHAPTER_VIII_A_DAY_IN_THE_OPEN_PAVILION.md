# Book II — Chapter VIII: A Day in the Open Pavilion

Michael Brock arrived at the common green before the pavilion had filled. The morning was still cool, and a narrow band of sunlight lay across the grass between the fountain and the playing field. He crossed the green in faded cut-off denim shorts, bare-chested, his broad shoulders and enormous arms moving with a controlled heaviness that made the pavilion posts seem narrower than they had the day before. His light-brown hair was short and slightly untidy. A close beard followed the line of his jaw, and his expression, serious when he was thinking, gave way to an easier look when he saw Joseph setting out the day's tools.

Michael had become used to the first pause. Men noticed him before they spoke. His chest was exceptionally broad, his deltoids rounded into the upper arms, and the weight of his thighs and calves made each step look deliberate. He did not hurry to explain himself. He put his small tool case on the bench, greeted Joseph, and asked what needed doing.

“Robert wants the new frame carried to the Cedar Span,” Joseph said. “Chase has marked the route. Gabe is checking the supports.”

“Then I'll see the frame before I carry it.”

Joseph smiled. “That's probably why they asked you.”

The pavilion had already begun to wake. Robert was standing beside a timber structure laid out on trestles. It was part of a removable display frame for the Evermeasure Board, built to hold a large map without covering the earlier entries. Chase had placed a measuring rod beside it and was comparing the frame's dimensions with the width of the covered passage. Gabe came across from the training court carrying a folded mat and two adjustable supports. Andrew was not there yet, which meant, Joseph said, that he was probably inventing something.

Michael looked over the frame. It was broad enough to require care at the corners, and its weight was uneven because the lower rail had not yet been fitted. He ran one hand along the joint, pressed his thumb against the brace, and asked Robert whether the central pin had been fixed.

“Not yet,” Robert said. “I wanted to see how it behaved before we locked it.”

“Then we shouldn't lift it by the upper rail.”

Chase looked up from his notebook. “I had the same concern.”

“You could have said so,” Robert replied.

“I was about to.”

Gabe set his mat down. “Before anyone lifts anything, Michael is warming up.”

Michael glanced at him. “I can lift it now.”

“I expect you can. That's not the point.”

The remark was plain enough that Michael laughed. He followed Gabe to the training court, where the equipment had been arranged for a short sequence of movements rather than a contest. Gabe showed him the shoulder and hip warm-up, then adjusted the support height so that Michael could move without forcing his joints into an awkward position. His arms were so large that the first set of supports looked almost delicate beside them, but the equipment was built to adjust. Michael took his time. He rolled his shoulders, opened and closed his hands, shifted his weight through his feet, and repeated the movement until the tightness left his upper back.

Gabe watched without turning the session into a performance. “Better?”

“Much.”

“You looked ready to pick up the whole building when you arrived.”

“I was considering it.”

“Robert would have asked you to put it back exactly where it was.”

Michael grinned. “Then I'd better start with the frame.”

By the time they returned, Andrew had arrived with the baseball and two new wooden markers. He examined Michael's shoulders, then looked at the unfinished frame.

“I was going to suggest a carry,” he said. “I see we've already got one.”

“You can suggest the route,” Michael replied. “But Chase gets to tell us whether it fits.”

Andrew looked delighted. “A division of labour. Very civilised.”

The first carry was not timed. Michael and Gabe lifted the frame from opposite ends, while Joseph steadied the lower rail and Robert watched the joints. The frame rose evenly, but the turn toward the covered walk was tighter than it had appeared on the plan. Michael stopped before the corner, lowered his end by an inch, and waited while Gabe adjusted his grip. They turned together. The timber passed between the posts without touching either one.

Chase marked the clearance. “That is the first clean passage.”

“First attempt,” Andrew said. “We can do better.”

“First clean passage,” Chase repeated. “The record should say what happened, not what you want to happen next.”

Michael set the frame into the temporary cradle. He did it slowly, with his fingers clear of the joint and his shoulders working under the load. The frame settled without a knock. Robert inspected the corners and found no fresh mark.

“Good,” he said. “Now I can fit the central pin.”

The second attempt used a different route. Andrew wanted the frame to pass through the pavilion's western opening, cross the common green, and approach the Cedar Span from the sheltered side. It was longer, but the turn was easier. Gabe suggested that Michael carry the heavier end while Joseph guided the lower rail. Michael agreed, provided they stopped at the resting bay rather than trying to prove they could finish without a pause.

Andrew laughed. “You've made the challenge sound responsible.”

“It is responsible.”

“That's not usually what people come here for.”

“Then today you'll get something new.”

They began again. The frame moved across the green in the morning light. Michael's chest and arms tightened under the weight, but his steps remained measured. He watched the ground in front of Joseph, matched his pace, and let the frame settle whenever the path changed. At the Cedar Span they stopped in the resting bay. Beyond the viewing slots, the eastern ground opened toward the hollow, and the far side seemed to sit at a different angle from the one Chase had drawn the previous evening.

Michael drank from the bottle Gabe offered him. Andrew rolled the baseball between his palms.

“I could have done that faster,” Andrew said.

“You weren't carrying it,” Joseph replied.

“I could have carried the other end.”

“Then you can do it on the next attempt.”

Andrew looked at Michael. “Would you let me?”

“Certainly. But you'll have to listen when I tell you to stop.”

Andrew considered this, then nodded. “That's fair.”

They returned to the pavilion after the frame had been placed. Robert fitted the central pin and tightened the lower brace. Chase recorded the route, the measured clearance, and the resting point. He left the weight field blank until it could be established with the proper equipment. Michael watched him write and approved.

“Don't estimate it from how it felt,” he said.

“I wasn't going to.”

“I know. I like that.”

The common kitchen had begun serving lunch by then. The men carried their plates to the long table beneath the pavilion roof. There was warm bread, roasted peppers, white beans with herbs, grilled tomatoes, and a large dish of eggs. Someone had made coffee strong enough that Andrew declared it a competitive event. Robert wanted a second pot. Chase said the first one had been measured correctly. Gabe took his plate outside and sat where he could see the training court without being watched by everyone who passed.

Michael sat beside Joseph. His shoulders filled much of the space above the bench, and he had to move his arms carefully when reaching for the bread. He listened while the others argued about whether the new frame should remain on the Cedar Span or be brought back to the pavilion after each exhibition. He suggested that it should have a storage position of its own, protected from rain and separate from the map wall. Robert liked the idea. Chase asked for a measurement of the storage alcove. Joseph said he knew a carpenter who could make a pair of removable rests.

Andrew looked from one man to another. “You've managed to turn lunch into another improvement meeting.”

“Only because you keep bringing us things to improve,” Gabe said.

“I bring opportunities.”

“You bring problems with good introductions.”

Michael laughed, and Andrew accepted the criticism as praise. The table remained noisy for a while. They spoke about the Long Return, the eastern hollow, the next exhibition, and a small repair needed on the pavilion's western door. There was no need to keep the conversation on Michael's physique. He was pleased when the others noticed what he could do, but he also enjoyed being asked what he thought about a hinge.

After lunch he went with Joseph to the Cedar Span to look at the oak bench. The bench had been moved slightly during the morning's carry, and one foot no longer sat perfectly against the timber floor. Joseph knelt to inspect it. Michael crouched beside him, the enormous muscles of his thighs folding beneath him, and held the bench steady while Joseph eased a small wedge into place.

“Try it now,” Joseph said.

Michael sat down. The bench gave a quiet creak, then held.

“Better,” he said.

“You're a severe test.”

“I've been called worse.”

Joseph laughed. He sat at the other end, and for a little while they watched the eastern hollow through the open side of the passage. The afternoon light had changed the colour of the timber. A few men were crossing the far path, but nobody was hurrying. The bench had been made for precisely this: a place where a man could stop without turning the stop into a failure.

“You could have gone back to the pavilion,” Joseph said.

“I wanted to see whether the bench was right.”

“And now?”

“Now it's right.”

Joseph nodded. He did not ask Michael to explain why he had stayed. They sat until the shade moved across the boards, then returned by the covered walk.

Andrew had found a new use for the frame. He wanted to carry it through the western opening once more, but this time without a spoken count. The two carriers would have to listen to one another's movement and keep the corners level through the turn. Michael agreed to take one end. Andrew took the other.

The first few steps were uneven. Andrew tried to move too quickly, and Michael stopped.

“Again,” Michael said.

“I know what you're going to say.”

“Then do it before I have to.”

Andrew laughed, but he slowed down. They began again. This time he waited for Michael's shoulder to settle before moving his own end. Their steps found a common rhythm. The frame crossed the green, passed the western opening, and reached the cradle without a scrape. It was not the fastest attempt of the day, but it was the cleanest.

Chase made a new entry. Andrew leaned over his shoulder.

“You're going to write that I won.”

“I'm going to write that you listened.”

“That's almost as good.”

Gabe added a note about the adjusted grip and the value of a rest point. Robert marked the frame as ready for its first exhibition. Joseph checked the bench once more. Michael looked at the board, where the day's work had become a record, and saw that his name appeared beside the carry rather than above the others. The distinction pleased him. He had come to take part, not to have the whole gathering rearranged around him.

The pavilion began to empty in the late afternoon. Robert went to check the theatre lighting. Chase carried his notes back to the Record House. Gabe returned to the Canvas House, leaving the training court prepared for whoever wanted it next. Joseph walked toward the House of Returns with the small tools under his arm. Andrew lingered, still thinking about a new version of the carry.

Michael stayed for a final look at the Cedar Span. His body had been the most conspicuous thing about him when he arrived, but the day had given it other uses: lifting without striking, holding a frame level, steadying a bench, and matching another man's pace. He flexed his hands once, then let them rest at his sides. He was tired in the ordinary way a good day's work left a man tired.

The evening message came through the private channel. It was from Kirk. He asked whether Michael had enjoyed the exhibition and whether the day had been too demanding.

Michael read it away from the pavilion, where no one could look over his shoulder. He answered that he had enjoyed the company, that Gabe had made him warm up properly, and that Andrew had finally learned to carry something without trying to race it. He asked how Kirk's own day had gone.

Kirk replied that it had been quiet. He had listened to the reports, but had not wanted to turn the afternoon into another public event. He was pleased that Michael had been there. Michael read the sentence twice. It was simple, and it belonged to him.

Michael left the board as it was. Tomorrow another man would bring a different idea, and someone else would answer it. The day had given Michael a place in the district: a man who could lift an extraordinary weight, listen when another man asked him to stop, and end the evening with a private kindness.

The pavilion lights remained on after he had gone. The frame stood safely in its cradle, and the bench held its place on the Cedar Span. Beyond it, the Twin Grounds rested in their changing shapes. The Long Return waited for morning.
