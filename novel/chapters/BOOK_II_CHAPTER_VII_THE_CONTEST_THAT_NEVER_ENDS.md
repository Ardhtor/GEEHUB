# Book II — Chapter VII: The Contest That Never Ends

The men had not finished talking about the Long Return. They had finished playing it for the day, put the ball away, checked the eastern hollow, and returned to their separate houses, but the game had followed them indoors. Andrew had drawn three new versions on the back of a breakfast sheet. Chase had measured the route again from the archived positions and found a way to make the first report more exact. Gabe had thought of a training circuit that would let a player approach the course from either side. Robert had spent an hour in the miniature theatre testing how the route might be projected at a larger scale. Joseph had taken the lower line twice in his mind and found a more graceful return.

None of them regarded this as an inability to leave a game alone. It was the game doing what a good game was supposed to do. One man's achievement made another man's imagination restless. A route that had been difficult on Monday became an ordinary starting point by Tuesday. What had impressed them at first was now something to improve.

The shared pavilion stood at the centre of the green, a broad timber building open on three sides, with a roof deep enough to keep rain from the benches and a long wall left clear for notices. Robert had proposed that the wall be used for a permanent exhibition of the residents' best work. Chase objected to the word *best*, because the word invited a ranking before anyone had agreed on a measure. Andrew objected to the suggestion that the exhibition should be quiet. Gabe wanted enough room for demonstrations to be repeated without turning the pavilion into a training hall. Joseph wanted a place where the men could put their work on display and then sit down to eat.

They met there the next morning and discovered that each had brought something.

Andrew arrived first with the ball and a new set of wooden arches. He had arranged them across the field so that a throw could pass beneath the first, strike the bank at an angle, return through the second, and finish near the original marker. The route was improbable enough to make Chase stop and look. Andrew stood beside it with his arms folded, waiting for the question.

“You've changed the game,” Chase said.

“I've improved it.”

“You've added two unmeasured surfaces.”

“I've added two opportunities.”

“That's not the same thing.”

“It is if you can make the ball come back.”

Chase took out his notebook. Andrew smiled. The argument was already a kind of applause.

Gabe arrived carrying a folded mat and a set of adjustable supports. He had devised a circuit that brought the player from a short warm-up into a series of controlled movements, then released him onto the Long Return at whichever point he chose. It was not a test of who could last longest. It was a test of how cleanly a man could change pace, recover, and continue. He demonstrated the first section, made a small adjustment to the support, and repeated it. The second version was smoother. He watched Chase notice the difference and smiled as though the observation were part of the prize.

Robert brought a plan for a new gallery on the pavilion wall. It would hold the route maps, the timed records, the photographs of each course variation, and small models of the arches and supports. The models would be removable. A later exhibition could replace the current arrangement without destroying it. Above the gallery, he intended to place a narrow band of polished wood on which each year's most unusual achievement could be written.

“Each year's?” Andrew asked. “You think this is going to go on for years?”

Robert looked at the field, where the new arches were waiting. “You have already made three versions of the game.”

“Four,” Chase said. “The second sketch has a different return angle.”

Andrew looked delighted. “Then it will go on for longer than years.”

Joseph arrived with a small oak bench that he had made for the Cedar Span. It was not ornate. The seat was wide enough for two men to sit without crowding each other, and the lower rail was set at a height that allowed a tired player to rest his feet. Joseph had rounded the corners by hand. He had also placed a small marker at one end, so that the bench could be returned to its exact position after the passage was checked.

Gabe examined the joints. “That's better than the one we have.”

“It doesn't move when you sit down,” Joseph said.

“Neither does the other one.”

“It moves a little.”

Chase checked the marker. “He's right.”

Andrew looked at the bench, then at Joseph. “You made a better bench than the one we already had.”

“I did.”

“Without telling anyone.”

“I was making a bench.”

Andrew laughed. “That's a very Joseph way to win.”

Joseph set the bench down. “I wasn't aware there was a contest.”

“There is now,” Andrew said.

The remark drew a laugh from the others, including Joseph. It was not quite a boast and not quite a challenge. The men had already begun to understand that the contests did not need an official start. A man could enter one by improving a tool, remembering a detail, or making a thing so well that the rest of the group had to look again.

Chase proposed that the pavilion wall should preserve not only winners but the method by which a result had been achieved. He wanted dates, starting conditions, routes, measurements, and a clear distinction between a record and a demonstration that had not yet been repeated. He suggested a board with separate columns for the current mark, the previous mark, and the next challenge. Andrew said that sounded like a scoreboard designed by a man who did not enjoy winning.

“It is a board designed by a man who wants to know what winning means,” Chase replied.

Robert liked the idea of a permanent record. Gabe wanted the board to leave space for different kinds of achievement. Joseph said that a man ought to be able to put something on the wall without having to claim that it was better than everything else. Andrew considered this, then suggested that the next challenge could be written underneath every entry.

“What would you call it?” Gabe asked.

Andrew looked around the pavilion. “The Evermeasure.”

Chase repeated the word, testing it. “It assumes that every result can be measured.”

“No,” Andrew said. “It assumes there is always another thing to try.”

Robert wrote it on the first clean panel. Beneath it, Joseph placed the marker from his bench. Chase entered the first Long Return measurements. Gabe added the controlled circuit. Andrew drew the double-arch throw. Robert pinned the model of the viewing screen. No one wrote a final winner. There was no column for the greatest man, the strongest man, or the man who had most impressed Kirk. The board was intended to preserve the work and make the next attempt possible.


For the first hour, the board worked exactly as they intended. The entries described an achievement, the method used, and a question that could guide the next attempt. Then Andrew added a small row of tally marks beside the double-arch route. He meant it as a joke, a way to remember how many times he had tried it. Gabe added a mark beside the circuit. Robert drew a small symbol beside the theatre model. Within minutes, the marks had begun to look like a ranking.

Chase erased the row.

Andrew stared at him. “What was that for?”

“It doesn't mean anything.”

“It means attempts.”

“Then write attempts. Don't put them in a column that looks like points.”

Andrew wanted to argue. He liked the idea of a visible contest, something that could be beaten and improved. But he also knew that the marks had begun to change the way the men spoke. They were asking who had more, rather than what had worked. Joseph's bench had been praised for its craft, then overlooked because it did not fit the new tally. Michael's lift had become the thing everyone wanted to repeat, whether or not he wanted to do it again.

Robert suggested that the board should have different kinds of entries. A route could have a clean passage record; a design could have a best-fit version; a demonstration could have a verified result; a recovery method could be preserved because it helped the men continue. None of those needed to be converted into one number. The board could show what a man had done without pretending that every achievement could be compared on the same scale.

Andrew looked at the erased space. “So we keep the contest and lose the leaderboard.”

“We keep the contest,” Joseph said. “We don't turn the men into the leaderboard.”

Andrew laughed once, not because the distinction was funny, but because he knew it was right. Chase wrote the attempt count in the margin and labelled it clearly. The board remained a record, not a verdict.

Michael Brock arrived while they were still arranging the entries.

He was a very large, heavily muscled man, and his presence changed the scale of the gathering before he had spoken. His shoulders and arms filled the simple outline of his training shirt; the size of his build was immediately apparent beside the pavilion's broad timber posts. He was not introduced as a resident of any of the four mansions, and no one assigned him a place there. He had come to take part in the exhibition.

Andrew looked him over with open interest. “You're going to make the rest of us work.”

Michael smiled. “That's what I thought I'd come for.”

It was the sort of answer the group could use. Andrew showed him the double-arch route. Gabe explained the warm-up circuit. Chase described the measurement rules, and Robert showed him the exhibition wall. Joseph made room beside the bench and asked whether Michael wanted to see how the lower route returned. Michael accepted each explanation without pretending he had already mastered it. He listened, looked, and then asked where the equipment was stored.

The next demonstration began with a long timber beam that Robert had brought to test the new gallery supports. It had to be lifted, carried across the field, and set into a cradle without twisting the joints. Andrew suggested two men. Gabe proposed a paired lift from the ends. Michael stepped forward and took the beam in both hands. His muscles tightened visibly as he raised it, but his movement was controlled; he carried it to the cradle and settled it into place without striking the timber against the supports.

For a moment the pavilion went quiet.

Andrew whistled. “All right.”

Michael stepped back. “It was a good beam.”

“It was a very large beam,” Chase said.

“And now it's in the right place,” Joseph added.

Gabe checked the cradle and found that the load had been set evenly. Robert looked at the gallery support and decided it could be made narrower. Andrew was already imagining a second lift, perhaps with a longer beam, or a more awkward turn. Michael saw the expression and laughed.

“You're planning the next one.”

“Of course.”

“Then make it interesting.”

The others laughed again. The demonstration had been impressive, but it had not ended the day. It had given the day another standard to work from. Robert revised the support. Gabe devised a safer route for the carry. Chase recorded the weight only after it could be established, rather than guessing from appearance. Andrew proposed a timed version. Joseph suggested that the beam should be carried by two men on the return, so that the next attempt could show coordination rather than simply strength.

Michael agreed to try it. The second lift was slower, but more exact. It required both men to adjust their pace. The beam reached the cradle without a sound. Andrew declared it the better performance, then immediately asked whether they could repeat it with a longer turn.

The men understood the pattern. The first demonstration had been a show of strength. The second became a show of coordination. The next might be a test of design, balance, timing, or accuracy. There would always be a way to change the question so that the previous answer was no longer enough.


Andrew proposed a timed version before the men had finished discussing the second carry. He wanted to know how quickly the beam could be brought to the cradle without losing the clean turn. Chase said the route had to be fixed first. Gabe asked whether the carriers had recovered from the previous lift. Robert wanted the lighting adjusted so the far corner could be seen. Each suggestion was sensible, but they began arriving too quickly for anyone to answer the last one.

Michael looked at the beam, then at Andrew. “I can do another one. I don't need to do it immediately.”

Andrew laughed. “I'm not making you.”

“No,” Michael said. “You're making the next one sound as if it has to happen before we leave.”

The remark was light, but it slowed the group. Gabe asked Michael whether he wanted to rest. Michael said he did, and sat on the bench while the others moved the equipment. Nobody argued. The next attempt could wait.

Andrew looked toward the board. He had already begun to imagine the time written beside Michael's name. He had not meant to pressure him, but he could see how quickly a demonstration had become an expectation. He put the pencil down and asked Gabe whether the recovery circuit should be listed with the event. Gabe said it could be listed as preparation, not as a score.

“Then the record will say what happened,” Chase said. “It won't say what we wanted to happen next.”

That distinction gave them room to continue without pretending that the next attempt had already been agreed.

Kirk listened to the public report from the receiver. He had been told about the new board, the route variations, and Michael's arrival. The men were proud of the day, and they wanted him to hear the best parts. Andrew described the beam lift with particular enthusiasm. Robert explained the gallery. Chase listed the measurements that still needed verification. Gabe described the circuit and Joseph added the small improvement to the Cedar Span bench.

Kirk listened without interrupting. “You've made a whole institution out of one game.”

“We've improved the game,” Andrew said.

“You've improved the pavilion too,” Robert replied.

“And the bench,” Joseph said.

“And the record,” Chase added.

Gabe folded his arms. “There's still room for another version.”

Kirk laughed. “I can hear that.”

Andrew leaned toward the receiver. “You should come and see the board.”

“I'd like to.”

“Maybe you could give us a new challenge.”

“I can give you an opinion. I won't move just because you want to see what happens.”

The correction was gentle, and the men accepted it. They had learned that Kirk could be part of their world without being required to turn his body into a demonstration. His approval mattered because he was a person they cared about, not because his movement would make the next contest more spectacular.


The receiver was returned to the table. Andrew wanted to ask Kirk whether he had enjoyed the day's demonstrations, but he did not call again. He had already heard the answer in the way Kirk had spoken about the pavilion and the board. The men could show him what they had made. They could ask for his opinion. They could not make his body the next event simply because the afternoon had gone well.

Michael sat with Gabe while the others worked on the gallery. He said he had enjoyed the beam carry, but he did not want every future visit to begin with a request to lift something heavier. Gabe agreed. He told Michael that the training court could be used for ordinary sessions, demonstrations, or nothing at all. A man could arrive, spend time with the others, and leave without having to produce a new record.

Michael smiled. “That sounds like a better invitation.”

Andrew heard them and came over. He said he had been thinking about a new challenge for the next visit. Michael told him he would listen to it when he came back. He did not promise to perform it. Andrew accepted the answer and began explaining the design rather than insisting on the result. The difference was small, but the conversation remained friendly.

The exhibition continued into the afternoon. The men were learning that every attempt left behind a question for the next one, and that the work of improvement was larger than the result written on the board.


As the afternoon lengthened, the men began to tire. Andrew wanted another run at the double arch, but Gabe pointed out that his release had become less controlled. Andrew said he could correct it. Gabe asked whether he wanted to correct it now or after a rest. Andrew looked at the ball, then at the recovery bench. He chose the bench.

Robert's theatre projection showed the last attempt from two angles. The front view made the return look clean; the side view showed that the ball had touched the edge of the arch. Chase recorded it as a near-success, not a completed return. Andrew groaned, then laughed. The result was still useful. They had learned which part of the route needed adjustment, and nobody had to pretend that a near-success was a record.

Joseph brought the bench back from the Cedar Span and set it beside the pavilion. He asked whether the next exhibition could include the repair work that made a course usable. Robert liked the idea. Gabe said the recovery circuit should be included as well. Michael suggested that the men who carried the beam should also be allowed to record the method that made the lift safe. Chase wrote these proposals beneath the main entries, not as a new competition but as things worth preserving.

The board began to look different. It still held the impressive demonstrations, but it also held the work that made them possible: the adjusted marker, the resting bench, the warm-up, the recovery route, the measured attempt that had not succeeded, and the correction that made the next attempt better. The men had not become less competitive. They had begun to see more of what a good performance required.

By evening the Evermeasure Board was already crowded. Robert promised another panel. Chase promised a proper index. Gabe wanted an area reserved for recovery and techniques that were worth keeping even when they did not win. Joseph wanted the old marks preserved after each revision. Andrew said there should be a trophy. Michael suggested that the trophy ought to be too large for the shelf, which made Andrew decide that it would need its own pedestal.

They were still arguing about the pedestal when the field began to empty.


Before anyone left, Chase asked the group to agree on a few rules for the next exhibition. An attempt would be recorded with its conditions and method. A failed or incomplete attempt would remain in the archive if it taught them something. A player could stop, rest, or decline a repeat without losing the value of what he had already done. Different kinds of achievement would remain in different categories. No one would be ranked by his body, his relationship with Kirk, or the amount of attention he received.

Andrew wanted a trophy, and the others let him keep the idea. But the trophy would mark an event or a method, not the worth of the man who won it. A later achievement could surpass the record without making the earlier one meaningless. The board would preserve both.

Gabe asked that the recovery space remain outside the main exhibition route. Joseph asked that the bench and the other useful objects be displayed as craft, not as consolation prizes. Robert agreed to make the gallery flexible enough to show several kinds of work. Michael said he would come back if there was room to play without being expected to repeat the beam lift. Andrew said there would be.

The rules did not end the competition. They made it possible for the men to keep enjoying it without requiring every gathering to become a test of who deserved to remain.

Later, when the common green had quieted, Michael received a private message from Kirk. They spoke briefly, away from the public channel. Kirk asked whether he had enjoyed the day; Michael said he had, and told him he had been thinking of him. Kirk answered in the same quiet register. Their relationship remained theirs, separate from the applause.


A small note appeared at the bottom of the board before Robert closed the gallery for the night. It was not a record. It was a reminder of the rules they had just agreed to: **The game may continue; the man may stop.** Chase added the date. Gabe placed the recovery circuit beside it. Joseph left the bench's drawing in the craft section. Andrew looked at the entries and found that the board had become more interesting, not less. It contained more ways to participate, more ways to improve, and more reasons to return.

Joseph came back from the House of Returns after the others had begun to leave. He stopped beside the board and read the new note. Andrew was still there, holding the chalk.

“Do you think it will work?” Joseph asked.

“It will work if we remember it.”

“That's not the same thing.”

“No,” Andrew said. “It's harder.”

Joseph looked at the list of entries. “I might play tomorrow. I might take the short route. I might just sit with Gabe and watch.”

Andrew nodded. “That counts.”

“You don't have to say that because of the note.”

“I know. I want you there whether you play or not.”

Joseph studied him for a moment, then smiled. “That's better.”

Joseph left the pavilion with Andrew. They walked together as far as the covered path, then separated toward their own houses. The Evermeasure Board remained behind them, carrying the day's results and the rules that would make another day possible.

The Evermeasure Board stayed illuminated after everyone had gone. Its entries would become the next day's starting points: better routes, heavier beams, cleaner measurements, and new designs. No exhibition would be final. The men would keep answering one another, not by erasing the last achievement but by making another.

The Evermeasure would still invite excess. Someone would always want a harder route, a heavier beam, a faster time, or a more spectacular answer. The men had not cured themselves of wanting to surpass one another; they had begun to recognise when the contest made them careless with the people beside them. The next challenge would test whether they could keep the game generous enough to be worth returning to.

## Continuity record

- **Book:** II — the Kirk expedition, Chapter VII.
- **Governing theme:** the men are inherently competitive and enjoy showing off. One-upmanship is an enduring, recursive culture, not a phase that ends with one final champion.
- **New tradition:** the **Evermeasure**, a continuing exhibition and challenge cycle in which each achievement becomes the next baseline.
- **New feature:** the **Evermeasure Board** in the common pavilion; it preserves dated achievements, methods, prior marks, and next challenges rather than ranking men's worth or erasing earlier records.
- **Competition:** Andrew improvises games and spectacle; Chase pursues exactness; Gabe demonstrates physical technique and development; Robert builds presentation and facilities; Joseph excels through quiet precision and craft; Michael brings a very large muscular presence and participates in the public exhibition.
- **Michael Brock:** established as an adult, very muscular man with a secret, gentle relationship with Kirk. The other residents do not know of the relationship in this chapter. His residence, biography, and the reason for the secrecy remain unestablished.
- **Kirk:** enjoys the men's inventiveness but retains bodily autonomy. No one may request a movement as a performance or treat his attention as a prize.
- **Joseph and Andrew:** their existing attention conflict continues; competition in the pavilion does not turn access to Kirk into a scored contest.
- **Future rule:** wins remain meaningful but temporary. The next challenge is always possible; no final champion closes the book's competitive engine.
- **Play and recovery:** the board's governing note is **The game may continue; the man may stop.** Attempts, methods, and recoveries can be recorded, but no player is required to repeat a demonstration or remain in a contest. Different kinds of achievement stay in distinct categories; no single score ranks the men.
