# Book II — Chapter VI: The Game Between the Houses

The first full morning in the residential country began with four different kinds of noise. From Laskin Mansion came the low clatter of books being moved and the sharper sound of a theatre technician testing the new lighting rails. At the Record House, Chase had opened the archive windows and was carrying two map cases onto the terrace. Gabe's training court gave back the regular knock of equipment being set into place, while the House of Returns remained quiet except for the small sounds of a fire being prepared and a door opening onto the sheltered garden. The four houses had been occupied for only a short time, but they were already ceasing to look like four new buildings. They were becoming four different ways of living.

Beyond them the paired grounds stretched away into a scale that resisted being taken in from one position. The residents had entered Kirk's two enormous chambers as travellers, but the new district made them inhabitants. The surrounding surfaces rose in long, rounded reaches, with sheltered folds and broad slopes that changed the apparent distance between one house and the next. The covered walks made the estates accessible without forcing a man to cross the open ground every time he wanted to visit a neighbour. From the upper windows, the landscape looked almost still. From the paths, it seemed to move by degrees, as though the country were remembering a different arrangement underneath the one currently visible.

The local atlas had given the region a working name: the Twin Grounds. It was not a claim that the two chambers were identical. They were paired, but they did not share every contour or every passage. The western ground held the first approach from the residential threshold; the eastern ground had a broader sheltered hollow and a more gradual rise. Between them ran a central fold, and across the fold the architects had placed the Cedar Span, a covered timber passage with resting bays and open viewing slots. Its foundations had been checked from both sides. Its exact position remained unmeasured, but its use was already clear: it let the residents cross between the two grounds without confusing one route with the other.

Andrew Sim had been awake for some time. He had sent Kirk two messages before breakfast, then waited by the common pavilion with the baseball turning between his palms. He had a reason for wanting Kirk to come down: he wanted to show him a game that had occurred to him while he was looking at the slope beyond the eastern court. He also wanted Kirk to answer. The distinction had become harder for him to maintain. If Kirk replied quickly, Andrew felt settled for a while. If the receiver remained quiet, he found another question to ask, another small observation to report, another excuse to bring himself within reach.

He told himself that the game needed Kirk's involvement. That was partly true. The terrain changed with Kirk's movement, and any activity across the Twin Grounds had to respect the fact that the landscape belonged to a living person. But Andrew was also waiting for the particular attention that came when Kirk addressed him directly. He wanted the pause before Kirk's answer, the recognisable warmth in his voice, the sense that the next moment had been made for the two of them. The want was becoming more demanding than Andrew intended to admit.

Chase arrived with the two map cases. “You've drawn a field.”

“Not just a field,” Andrew said. “A route that turns into a field and then turns back into a route.”

“That's usually what a field is.”

“Not this one.”

Chase set the cases on the table. Andrew showed him the sketch. The proposed game began on the western ground, passed through the covered span, curved around a low rise on the eastern side, and returned by a broad sheltered passage. Two cedar markers established the starting line. A third marked the point at which a player could choose either the shorter route or the long loop. A shallow arch near the eastern hollow would return a rolling ball toward the central field. Andrew had drawn the ball's path as a succession of curves, each one measured from a different starting position.

Chase studied the sketch. “You've left out the change between the two sides.”

“I haven't. I don't know what the change is yet.”

“You've drawn them as though the surfaces are fixed.”

“They won't be. That's the point.”

“Then the map needs two layers.”

Andrew looked pleased. “That's why I wanted you.”

Chase marked the western route in one pencil and the eastern route in another. He did not assign colours to the chambers as though one were the original and the other a copy. Instead he gave each a separate line style and left the central fold unfilled. He added a small box for Kirk's reported sensations, another for direct measurements, and a third for Andrew's hypotheses. The game could proceed only when the three were distinguishable. A ball could show a slope; a survey could establish a distance; Kirk could describe a movement. None of those accounts could replace the others.

Gabe came over from the Canvas House carrying a pair of training bands and a folded towel. He listened to Andrew's explanation, then walked the first section of the proposed route. The ground was wide enough for several men to move abreast, but the turn beyond the Cedar Span would need a better resting surface. Gabe proposed a warm-up station near the western marker and a recovery bench at the eastern hollow. He did not want the course to become a test that rewarded whoever could keep going longest. It should allow a man to stop, change his route, or join the game halfway through.


He demonstrated the warm-up before anyone began. It was a short sequence of shoulder movement, wrist and hand preparation, a walk along the covered span, and a few controlled turns at the western marker. He asked the men to notice how the ground felt underfoot and whether the covered passage changed the way they carried the ball. Nobody was required to complete the whole sequence. Gabe wanted the residents to understand that preparation was part of the game, not a penalty before it.

The recovery bench was placed where a man could see the eastern hollow without standing in the middle of the route. Gabe checked that there was enough room to sit and rise without blocking the return line. He also marked an alternate path that avoided the hollow entirely. Andrew wanted to make the long loop the main event, but Gabe insisted that the shorter route remain equally valid. A man who chose the shorter line would still be playing the Long Return.

“It's a game,” Andrew said.

“Then it should be possible to enjoy it,” Gabe replied. “Not everyone has to prove something every time.”

Andrew considered that, then drew the alternate path more clearly on the map. He was not entirely convinced, but he could see that the course would be better if more than one kind of player could use it.

Robert arrived last. He had spent the morning in his library, where the second staircase had already proved useful. He had brought a small box of brass fittings from the workshop beneath Laskin Mansion and wanted to know whether the viewing arch could be fitted with a sliding screen. The arch should frame the game when the residents wanted to watch, but it should also be possible to close the view and use the room for something else. The group agreed. Robert asked whether the game might later be shown in the miniature theatre, with a map projected beside the image. Andrew immediately began inventing a commentary for it, and Chase told him not to narrate a route that had not yet been walked.


Robert went back to Laskin Mansion and returned with a portable frame for the viewing arch. It held a narrow screen that could be slid across the opening when the gallery was not in use. He fixed a small lamp above it and asked Andrew to roll the ball once while he watched from the side. The screen showed the ball's movement, but the arch concealed the far corner of the eastern hollow. Robert shifted the lamp and added a second viewing angle. The two views made the route clearer without pretending that one could replace the other.

Chase asked him to label the second view as a reconstruction if it was later projected in the theatre. Robert agreed. The residents would need to know whether they were looking at the actual course, a measured drawing, or a model of a route that had not yet been checked. Andrew wanted the presentation to be dramatic. Robert wanted it to be honest. They decided that both aims could be served if the labels remained visible.

The viewing arch was left open for the first game. The screen could be used later, when the residents had enough attempts to make a comparison worth showing.

Joseph approached from the House of Returns. He had heard the talk from the garden path and stopped beside the table. “What's the game?”

Andrew showed him the sketch. Joseph followed the route with one finger, then looked toward the broad eastern slope. “It would be easier if the return came through the lower passage.”

“That passage hasn't been surveyed,” Chase said.

“Then it can be the second version.”

Andrew looked at Joseph and smiled. “You can be on my team.”

Joseph smiled back, but his attention remained on the map. “I haven't agreed to play.”

“That's why I'm inviting you.”

“Then let me decide after I've seen the course.”

Kirk's receiver sounded before Andrew could answer. The group fell quiet. Andrew reached for it first.

“Morning,” Kirk said.

“Morning. We have a game.”

“I've heard.”

Andrew looked up. “You've heard already?”

“Chase sent the map through. He included the separate layers.”

Chase raised one hand in acknowledgement. Kirk continued. He liked the idea of a route that made use of both grounds, but he wanted to know where the residents planned to stop if the terrain shifted. He also asked whether they intended to use the eastern hollow. The residents said yes, but only after a fresh check. Kirk told them that he had noticed a small change in the area during the night. It had not been painful, and he did not think it would affect the main path, but the lower edge of the hollow might have lifted.

Andrew leaned closer to the receiver. “Can you move it again while we're watching? Just a little. We could see how the route changes.”

Kirk did not answer immediately. “I can tell you when I move. I don't want to be asked to repeat a bodily movement as though I'm operating a piece of equipment.”

Andrew's expression changed. He knew the correction was fair, but he had hoped Kirk would enjoy the idea. “I wasn't asking you to do it for the game.”

“You were asking me to do it while you watched the game.”

There was no accusation in the words. That made them more difficult to evade. Andrew looked down at the sketch. “I see the difference.”

“Good. I'll let you know when I next change position. Until then, use the ground that's been checked.”

The receiver clicked softly. Andrew set it back on the table. He had not been refused the game, or Kirk's company, but he had been reminded that Kirk was not obliged to turn every movement into a demonstration. For a moment Andrew felt the old urge to call back with another explanation. He resisted it and picked up the ball instead.

The first walk of the course began after the residents had checked the Cedar Span and marked the eastern hollow. The route looked shorter from the pavilion than it did from ground level. The covered passage carried them across the central fold, and the view through its open slots changed as they moved. On the western side, the buildings appeared close together. At the middle of the span, they seemed to stand on separate shores. From the eastern opening, the four mansions became a small cluster behind the broad rise, and the landscape beyond them opened into a long amber distance.


The first inspection was done without a ball. Chase walked the covered span and measured the distance between the markers. Robert stood at the viewing arch and asked each man to stop at the point where the western ground disappeared behind the central fold. He wanted to know which parts of the route could be seen from the gallery and which would have to be shown through a second view. The group agreed that the arch should not be used to judge a player's performance. It was a way to observe the course, not a place from which to rank the men.

Gabe tested the recovery route and returned along the covered path. Joseph walked the lower edge of the eastern hollow, checking the surface where the ball had turned in Andrew's sketch. He suggested a small marker at the place where the two routes separated. Chase added it to the map as a temporary marker, not a permanent landmark. The residents had learned from the changing lower country that a mark should identify the latest checked state without pretending to fix the terrain forever.

Only after the walk did Andrew bring out the ball. The game was ready to begin, but the route had been examined as a place people would use, not merely as a line on a drawing.

Andrew started the game with a short roll. The ball passed beneath the first arch, curved toward the eastern hollow, and came to rest near Gabe's proposed recovery bench. Gabe walked it back. The second roll travelled farther. The third struck the shallow wall at the edge of the hollow and returned through the arch as Andrew had predicted. Chase recorded each start point and the final resting place. Robert watched from the viewing room and sent a message asking for the angle of the return to be added to the projection.

Joseph took his turn. He chose the lower line rather than the long loop, then stopped at the covered bay to look back across the span. “You can see the two grounds from here,” he said. “But you can't see the same thing from either side.”

“That's why I drew two routes,” Andrew replied.

“You drew them because you wanted a game.”

“That's also true.”

Joseph rolled the ball. It followed the low line, turned beneath the arch, and came to rest near the western marker. Andrew clapped once and called it a return. The others laughed. Joseph took the ball back and handed it to him. The game had begun to feel like a shared activity rather than Andrew's private attempt to bring Kirk into the afternoon.


Gabe took a turn next. He chose the longer loop but stopped at the recovery bench before returning to the western marker. He wanted to see whether the ball could be carried without losing the rhythm of the route. Robert tried a slower roll from the upper edge, and Chase repeated Andrew's first line from the same starting mark. The results were different, but the differences were useful. One man favoured a clean return; another wanted to discover how the ball behaved near the hollow; a third was more interested in whether the route could be measured consistently.

Andrew began to call the attempts by their order. Chase corrected him when he described a later run as a record. It was only another attempt. The men agreed that they would not name a best route until they had repeated the same course under the same conditions. For now, the purpose was to learn the grounds and enjoy the game.

Joseph chose the shorter path once more, then sat at the recovery bench and watched the others. He was not obliged to take another turn. Andrew asked if he wanted to try the long loop, and Joseph said he might later. Andrew accepted the answer and returned to the starting line. The moment passed without becoming a negotiation.

Then the receiver sounded again.

Andrew turned before anyone else moved. Kirk was calling to ask whether the eastern hollow had been checked after the second roll. Chase answered first, giving the measured readings. Gabe described the surface underfoot. Joseph added that the lower line was clear but that the resting bay needed a second handrail. Kirk listened to all three accounts and thanked them. He then asked Andrew what the ball had shown.

Andrew answered quickly. He described the three paths, the return beneath the arch, and the difference between the western and eastern slopes. Kirk asked one more question, and Andrew felt the familiar lift of being singled out. He began explaining an additional variation he had been saving for Kirk.

Joseph stepped away from the group.

It was not a dramatic departure. He put his hands in his pockets and walked back along the Cedar Span toward the House of Returns. Gabe watched him go, then looked at Andrew. Chase continued writing, but his pencil stopped at the edge of the page. Andrew saw both reactions. He also knew that Joseph had not been required to leave. The decision had been made because the conversation had once again become something Andrew wanted to keep for himself.

Kirk noticed the silence. “Has Joseph gone?”

Andrew looked toward the span. “He's heading home.”

“Was he finished?”

“I don't know.”

Kirk waited. Andrew could have said that Joseph was tired, or that he had a private appointment, or that the game had simply moved on. Instead he said, “I kept talking after he'd already given you his part of the report.”

“That isn't the same as asking him to leave.”

“No. But it makes it harder for him to stay.”

The receiver remained open. Kirk did not offer a quick absolution. He asked Andrew whether he wanted to tell Joseph that he had noticed. Andrew said yes, but he did not want to make another interruption. Kirk suggested that Andrew wait until the game had been put away, then ask whether Joseph wanted to speak. Andrew agreed.

The afternoon continued. Gabe helped adjust the recovery bench. Robert tested the viewing screen and invited the others to see how the game looked from the library gallery. Chase prepared a route sheet that showed the first three ball paths beside the measurements. The long loop remained open as a proposed variation, not a confirmed course. The men played two more rounds, changing teams and taking turns at the starting line. Andrew was still competitive, but he began to watch the other players rather than keeping one ear fixed on the receiver.


The second round was more relaxed. The residents began to call out small observations rather than instructions. A ball that caught the edge of the hollow was described as a useful return, not a mistake. A man who chose the short route was allowed to finish without being asked why he had not taken the longer one. Gabe moved the recovery bench a little farther from the marker so that the route remained clear. Robert tested the screen from the library gallery and found that the side view made the turn easier to understand. Chase wrote the adjustment beside the map, leaving the first position visible.

Andrew still wanted to win. He liked the feeling of a clean run and the prospect of being the man who found the best line. But he began to notice that the others were enjoying the course in ways that did not require him to lead every attempt. He laughed when Robert's ball took an unexpected turn. He asked Gabe to show him how he had controlled the longer loop. He let Joseph keep the ball after the lower route, rather than asking for it back immediately.

The receiver remained quiet for a while. Andrew glanced toward it once, then returned to the game. The absence of a message did not mean that the day had stopped. The residents had work to do, and the course was becoming interesting on its own terms.

When they returned to the pavilion, Joseph had not come back. Andrew carried the ball to the House of Returns and stopped at the outer path. The new bench was not there yet. He stood a little way from the door, unsure whether to knock. He could hear a quiet movement inside, perhaps a chair being moved or a book placed on a table. The house had a life of its own. He could not assume that because he wanted to speak, Joseph was ready.

He knocked once.

Joseph opened the door after a moment. “Hello.”

“Can I talk to you?”

“Yes.”

Andrew held the ball against his side. “I noticed you left when I got Kirk on the receiver. I wanted to keep the conversation going, and I didn't leave room for you to finish being there.”

Joseph looked at him for a moment. “You didn't tell me to go.”

“I know. But you shouldn't have to wait until I remember you're part of the conversation.”

Joseph stepped back from the doorway, though he did not yet invite Andrew inside. “I don't want to compete with you for the right to speak to him.”

“You shouldn't have to.”

“I also don't want you to pretend you don't want his attention. You do. I can see it.”

Andrew nodded. “I want it a lot.”

“Then ask for it. But don't make every room into a place where I have to move aside.”

Andrew's first instinct was to promise that he would stop. The words came close, but he recognised that he could not honestly promise to stop wanting what he wanted. He could promise something about his conduct. “I'll ask him for time instead of taking it whenever I can. And I'll let him finish what he's doing with you.”

Joseph considered this. “That's a beginning.”

Andrew held out the ball. “You can keep this until tomorrow, if you want. I think I owe you the next serve.”

Joseph took it, then smiled faintly. “You don't have to pay me back with a ball.”

“I know. It's just a ball.”

“Then come and have tea.”

That evening Kirk came to the House of Returns as promised. He and Joseph sat by the hearth. Andrew did not call; he had been given a time to see Kirk the next morning, and he chose to keep it. His longing remained, but it now had a boundary he could practise respecting.

Before they separated, Chase added a rule to the route sheet: a player could stop at any marker, take the shorter path, or leave the course without losing the value of the attempt. The Long Return would record the route, not the worth of the man who took it. Andrew read the note and accepted it.

Joseph returned the ball to the common table. Andrew thanked him and asked whether he wanted to play again the next day. Joseph said he would decide after breakfast. Andrew let the answer remain open. The residents left the course assembled and quiet, with its markers visible and recovery bench in place. The game had a route, but no winner and no final form.

## Continuity record

- **Book:** II — the Kirk expedition, Chapter VI.
- **New local region:** The Twin Grounds, the paired residential terrain in Kirk's two chambers. They are related but not assumed to be identical.
- **New named features:** the Cedar Span, the Long Return course, the western and eastern markers, the eastern hollow, and the viewing arch. Their exact positions remain unmeasured.
- **Residents:** Robert, Chase, Gabe, and Joseph continue living independently in their four mansions. Their routines and interests develop while the narrative is elsewhere.
- **Giant play:** The Long Return becomes a shared route game using rolling-ball paths, the covered span, the hollow, and changing terrain. It is cooperative and revisable rather than a contest for dominance. Players may stop, change to the shorter route, or leave without losing the value of an attempt. The first route has no winner or final form; its route sheet records what was tried rather than ranking the men.
- **Kirk:** He is consulted about movements that could affect the residents, but he is not asked to perform bodily movements on command. His sensations and choices remain his own.
- **Andrew Sim:** His need for Kirk's attention is more visible. He interrupts less successfully, notices Joseph's withdrawal, and begins to ask for time rather than monopolizing shared conversations. The underlying need remains unresolved.
- **Joseph:** He names the effect of Andrew's behaviour without turning it into a contest. His relationship with Kirk remains an active, chosen continuity.
- **Chase:** Separates direct measurements, Kirk's reports, and Andrew's hypotheses in the route record.
- **Gabe:** Ensures the game has warm-up, recovery, and alternate routes rather than rewarding only endurance.
- **Robert:** Develops the viewing arch and miniature-theatre projection as part of the district's recreational life.
- **Atlas instruction:** Add the Twin Grounds and named game features as unmeasured local landmarks. Do not add them to the Veyrthalis map or Kirk's separate mental-house map.
- **Next movement:** The Long Return is ready for a second variation, but the eastern hollow and lower approach remain subject to a fresh survey after Kirk's next reported movement.
