# Book II — Chapter V: Four Mansions

The invitation arrived at the Measure House on a folded sheet of heavy paper. Kirk had dictated it through the receiver, and the expedition leader had written it down without shortening the terms. He wanted the men who had been travelling through his country to have somewhere permanent to stay, if they wished. The new residential ground lay beyond the lower route, in a region that had begun to open within the paired chambers of his anatomy. The ground had been examined from the safe approach; it was broad, sheltered, and capable of supporting several separate estates. Kirk had not asked the expedition to settle there. He had offered the possibility, and each man would decide for himself.

The first name on the sheet was Robert Laskin.

Robert had spoken of a home before the others did. The earlier Laskin Residency had begun as an eccentric but carefully considered proposal: a private settlement with proper lighting, a library, a little recreation hall, and a view across the enormous country. He had not wanted to occupy a corner of someone else's house as a novelty. He wanted a home with rooms that could become familiar, a place where he could read without being interrupted and receive visitors without having to explain why he lived there. The new plan gave that proposal its full scale. His mansion would have a broad entry court, a high library with two levels of galleries, a miniature theatre, a recreation hall, gardens sheltered from the main passage, and a viewing room whose long windows looked toward the distant folds of the surrounding landscape. Robert read the plan twice, then asked for a second staircase and a smaller room where he could keep his books close at hand. The architect added both.

Chase chose a different kind of house. He wanted somewhere quiet enough to hear himself think, but not so isolated that the world could be reduced to whatever he happened to be looking at. The Record House was built around an archive gallery and a pair of studies. One study held the first account of an event; the other held later revisions. Neither could be closed merely because the other had become more convenient. A map room opened onto a terrace, and a modest observatory stood above the eastern wing. Chase asked for cabinets that could be moved without damaging the walls and a long table wide enough for several men to lay out separate versions of a route. The house suited him because it did not demand that he be a narrator at every hour. He could keep the record, then close the notebook and spend an afternoon repairing a chair, walking the grounds, or sitting with a friend who did not want to discuss what had happened.

Gabe spent longer with his plan. The Canvas House needed room for the work that had shaped his life, but he did not want every room to become a display of transformation. He asked for a training court with adjustable equipment, a large open studio, and a gallery where earlier baselines could be seen without being placed in order of worth. A greenhouse and a long garden walk were added after he noticed that the original plan offered too many places in which people might watch him and too few places where he could be alone. There would be a private recovery room, a quiet dining room, and a garden court in which a person could sit without being asked what came next. Gabe approved the revised plan. He liked the idea that his house would allow him to grow, but would not require it.

Joseph's house was the last to be placed. He did not ask for the largest estate or the most prominent view. He wanted a central hearth, a library, comfortable guest rooms, and several sitting rooms with different degrees of privacy. One of the architects suggested a grand hall that would make the house visible from the shared green. Joseph declined. He asked for a sheltered walk that reached the common grounds, but he wanted the front door to be somewhere a visitor could approach without appearing before the whole district. The House of Returns took shape around that request. Its rooms were generous but not ceremonial. A broad timber stair rose beside the hearth; a small gallery held objects that had accumulated meaning over time; and a quiet room at the back opened onto a garden protected from the main route. Joseph wanted a place where a person could come back without being required to make a speech.

The four mansions were not arranged as a row of competing monuments. They stood around a shared green, with covered walks connecting their outer courts. The green contained a long table, a fountain, several mature trees, a playing field, and a small pavilion where residents could gather when nobody wanted to enter another man's home. The architects built a common kitchen and a recreation hall at a respectful distance from the private gardens. The paths were wide, the thresholds were easy to read, and every estate had a return route that remained open even if the surrounding ground changed. The measurements were entered as provisional. Kirk had given the district permission to exist, but the country still moved, and the builders would not pretend that their first plan had become permanent truth.

When the four residents arrived, Robert went first to the library. He checked the height of the lower shelves, tested the reading lamps, and asked whether the theatre could be made dark enough for a small private screening. Chase went directly to the archive gallery and set the first map beside its revision. He left a space between them for the next account. Gabe walked through the training court and then spent more time in the garden than in the studio. Joseph stood at the hearth while the others came and went. He did not need to be the first person to occupy every room. He wanted to know that the house could hold an ordinary evening.

Andrew Sim arrived before the welcoming meal had been served. He had come down from the Measure House with his field bag and the baseball he had used in the earlier survey. He asked where Kirk was. The architect explained that Kirk had been resting and would send word when he was ready to speak. Andrew nodded, but he remained near the entrance. When the receiver sounded, he crossed the hall before anyone else could answer.

“You've built a whole neighbourhood,” Kirk said.

“Four houses,” Andrew replied. “That's practically a town.”

“Are they happy with them?”

“They haven't stopped inspecting things. Robert's in the library. Chase is probably measuring a cabinet. Gabe went outside. Joseph—”

Andrew stopped. Kirk waited.

“Joseph is in his house,” Andrew finished.

“I know where Joseph is.”

There was no sharpness in Kirk's voice, but Andrew heard the distinction. He looked toward the covered walk that led to the House of Returns. He had already been at the receiver twice that morning, first to tell Kirk about the new approach and then to ask whether the district would have a common playing field. Both conversations had been pleasant. Both had ended when Kirk said he needed to rest. Andrew had come back anyway, telling himself that the next question was important enough to justify it.

“I wanted to see you,” Andrew said.

“I know. We can talk. I also promised Joseph that I'd visit his house tonight.”

Andrew's expression changed. “Tonight?”

“Yes.”

“I thought we'd look at the new route.”

“We can look at it tomorrow.”

“It's only a short walk.”

“Andrew.”

The name was enough to make him pause. Kirk did not sound angry. He sounded present, and Andrew had to listen to the words rather than the possibility of another invitation hidden inside them.

“I want time with you,” Andrew said. “I don't like arriving and finding out you've already made plans.”

“You're allowed to want time with me. You're not entitled to every part of it.”

Andrew looked down at the ball in his hand. He was used to beginning things, making a joke, creating a reason for everyone to move. This was not a problem he could solve by inventing another activity. Kirk's plans with Joseph were real. The promise had already been made. Andrew could dislike it without having the right to erase it.

“I know,” he said, though he did not yet know what to do with the feeling.

Kirk asked him to stay for the meal. Andrew agreed. He sat at the long table with Robert, Chase, and Gabe, and tried to join the conversation. Robert described the library's second staircase. Chase explained why the two map studies needed separate lighting. Gabe spoke about the garden's quiet corner and the relief of having a place where nobody expected a new baseline from him. Andrew listened, but his attention kept returning to the receiver. When it remained silent, he found himself inventing reasons to check whether Kirk had sent another message.

Joseph arrived later. He had taken a walk through his new garden and returned with a leaf caught in the cuff of his sleeve. Andrew noticed him, then looked back toward the receiver. The movement was small, but Chase saw it. He said nothing. Gabe noticed too, though he waited until the meal had ended before asking Joseph whether he liked the house.

“I do,” Joseph said. “It has somewhere to go when I don't want to be in the middle of things.”

“That's useful.”

“It is.”

Joseph glanced at Andrew. He did not accuse him of anything. He had already noticed the repeated calls, the extra visits, the way Andrew could turn a casual question into a reason to keep Kirk on the line. He also understood that Andrew's need was not entirely a performance. Andrew wanted something from Kirk that he did not know how to ask for without making it urgent. Joseph could recognise the feeling and still be hurt by what it was doing to the space between them.

Kirk arrived at the House of Returns after the meal. The route had been checked, the return path was clear, and the visit had been arranged with Joseph beforehand. They sat beside the hearth and spoke for a while about the new district. Joseph showed him the gallery, the sheltered walk, and the garden at the back. Kirk asked what he would change. Joseph said that he might want a bench near the outer path, somewhere a visitor could stop before deciding whether to come in. Kirk said they could add one.

They did not spend the whole evening talking about Andrew. They spoke about the earlier promises they had made, about the things that had changed, and about the difference between having a place and being able to return to it. Joseph admitted that Andrew's sudden abundance of attention had made him feel less able to approach Kirk without interrupting. He did not ask Kirk to send Andrew away. He asked whether the time they had promised to one another could remain real even when someone else wanted it.

Kirk answered that it could. He would not make Joseph compete for a promise already given, and he would not ask Andrew to pretend that he did not want closeness. The work would be to speak plainly about time, ask before interrupting, and accept that a person's affection for one man did not cancel the life he shared with another. Joseph said that was what he wanted: not a victory, but a relationship that could still be lived without continually defending its place.

The next morning Andrew waited at the common pavilion. He had brought the baseball and a new map, but he did not immediately call Kirk. He watched the four houses wake in their separate ways. Robert opened the library windows. Chase carried two versions of the same map to the archive terrace. Gabe crossed the garden with a cup of coffee and left the training court empty. Joseph came out of the House of Returns and walked toward the long table.

Andrew wanted Kirk to appear. The feeling was strong enough to make him restless. He could have sent another message. Instead he stayed where he was until Joseph reached the pavilion.

“Is Kirk coming?” Andrew asked.

“Later,” Joseph said. “He said he'd meet you after the survey.”

Andrew nodded. “I know. I just wanted to check.”

Joseph looked at the ball, then at Andrew. “You don't have to pretend you don't want him around.”

“I don't.”

“But I need you to understand that I live here too. I'm not an obstacle between you and Kirk.”

Andrew looked away. He had a quick answer ready, something funny and defensive, but none of it would improve the truth. “I know you're not.”

“Then let me have the time he promised me.”

Andrew rolled the ball once between his palms. “All right.”

It was not the end of his need for Kirk's attention. It was not even the end of the day in which he would feel it sharply. But it was the first time he had allowed the need to remain a feeling rather than turning it immediately into another request. Joseph did not thank him as though Andrew had performed a favour. He simply nodded and went to the table. That made the exchange more useful than praise would have.

When Kirk arrived later, Andrew greeted him with the familiar grin. The chemistry was still there. It had not been destroyed by a boundary, nor did it entitle Andrew to ignore one. Kirk took the map, asked about the new route, and listened while Andrew explained the measurements. Then he asked whether Andrew wanted to walk the playing field with him. Andrew said yes.

Across the green, the four mansions had begun to acquire their own rhythms. Robert's library was already receiving its first visitors. Chase had added a shelf for maps that remained disputed. Gabe had moved a chair into the garden and left the studio doors closed. Joseph was preparing the House of Returns for an evening meal. The district was not a monument to Kirk's generosity or a theatre for Andrew's jealousy. It was becoming a place where four men could live, and where their separate lives would continue to alter the relationships around them.

The country beneath them had not stopped moving. The maps would need another revision. The mansions would need adjustments, and the paths would need to be checked after each significant shift. But the houses had been built with the understanding that a home is not made permanent by pretending its surroundings will never change. It is made durable by keeping room for repair, return, and the next conversation.
