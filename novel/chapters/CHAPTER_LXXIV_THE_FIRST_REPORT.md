# Chapter LXXIV — The First Report

Morning came through the eastern windows in a long, pale band, finding the table before it found the men. Joseph and Kirk had remained in the room until the lamps were nearly empty, and when they finally went upstairs they did not make a ceremony of leaving the chairs where they were. One stood close to the other, angled slightly inward. The distance between them had not vanished, but it no longer looked like an accusation. The House kept the arrangement without marking it as a conclusion.

Luke was already in the kitchen when they came down. He had opened the back door to let the cold air clear the smell of coffee and warm bread, and the table was crowded with plates, a bowl of apples, and a small notebook in which he had written the day's possibilities. Tyler had placed a screwdriver beside the sugar bowl. Nobody knew why until the first man entered with a damp strip of paper in his hand.

He did not come down the stairs. He was simply present in the doorway, wearing the same dark coat and carrying himself with the quiet balance Luke remembered from the corridor. His face looked less remote in daylight, though it was still difficult to decide whether he was tired or had merely stopped performing the expression the House expected of him.

"There's a report from the north arcade," he said.

Luke glanced at the paper. "A report?"

"A practical one. The rain has filled the lower channel. Water is beginning to pass beneath the eastern threshold."

Tyler reached for the screwdriver, then reconsidered and put it down. "How long?"

"Since before dawn. The water is not deep, but the stone has begun to loosen."

"Why bring it here?" Kirk asked.

"Because the House has a passage there, and because the people who use it have asked whether anyone can help."

The first man set the paper on the table. It was not a command, nor a message written in the House's strange hand. It was an ordinary maintenance note, folded once and marked with a location, a measured depth, and the name of the caretaker who had found the problem. A little water stain had spread across one corner. Joseph read it twice.

"We can go look," he said. "Looking doesn't mean promising a repair before we know what it needs."

The first man nodded. "That distinction is why I brought it."

Seth and Nick were already audible in the room, their voices carried from the open doorway and the kitchen window as though the day had begun before the men had fully noticed it.

SETH: There's the first thing the House has asked them to do that isn't about the House itself. A drain, a threshold, a bit of water getting where it shouldn't. That's a much better test than another mysterious door.

NICK: And the report has a name on it. Somebody found the water, measured it, and asked for help. The House hasn't invented a person to justify the work. The person is already there.

Luke folded the report and put it in his notebook. "All right. Production is a site visit. Tyler, bring the measuring kit. Kirk, see whether the service cart is free. Joseph, take the notebook and make sure we record what the caretaker actually asks for. No one starts breaking stone because it looks loose."

Tyler smiled. "You have learned something."

"I learned that you break things when nobody is watching."

"I repair them."

"Afterward."

They ate before they left. The food was ordinary and plentiful: eggs, toast, sliced pears, and a pan of potatoes left over from the previous evening. Kirk poured coffee for Joseph without asking how he took it. Joseph accepted the cup and looked at him for a moment, then moved his chair a little closer. It was not an apology, and Kirk did not treat it as one. They had agreed that choosing again would be a practice rather than a single event. The morning gave them a small place to begin.

The north arcade lay beyond the House's familiar grounds, past the low bridge and the road that curved behind the old library. Rainwater still ran along the gutters. The stones were dark, and the air had the mineral smell of wet masonry and leaves pressed against the pavement. A caretaker named Neral waited beneath the covered entrance with his sleeves rolled up. He was an adult man with a narrow face, strong hands, and a habit of looking at the ground while he spoke, as though he trusted surfaces more than expressions.

He showed them the channel. The water had found a break where an older repair met the original stone. It had begun to seep beneath the threshold, lifting a line of fine grit and leaving a darker mark along the lower wall. The damage was not dramatic, but it was clear. Tyler measured the opening and checked the fall of the channel. Kirk walked the route the water had taken and found a second obstruction beneath a grate. Joseph spoke with Neral about when the leak had first appeared and whether anyone had tried to clear it.

Nobody rushed.

The first man stood near the entrance, watching the men work. Luke noticed that he did not move toward the channel, and he did not ask for a tool. His attention stayed on Neral whenever the caretaker spoke. The House might have sent him as a messenger, but he was not behaving like an owner of the problem.

"We've got two issues," Tyler said after a while. "The grate is blocked, and the channel's been patched with something too rigid. The stone moves a little with the temperature. The patch doesn't."

"Can you fix it?" Neral asked.

"We can clear the grate today. The joint needs a proper repair, but we should take a sample and check what the original mortar is doing. I won't guess at the mix."

Neral seemed relieved by the answer. He had expected either a grand promise or a refusal. Instead he received a plan with a boundary.

Kirk found the grate's lifting points and brought the service cart around. Luke and Tyler cleared the leaves and compacted grit while Joseph recorded the depth of the channel and the places where the water had reached the wall. The first man held the cover steady when Neral asked him to, and for a short time his dark coat was wet at the cuffs. The task required no revelation. It required four people to lift something safely and another to keep the record.

When the obstruction came free, water moved through the channel with a low, steady sound. The level dropped. Tyler checked the fall again and asked Neral to watch the threshold during the next rain. They agreed to return after a sample had been examined. The work was not complete, but the passage could be used again.

On the walk back, Luke noticed a narrow strip of pale stone set into the wall beside the arcade. It was not a plaque. It had no inscription. Someone had fitted it there to replace a piece that had broken years before, and the new stone had a slightly different grain. Its edges were clean, but the older blocks around it had worn smooth.

"That repair is newer," Joseph said.

"Yes," the first man answered.

"Do you know who did it?"

"No."

"Doesn't that bother you?"

The first man considered the question. "It used to. I thought a repair needed a name in order to remain part of the history. Now I think the stone is allowed to keep the hand that fitted it without keeping the hand's identity."

Tyler looked back at the channel. "That's a very convenient philosophy for someone who won't give us his name."

The first man did not become angry. "It may be convenient. It may also be true."

Kirk stopped beside the low wall. "Those aren't opposites."

For the rest of the walk, nobody tried to settle the matter. The first man continued with them, not in front and not behind. At the bridge, Joseph and Kirk walked side by side. Their shoulders touched once when the road narrowed, and neither man moved away.

Back at the House, Luke opened his notebook and wrote the report's outcome beneath the original request. The channel had been cleared. The obstruction had been removed. The stone joint remained under observation. A material sample would be examined before a permanent repair was proposed. Neral had been given the date of the next visit. The record did not say that the House had saved the arcade or that the men had solved its problem. It said what had happened.

Tyler read the note and approved it. "That's accurate."

"High praise," Luke said.

"Don't get used to it."

The first man stood near the table. The morning light had moved farther across the floor, and the arrangement of the chairs from the night before remained visible in the adjoining room. Joseph looked at them, then at Kirk. He did not move either chair.

"Is that what the House wanted?" Luke asked.

The first man turned toward the window. "I don't know what the House wants. I know what the people at the arcade asked for."

Seth laughed softly from somewhere above the staircase.

SETH: That's the report, then. Not a prophecy. Not a legend. Somebody needs a channel cleared, and the men have gone to see what can be done.

NICK: The important thing is that the next chapter already has somewhere to go. They'll return with the mortar sample. They'll see whether the repair holds. And the House will have learned that a request can arrive from outside its walls without becoming an order.

That evening, Joseph and Kirk sat together at the table. They spoke about what they had misunderstood, and about the difference between giving a man space and leaving him to interpret silence. They did not settle everything. They agreed on one practical thing: when either of them needed to be alone, he would say so, and the other would not be required to guess.

The two chairs remained where they were. The first report lay beside Luke's notebook. Outside, water continued to pass through the north channel, carrying a little grit toward the river.

The House had not become a person. It had become a place that could receive a request, preserve the person who made it, and wait for the answer to arrive through work.
