# Chapter LXXIX — The Giant on the Western Road

The rain had cleared shortly before breakfast, leaving the roofs of the Scale Commons dark and reflective and the long western road bright where water had settled into its shallow joints. The six men were in the outer court with the repaired table from the previous morning. They had brought it outside to check the new brace in open air, where the paving was level and the light made it easier to see whether the frame had settled. They were all large men by the standards of the older districts, and none of them had remained at the same measurements for long. Luke's shoulders stood well above the upper rail; Andrew could carry the long end of the table without changing his grip; Tyler's notebook sat lower against his chest than it had a week before; Chase and Joseph had outgrown the first work jackets kept in the House; and Gabe, who had been pleased with his new breadth at breakfast, could feel the seams of his shirt drawing tighter whenever he reached across the tabletop. Their bodies had become familiar to one another through years of meals, repairs, practice, teasing, and the repeated business of learning how to occupy a room together. The table was no longer the largest thing they had to accommodate, but it remained the thing they were responsible for.

Andrew was checking the brace with one hand while trying to balance a mug on the far corner. Tyler had warned him not to put weight on that side until the final inspection was complete. Andrew had taken the warning as an invitation to invent a test involving coffee. Joseph had moved the mug before it could spill, and Chase had quietly placed the old fastener in its labelled tray. Luke stood at the other end with his palm against the oak, feeling for a tremor when Andrew leaned into the frame. Gabe watched the whole arrangement with a smile. It was an ordinary morning among friends, and the comfort of it had begun to feel like a permanent feature of the Commons. Then the tower's shadow crossed the paving.

It arrived before the man did, moving over the courtyard wall in a slow, broad band that blotted out the wet stone and climbed the side of the old bell tower. The men looked up. On the western road, a giant was approaching the upper slope, his head already above the tower roof and his shoulders spreading across the pale sky. His body was still changing. The movement was gradual but unmistakable against the fixed lines of the road: his shoulders rose another fraction, his chest deepened beneath a dark travelling shirt, and his stride lengthened so that the space between the painted route markers seemed to diminish beneath his feet. The men could hear the delayed impact of each step through the stone before the sound reached the courtyard. He was not rushing. He followed the broad pale bands painted along the road, placing each foot inside the marked lane and pausing whenever the signal flags changed.

**SETH:**

Look at the tower. We have a man whose shoulders are making the architecture reconsider its career.

**NICK:**

And he is waiting for the flag. It is a very large entrance, but at least somebody read the instructions. The giant stopped before the narrow bridge. He lowered himself slightly to inspect the support wall, then waited for the signal keeper to raise a green flag. Even at that distance the men's eyes were drawn toward him. He had a broad, powerful frame, with a close-trimmed dark beard and light hair pushed back from his forehead by the damp wind. His face was calm and intent. A dark blue travelling shirt fitted closely across his shoulders and upper back; the sleeves had already begun to pull toward the elbows as his arms increased in girth. His trousers were reinforced at the knees, and his boots rested carefully on the road as though he were aware of every load he placed upon it. He looked like a man who had travelled a long way and intended to finish the journey without making someone else's work harder.

Gabe's fingers closed around the edge of the table.

"He sickens me."

Andrew turned first. Tyler looked up from the brace. Chase did not reach for his notebook. Joseph watched Gabe rather than the road, and Luke, who had known him long enough to hear the difference between anger and a request to be contradicted, did not answer immediately.

**LUKE:** “What part?”

Gabe gave a short laugh. “You heard me.”

**LUKE:** “I heard the words.”

**GABE:** “Then you heard enough.”

Luke moved around the table, but stopped at a distance that left Gabe free to remain where he was. Luke's instinct was to close the space when a friend went quiet. He had learned, slowly and sometimes awkwardly, that a person could need him nearby without needing him to take charge. Gabe noticed the pause. His hand loosened on the tabletop.

**GABE:** “Everyone turns. Every time he moves, everyone turns. The whole court. The men at the bridge. The people up in the windows. They talk as though he's the only thing that's ever happened.”

**ANDREW:** “He is the largest thing happening at the moment. That doesn't mean he's the only thing.”

**GABE:** “That's not helping.”

**ANDREW:** “I wasn't trying to help. I was making a distinction.”

Tyler closed his notebook. “A useful one. We have one confirmed observation: a man is approaching the western crossing, and the bridge is under inspection. We do not know that the whole city is changing. We do not know that every district has a giant of his scale. We do not know that the growth we can see here is occurring everywhere.” Gabe looked at him. “You make it sound like a report.”

“It is a report.”

“That's the problem. You can write anything down and still not say what it feels like.”

Tyler accepted that without becoming defensive. He looked once toward the road, then back at Gabe. “You're right. I can record the change and miss what it is doing to you. But the feeling doesn't make the rest of the claims true.” Chase had remained beside the table. He watched the space between Gabe and Luke, the familiar interval that appeared whenever one of them wanted the other to understand something without being asked directly. He knew Gabe liked to be noticed, but disliked the moment when attention began to feel like an obligation. He knew Luke could make a room warmer simply by bringing a chair nearer, and that the same gesture could feel like pressure if he did it before asking. He also knew that his own habit of recording the scene could turn people into evidence when they needed him to be a friend.

**CHASE:** “Do you want me to write down what you said?”

Gabe looked at him sharply. “No.”

“All right.”

“Don't make it the thing I said about him. Don't make it the sentence everyone remembers.”

“I won't.”

“How do I know?”

“Because I can remember more than one thing about you.”

Chase did not say it dramatically. He put the notebook back into his coat and stayed. Gabe looked at him for a moment, then away. That was enough for now. The friendship had not been repaired or tested by a great declaration; Chase had simply refused to let one angry sentence replace the history he already knew.

At the bridge, the green flag lowered. The giant had stepped back from the edge and was speaking to the signal keeper through the brass listening horn fixed to the tower. His voice travelled down the stone channel with a low resonance. The men could hear only parts of it, but the keeper's answer was clear: the western span had shifted at the middle joint, and a full crossing should wait until the supports had been inspected. The giant nodded, placed one hand against the outer wall, and stepped back onto the wide approach. His fingers were enormous beside the tower's stonework, but he did not pull at the wall or test it with force. He waited for the inspection team to arrive.

A group of men had gathered along the lower road. Their conversation travelled faster than the signal flags. Someone said that every bridge in the country would need rebuilding. Another announced that the whole western ridge was probably moving. A third had heard that a giant in the northern provinces stood taller still. The claim passed from one man to another and returned larger than it had left. They were doing what the Institute's men had begun to recognise in themselves: turning what they could see into a theory about everywhere. Joseph stepped closer to the court gate. “One bridge has shifted. One man has stopped before crossing it. That's what we know.” Andrew looked toward the northern road. “There may be another one.”

“There may be,” Joseph said. “But we haven't met him.”

“That's fair.”

“Don't sound so disappointed.”

Andrew grinned. “I'm not disappointed. I'm keeping the possibility open.” Luke looked past the gate toward the giant. “He's waiting for help.” Gabe gave a small, involuntary shrug. “Everyone is going to help him.”

“Maybe,” Luke said. “Or maybe they're going to stand there and talk about how big he is.”

He offered no judgement about which response would come first. He only asked Gabe, “Will you come with us?” Gabe looked at the others. The offer was not an order, and Luke did not begin walking until he answered. After a moment Gabe nodded. They left the table where it stood, its new brace holding, and crossed the courtyard together. Their own height and breadth changed the rhythm of the walk. Luke had to lower his head beneath the covered passage; Andrew turned his shoulders to pass the tool rack; Tyler carried his notebook against his side so that it would not catch on the doorframe. Gabe walked last. As he followed the others, the seam beneath his arm tightened again. His shoulders were still widening. The growth did not wait for the conversation to end.

The inspection team had reached the western bridge. The giant had lowered himself onto one knee beside the signal platform so that the keeper could speak with him without shouting. Even in that posture, his head stood high above the tower. The damp wind moved through his light hair and across the broad line of his back. He listened while the keeper explained the shifted joint. When the keeper finished, the giant asked whether the support had moved during the rain or only after his first approach. The answer came back that the first reading had been made after the rain, before he arrived. He nodded and asked for the earlier mark. Tyler watched the exchange. “He's asking for a comparison before he decides what caused it.”

“Good,” Joseph said. “Then he's not asking us to pretend we know.”

The giant turned toward them. The signal keeper introduced the group and explained that they had helped repair the outer table and had experience checking large shared structures. The man looked from Tyler's notebook to Andrew's hands, then to Luke, who had already picked up the spare brace from the maintenance cart. His gaze did not linger on any one body as if he were choosing a favourite. He was assessing who could do which part of the work.

“My name is Dorian Vale,” he said. “I came along the western road because the lower crossing was reported as serviceable. I can wait here, but I need to know whether the route can carry me before the light changes.”

His voice was clear, low, and unhurried. He did not ask them to admire his size or excuse it. He had a route to complete and a bridge that might not be safe. Tyler introduced himself and asked to see the inspection marks. Andrew walked the length of the lower approach, listening to the sound under his boots. Luke set down the brace and checked the stone around the outer support. Chase remained near the signal keeper, asking whether the earlier readings had been preserved. Joseph helped move the loose measuring board to a level place. Gabe stood at the edge of the group, uncertain whether he wanted to be there. Dorian noticed him. “Would you rather work on the lower joint?” Gabe looked up. “Why?”

“Because you are standing beside it, and the others have already taken the upper readings. I don't know whether you want the task.”

It was a simple question, but Gabe felt the room inside it: Dorian had noticed him without deciding what he wanted. He glanced at Luke. Luke did not answer for him. “I'll check it,” Gabe said. He crouched by the lower support. His growing shoulders made the movement slower than it had been the week before, and he had to shift one foot farther out to keep his balance. He ran his hand along the stone seam and found a fine line of grit beneath the inner plate. The joint had not failed, but it had moved enough to disturb the bedding. He called Tyler over and showed him the mark. Tyler examined it, then compared it with the signal keeper's record. The evidence suggested that the first movement had occurred during the rain, but the new load had altered the position slightly. Dorian had not caused the original defect, though his approach had made the limit visible.

“That's the distinction,” Tyler said. “The bridge was already moving. His approach showed us how little reserve remained.”

Gabe looked toward Dorian. He had heard him. He did not look offended; he looked relieved to have a usable answer.

**DORIAN:** “Thank you. I would rather know that than be told it is safe because everyone wants the road open.”

The work became more exact. Tyler set the measurements. Andrew tested the lower surface by moving a weighted frame over the approach, then listened for the change in resonance. Luke held the brace in place while the keeper marked the shifted line. Chase found the original record and placed it beside the new reading, leaving the dates and sources visible. Joseph went back to the court for the longer timber support. Gabe stayed at the joint, holding the gauge steady while Tyler checked the distance. While they worked, Dorian continued to grow. The change was slow but unmistakable. His shoulders rose against the tower's upper line; the angle of his arms altered as their mass increased; the dark fabric at his upper back stretched and settled over a broader frame. When he stood to give the men room, he had to place one hand against the higher wall to steady himself. His height made the bridge seem smaller, but his care made the inspection possible. He waited through each measurement and did not ask anyone to shorten the process for his convenience.

Andrew glanced up at him. “You grew while we were checking the brace.” Dorian looked down at his shoulder, then at the tower mark. “A little.”

“Does it usually happen while you're standing still?”

“Sometimes. It is easier to notice when I have something fixed beside me.”

Andrew looked delighted. “That's what the fence did.” Tyler glanced at him. “The fence?”

“Back at the field. The same sound changed when the people were standing inside the dugout. A fixed place tells you what has changed around it.”

Tyler returned to the gauge. “That's actually useful.” Andrew's expression brightened. “You could have said that without sounding surprised.”

“I was surprised.”

They both laughed. It was a small exchange, but it restored the particular rhythm of their friendship: Andrew making a connection no one else had asked for, Tyler testing it instead of dismissing it, and each man enjoying the other's difference without having to win. Dorian watched them with an expression that suggested he had understood the joke even if he had not heard the whole earlier story. Gabe held the gauge steady. His own arms were changing. The sleeves of his shirt had climbed farther up his biceps, and the fabric across his chest had begun to pull when he leaned forward. He looked at Dorian again, not with the first sharp resentment but with a more complicated attention. He was not taking Gabe's place. He was doing something Gabe respected. He did not yet know whether he liked the man, and he did not need to decide before the work was finished.

When the inspection was complete, the bridge remained closed to a full crossing. The team had identified the shifted support, recorded the readings, and placed a temporary brace so that the lower approach could be used for inspection equipment. Dorian accepted the result without complaint. He asked the keeper when the permanent repair could be reviewed, then thanked each man for the part he had taken. He thanked Gabe last, not because his work mattered most, but because he had kept the gauge steady while the others moved around the joint.

**DORIAN:** “You caught the grit under the plate. I might have blamed the wrong load without that.”

**GABE:** “It was there.”

“Yes. You saw it.”

The words were ordinary. Gabe had expected another comment about the giant, another exchange about scale, or some request that he demonstrate how much he had grown. Instead Dorian had noticed his work. He did not know what to do with that, so he nodded. The group returned to the courtyard by the longer path. Dorian remained at the western platform while the signal keeper prepared the next report. The men could still see him above the wall, and his shadow moved slowly across the road as he shifted his weight. Around the court, other people continued to say that every road would have to change, every district would be affected, every man would grow. Tyler corrected one account, then stopped when he realised the man repeating it was not asking for information. Joseph suggested they wait for a report from the next district. Andrew had already begun sketching a way to compare the bridge's sound before and after repair. Chase wrote down the actual findings, then closed the notebook before anyone asked him to turn Dorian's height into a headline.

Gabe stood beside Luke at the repaired table. The men were all growing in their own ways. Luke's back had broadened since breakfast; Tyler's hands seemed larger around the notebook; Andrew had to adjust his grip on the pencil; Chase's old jacket pulled at the shoulders; Joseph had become taller against the window frame; Gabe's sleeves had shortened another fraction. They were not changing at the same rate, and no one could use the group as proof that the whole world had changed. But they were changing together, which was a different kind of fact. “I was jealous,” Gabe said. Luke looked at him. “I know.”

“Don't say it like you knew all along.”

“I didn't. I guessed.”

Gabe watched the western wall. “When he arrived, everyone looked at him. I wanted them to keep looking at me. I hated that I wanted it.” Luke rested one hand on the table rather than on Gabe's shoulder. “You don't have to be ashamed of wanting to be noticed.”

“I don't want to need it.”

“Those aren't the same thing.”

Gabe considered that. His friend had not offered him an easy escape. Luke knew what it was to enjoy being the largest man in a room and what it was to discover that somebody else had changed the room again. He also knew that his own kindness could become another demand if he tried to make Gabe feel better before Gabe had finished speaking. “I wanted you to notice,” Gabe said. Luke looked directly at him. “I did.”

“Not just the growth.”

“I know.”

“No. I don't think you do.”

Luke went quiet. The old instinct rose in him—to close the distance, reassure, make a place, put his hand on the broadening shoulder. He let it pass. “Tell me, then.” Gabe looked at him, surprised by the invitation. “I wanted you to notice that I was pleased. I wanted you to be pleased with me. And when everyone turned toward him, I felt stupid for having been pleased in the first place.” Luke nodded. “I was pleased. I should have told you.” Gabe gave a short breath that was almost a laugh. “You were fixing the table.”

“I can do both.”

“Apparently.”

Luke smiled, but did not turn the moment into a joke. He moved the chair beside him a little farther out and waited. Gabe sat. The chair had been reinforced for their changing size, and it took his weight without a sound. Luke sat opposite him. Their knees nearly touched beneath the table, but neither man had to rearrange himself to make the conversation fit. They had learned that sort of accommodation over time.

Across the courtyard, Tyler and Andrew were already arguing over the shape of the sound map. Tyler wanted a repeatable test; Andrew wanted to include the moment when the bridge answered a moving load. Chase was asking whether the signal keeper would permit a copy of the original readings to be kept with the repair record. Joseph had gone to fetch bread from the kitchen and had returned with enough for everyone, including the keeper who had stayed behind. Their relationships were present in the ordinary friction of the work. No one was suddenly transformed into a perfect friend. They kept having to interpret one another, revise a first impression, and decide whether to stay.

Dorian returned to the western approach later in the afternoon, having received permission to use the lower inspection platform. He had grown again. His head now rose well above the old tower's roof, and the shoulders that had seemed immense in the morning had widened enough to alter the line of his shadow across the gate. He stopped at the agreed marker, waited for the signal, and lowered himself until he could speak with the keeper. The men watched, but this time they did not fall silent as one body. Luke continued talking to Gabe. Tyler kept his finger on the measurement line. Andrew stopped only long enough to hear the bridge's note as Dorian shifted his weight. Chase asked before taking a photograph. Joseph carried the bread inside.

Gabe stood after a moment and walked to the signal platform. Luke did not follow immediately. He watched Gabe go, then returned to the table. There was trust in allowing the conversation to happen without supervising it. Gabe stopped at the platform's outer rail. Dorian looked down.

“I owe you an apology,” Gabe said. “I said you sickened me. You didn't do anything to me.”

Dorian considered him. “You don't have to like me.”

“I know.”

“But you don't have to make me responsible for what you're afraid of.”

He nodded. “I was jealous.”

“Of me?”

“Of what happened when you arrived. Everyone looked at you, and I wanted to be the person they were looking at.”

Dorian glanced toward the courtyard. His height placed the whole gathering within one broad field of view, but he did not speak as though the men were beneath him in any other sense. “I know what that is like,” he said. “People see the size first. Sometimes they see the work after. Sometimes they never get there.” Gabe looked up. “Does it bother you?”

“Some days. Other days I need the size to do the work. I won't pretend it is always one thing.” He shifted one foot within the marked area. His body rose another small increment, visible against the tower. “I came here because the western road needs a safer crossing. I would like to be able to visit without asking six people to stop what they are doing every time I arrive.”

Gabe laughed, this time without bitterness. “We're trying to make the place fit everyone.”

“Then keep doing that.”

He offered his hand across the rail. Dorian lowered his carefully. His fingers were enormous beside Gabe's, but he met the gesture without turning it into a performance. The contact lasted only a moment. It was not a friendship yet, and neither pretended otherwise. It was an agreement to begin again with something more honest than the first sentence. When Gabe returned to the table, Luke was still there. The others had started arranging the evening meal. Andrew had drawn a rough sound map on the back of the inspection sheet; Tyler had marked which lines were observation and which were interpretation. Chase had left a blank space for Dorian's own account. Joseph placed a second loaf beside the first. The men had made room for a new record without deciding in advance what it would mean.

**SETH:**

That's better than the crowd's version. They wanted to say the whole world had changed. What changed here was more particular: a bridge was found unsafe, a giant was met on his own terms, and one friendship had a difficult conversation it might otherwise have avoided.

**NICK:**

And the bodies are still changing. Look at Gabe's sleeves. Look at Luke's shoulders. Look at the marks Tyler has added to the table leg. They haven't finished growing, and none of them has to become the same man as the others.

**SETH:**

Dorian will return when the western crossing is ready.

**NICK:**

And Gabe will have to decide what he wants their next meeting to be. That is a better question than whether he can be the largest person in the courtyard. At dinner, the men sat around the repaired table. Its brace held, the old fastener remained in its labelled tray, and the western bridge report lay folded beside Tyler's notebook. Dorian had remained at the survey camp for the evening; he had his own route to plan and a repair schedule to discuss with the keeper. His absence did not empty the room of what had happened. Andrew wanted to test the sound map after the repair. Tyler wanted the original readings. Chase wanted Dorian's permission before including his image in the archive. Joseph wanted the group to eat before the food cooled. Luke wanted to know whether Gabe would come on the next inspection. Gabe said he would.

He did not say he would because he had forgiven himself completely, or because he had become indifferent to being seen. He said yes because the western road was a place they could return to, and because the men at the table would be there with him. Outside, the tower remained where it had always stood, though its old height no longer seemed like a reliable measure. Far beyond it, Dorian continued along the ridge, his silhouette slowly changing against the last light. The Commons had not learned what was happening everywhere. It had learned who would walk back to the bridge together.

The next morning, Tyler would receive a report from a second district. It would contain a different kind of measurement, an unfamiliar name, and a claim that the men at the western gate would be tempted to recognise too quickly. The report would have to be read before it could become a world.
