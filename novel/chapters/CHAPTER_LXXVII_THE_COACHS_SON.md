# Chapter LXXVII — The Coach's Son

The training hall was quiet enough that the first sounds of the morning carried across it: the click of a collar settling against a bar, the low movement of the ventilation above the rafters, and the rubber soles of Travis's shoes crossing the floor toward the platform. He had arrived before the other members of the group. His father was already there, standing beside the rack with a clipboard tucked under one arm and a towel over his shoulder. To most of the men who trained at the hall, Mr. Kaufman was simply Coach Kaufman: exacting, patient, difficult to distract once a session had begun. To Travis he was also the man who had taught him to ride a bicycle, who had waited outside a school auditorium after a performance, who knew the difference between his silence when he was concentrating and his silence when he was angry. The two roles had occupied the same life for so long that neither of them always noticed when one had begun to crowd the other.

“You're early,” Mr. Kaufman said.

“So are you.”

“I work here.”

“I train here.”

His father glanced at the empty platform. “Then let's see what you brought in today.”

Travis set his bag down and began his warm-up. He had been working toward a stronger, more consistent lift for several months. The numbers were not secret, but he had stopped wanting them to be the first thing discussed whenever he entered the hall. Every session seemed to begin with a question about whether he had improved, whether he could add weight, whether the last attempt had been a step forward. He knew his father meant to help. He also knew that a coach could make a person feel measured even when the clipboard remained closed.

Mr. Kaufman watched the first movement without speaking. He corrected the position of Travis's feet with a small gesture, then asked him to repeat the setup. Travis did. The second repetition was steadier.

“Better,” his father said. “Keep that position when the weight comes up.”

“I know.”

“I know you know. I'm asking you to do it.”

Travis looked at him. The answer had come out sharper than he intended. Mr. Kaufman lowered the clipboard.

“What's going on?”

“Nothing.”

“That's not an answer.”

“It's the answer I have right now.”

The coach nodded once. He did not press. Travis finished the warm-up, loaded the bar, and began the first working set. The movement was sound, but he rushed the final repetition. His father called the set there, before the next attempt could become a matter of pride. Travis stepped away and sat on the bench, breathing hard.

“You stopped me.”

“Yes.”

“I had another one.”

“You had another one if you wanted to turn the last repetition into a different lift.”

Travis looked down at his hands. “You always say that.”

“Because it's still true.”

“Sometimes I want you to let me work it out.”

Mr. Kaufman rested the clipboard on the rack. “I do let you work things out. I also have a responsibility to stop a set when the movement changes enough that the risk is no longer worth it.”

“I know what your responsibility is.”

“Then tell me what yours is.”

Travis looked up. The question irritated him because it was fair. He had expected another instruction, perhaps a reminder that effort did not excuse poor technique. Instead, his father had left the question with him.

“To know when I'm done,” Travis said.

“And?”

“To know why I'm doing it.”

Mr. Kaufman waited.

“And to decide what I want from the session without having to prove something every time I walk in.”

The quiet did not mean the gym had stopped. It meant the two of them had begun to hear the building around their conversation. The ventilation moved above the rafters, pulling cool air through the high windows. A barbell settled onto its hooks with a dull metallic sound. Somewhere near the storage bay, a man laughed at something another resident had said, and the sound travelled across the rubber floor before dissolving beneath the ceiling. The hall was large enough that a person could feel alone in it while several men trained nearby. Its floor had been reinforced in stages, and the original timber platform remained at the centre as a reminder of the Institute's earlier dimensions. Newer platforms stood alongside it, their surfaces wider and their supports heavier. The racks had been raised, the collars enlarged, and the storage system rearranged so that the men could handle equipment without having to force their bodies into spaces designed for someone else.

Travis sat on the bench and leaned forward, his forearms resting against his thighs. He had grown in recent months, enough that his old warm-up shirt no longer sat comfortably across the shoulders. The sleeves tightened when he reached forward, and the hem had begun to ride up during squats. He had been pleased by the changes at first. He liked the feeling of a stronger back, the new density through his chest, the greater stability in his legs. He still liked those things. What he disliked was the assumption that every improvement had to be converted into another target. The men around him were growing too, each at a different rate. The hall was full of shifting baselines. A new shoulder measurement could be a private source of satisfaction, or it could become the first question a person heard every time he walked through the door. Travis had begun to want a session in which nobody asked what came next.

Mr. Kaufman stood beside the rack with the clipboard lowered. The coach's posture was still attentive, but his expression had softened. He was a strong, broad man whose own frame had changed with the Institute. His shirts needed more room across the back, and the old staff jacket had been replaced by one with a reinforced shoulder seam. He had not grown as quickly as some of the residents, but he had grown enough to notice the way his son now stood beside him. Travis was approaching his height, and the comparison had begun to shift in ways neither of them had expected. It was one thing to watch a son grow taller. It was another to see the familiar body of a young adult man becoming a powerful frame in its own right, still carrying the habits of the boy he had raised.

Mr. Kaufman remembered teaching Travis to brace his feet on the kitchen floor when he was young enough that the bar had been only a training stick. He remembered the first time Travis had asked to lift with the older men, and the way he had tried to hide his excitement by pretending not to care. He remembered the first competition, the long drive home, the meal eaten in silence because the result had not gone as Travis hoped. Those memories were not all fond. They included arguments, missed calls, stubbornness, and the years in which his son had wanted advice only after he had already made the decision. The coach's clipboard held none of that. It contained sets, repetitions, load, rest, and notes about movement. The father had to remember the rest without turning it into an instruction.

**SETH:** “This is one of those gym scenes in which the equipment is not the difficult part.”

**NICK:** “The equipment is beautifully organised. The difficult part is that the coach has to remember he isn't coaching the whole person.”

**SETH:** “And the son has to remember that his father can be right about a bar path and still need a reminder about lunch.”

Travis rubbed his palms together. The skin was warm from the bar, and there was a faint chalk line along the heel of one hand. He had always liked the physical clarity of training. A weight either moved or it did not; a repetition either held together or it did not. It had helped him through periods when other parts of life were uncertain. But his father could make the gym feel like a place where every movement was also a judgement about the person doing it. Mr. Kaufman did not intend that. He was a careful coach, and he believed that good technique protected the lifter's future. Yet the more he tried to prepare Travis, the less room there seemed to be for Travis to discover his own way of working.

The first set that morning had shown the problem. Travis had rushed the final repetition because he wanted to prove he could complete it. His father had stopped him because the movement had changed. Both men were right about something. The difficulty came from the fact that neither had said what he was protecting. Travis had been protecting his sense of independence; his father had been protecting his son's future ability to keep training. Once those concerns were named, the argument could become a conversation instead of a contest.

**TRAVIS:** “I don't mind you correcting me. I mind feeling like I'm waiting for you to tell me whether I did it properly.”

Mr. Kaufman nodded slowly.

**TRAVIS:** “I want to know what I did before you tell me what you saw. Sometimes I can feel that something went wrong, and I want to say it first. I want you to hear me before the clipboard comes out.”

The coach looked at the paper under his arm. It had a list of the morning's sets, a note about the bar path, and a short record of the last session. The clipboard had become so familiar that he sometimes carried it into conversations without noticing. He set it on the floor beside the rack. The movement was small, but Travis watched him do it.

**MR. KAUFMAN:** “All right. Tell me what you felt on that last repetition.”

Travis looked toward the platform. “I lost the pause at the bottom. My balance moved forward, and I tried to finish it by pushing harder.”

“That's what I saw.”

“I know. But I felt it before you stopped me.”

“Then you should tell me.”

“I wanted to.”

His father waited. Travis understood that he was being given time rather than a demand. He took a breath and looked down at his hands. His fingers had become thicker through the knuckles, and the veins stood more clearly along the back of his wrists. He liked the strength he was building, but he was still learning how to inhabit a body that changed while he was learning to use it. There were days when the bar sat differently in his hands, when his stance seemed wider, when his knees tracked along a slightly altered line. He did not want every change to be interpreted as progress or failure. Sometimes it was simply new information.

“I want to train in a way that lets me notice the changes,” he said. “Not just chase them.”

His father looked at him. “I can do that.”

“Can you?”

“Yes.”

The answer was direct. Travis had expected a qualification. The absence of one unsettled him, but in a useful way. His father was listening without needing to prove that he had already understood. The coach's work would still involve correction, but the correction could begin from what Travis reported rather than from what the clipboard demanded.

The hall seemed to grow quieter around them. A door opened at the far end and someone wheeled a stack of plates toward the storage bay, but neither man turned.

His father nodded. “That's reasonable.”

Travis had prepared himself for an argument. The absence of one left him uncertain.

“Is it?” he asked.

“Yes. But I need you to tell me when the goal changes. I can't coach a target you haven't given me.”

“I don't always know until I'm here.”

“Then we can start by asking. You don't have to arrive with a complete answer.”

Travis leaned back against the wall. “Sometimes I don't want Coach Kaufman.”

His father looked at him carefully. “Who do you want?”

“My dad.”

The answer did not sound as simple once it had been spoken. Mr. Kaufman took the towel from his shoulder and folded it across the bench. For a moment he seemed to be considering whether he should say something as a coach or as a father. Then he sat beside Travis, leaving a little space between them.

“All right,” he said. “I'm here.”

They remained there while the hall continued its morning routine. Mr. Kaufman asked whether Travis had slept. Travis said he had, though not especially well. They spoke about the family car, a leaking tap, and a film Travis had been meaning to watch. His father listened without turning each answer into a lesson. The conversation moved slowly, and that was what made it useful. Travis began to feel that he had returned to the room rather than been brought back under examination.

After a while Mr. Kaufman stood and picked up the clipboard.

“Do you want to finish the session?”

“Yes. But I want to change it.”

“Tell me how.”

“I want the next sets lighter. I want to work on the pause at the bottom, and I don't want you calling out every repetition. Let me tell you what I notice first.”

His father considered the request. “That works. I'll watch the movement. You give me your account between sets, and I'll add anything that materially changes the next attempt.”

“Deal.”

The session resumed. Mr. Kaufman stood at the side of the platform, attentive but quieter. Travis completed the sets with less weight and more control. Between them he described what he felt: where his balance shifted, when his breath became difficult to coordinate, and how the pause changed his confidence in the movement. His father offered one correction about the bar path and then let him try again. The set ended with Travis sitting on the edge of the platform, breathing steadily while the bar rested in its hooks. He had done fewer repetitions than he once would have considered worthwhile, but he felt more aware of his body than he had after many harder sessions. The movement had become clearer. He could tell where his balance shifted, how much room his knees needed, and how his shoulders settled when he stopped trying to rush the lift. The new breadth through his upper back made the old warm-up shirt pull when he leaned forward. He tugged the hem down, then laughed at himself.

**TRAVIS:** “I'm going to need another shirt.”

His father looked at the fabric. “You were saying that last week.”

“I thought it had more time.”

“So did the shirt.”

Travis smiled. Mr. Kaufman brought him a towel and a bottle of water. He did not mention the latest increase in Travis's measurements. He had noticed it, of course. The change showed in the way the young man's shoulders now filled the space between his neck and arms, and in the more substantial line of his thighs as he stood. But the session had been about movement and judgement, and the coach was learning not to let the visual change take over every conversation.

They walked around the platform together. Mr. Kaufman asked Travis to show him the point at which the pause became difficult. Travis demonstrated without a weight, then repeated it with the empty bar. His father watched his feet and the line of his back. He made one correction, then asked Travis what he noticed. Travis adjusted his stance and tried again. The second movement was smoother. It did not look spectacular. It looked useful. Mr. Kaufman liked that more than he expected.

**MR. KAUFMAN:** “That's the one I'd keep.”

**TRAVIS:** “Because it looked better?”

**MR. KAUFMAN:** “Because you knew what changed.”

Travis nodded. He had wanted his father to say something like that for a long time, but he had not known how to ask. The problem had not been a lack of praise. It had been the feeling that praise arrived only after the coach had decided what the movement meant. Now the two men had a new sequence: Travis would describe his own experience, his father would add what he had observed, and together they would decide what to change next. It would not always be easy. Mr. Kaufman would sometimes see a risk that Travis did not, and Travis would sometimes want to repeat a movement his father thought should be left alone. They would have to keep talking.

When they moved the plates back to the rack, Travis took the smaller collars and his father carried the heavier ones. The collars were broad enough for Travis's hands, though the metal edges left a cool pressure against his palms. He placed them in the storage slot and stepped back. His body felt warmer and more settled than it had at the beginning of the session. He was still growing, and the old shirt would need replacing, but he did not feel compelled to add weight to the bar simply to prove the growth meant something. He could finish a session well and go home with his father.

The next repetition was better, not because someone had demanded more from him, but because he understood what he was trying to do.

When the group arrived, they found the two men reviewing the session sheet. Mr. Kaufman had written the work down in his usual compact hand. Travis took the pencil and added a note beside the final set: **repeat the same load; do not increase until the pause stays consistent**.

His father read it. “That's a good call.”

“It's my programme.”

“It's your training. I'm still your coach.”

“And my dad.”

Mr. Kaufman smiled. “That part doesn't go on the sheet.”

“No?”

“No. The sheet is for the work. The rest is for us.”

They packed the plates away together. Travis carried the smaller collars to the storage rack while his father checked the platform and returned the clipboard to its hook. Nothing in the hall had changed in any visible way, but the arrangement between them had shifted. Mr. Kaufman would still coach him. He would still correct him when the movement required it, and Travis would still sometimes dislike hearing the correction. But the son would be allowed to speak before the coach decided what the session meant.

At the door, Travis paused.

“Are you coming home for lunch?”

“I was planning to.”

“Not as Coach?”

His father picked up his bag. “I'll try to leave the clipboard here.”

Travis laughed. “Good.”

They crossed the courtyard toward the dining hall. The morning air was cool, and the stone path still held a little dampness from the previous night's rain. Travis walked with his hands in the pockets of his training shorts, feeling the new weight of his shoulders settle as his breathing returned to normal. His father kept pace beside him. For once, neither man talked about the next session. They spoke about the family car, a film Travis had seen with a friend, and whether the small grocery shop near the south gate still sold the bread his father liked. The conversation was ordinary, but that was precisely what Travis had wanted. He could be a man who trained seriously and still be a son who knew his father's preferred bread.

The dining hall was bright with the morning light. Long tables stood beneath timber rafters, and the kitchen counter carried trays of eggs, potatoes, fruit, toast, and coffee. The room was already busy with adult men returning from training or preparing for the day. Some were broad enough to make the original chairs look narrow; others were still adjusting to the way their bodies had changed overnight. The Institute had responded with deeper seats, stronger legs, and tables that could be extended. Travis and his father found a place near the window. Mr. Kaufman brought two plates, then sat across from him rather than beside him. The distance was not a problem. They could talk across a table without turning the conversation into a coaching session.

**TRAVIS:** “Do you ever get tired of being Coach?”

His father thought about it. “Sometimes.”

“Why do you keep doing it?”

“Because I like helping people learn something they couldn't do before.”

“Even when they don't listen?”

“Especially then.”

Travis grinned. “That sounds exhausting.”

“It can be.”

They ate for a while. His father did not ask how the last set had felt. Travis described it because he wanted to, explaining the point at which the pause had become easier and the way his balance had changed. Mr. Kaufman listened, then added one observation about the position of his feet. Travis considered it and agreed. The correction did not feel like an evaluation. It was part of a conversation between two men who cared about the same work.

When the meal was nearly finished, Mr. Kaufman reached for his coffee and paused. “I don't always know when you want your father and when you want your coach.”

Travis looked at him. “I don't always know either.”

“Then we'll ask.”

“That's it?”

“That's a start.”

Travis laughed. “You make it sound like a programme.”

“Everything sounds like a programme when you say it in this building.”

They finished breakfast and walked toward the entrance. The training hall was visible across the courtyard, its high windows catching the sun. Travis could see the platform where he had worked that morning. He did not feel the need to return to it. The session had ended properly. His father carried the clipboard in one hand, then stopped and left it on the bench by the door. Travis noticed.

**TRAVIS:** “You remembered.”

**MR. KAUFMAN:** “I said I'd try.”

“Good.”

They walked on together.



They walked out together into the cool morning. The hall remained open behind them, ready for the next group, and the session record stayed on its hook with the new note in Travis's handwriting. It did not describe the whole relationship. It only preserved what they had agreed to do next.

On the path toward the residential wing, Travis walked close enough to his father that their shoulders nearly aligned. He had grown again, and his frame now carried more of the space around him. Mr. Kaufman noticed the change without making it the first subject of the morning. At the next doorway, he paused and let Travis pass ahead. The gesture was small, but Travis noticed it. His father was not measuring him against the old doorway or trying to decide what the change required. He was simply walking beside him. Travis looked back and grinned.

**TRAVIS:** “Come on, Dad.”

Mr. Kaufman followed. The clipboard remained behind in the gym, and for the next few minutes the two men had nothing to do except get home together.
