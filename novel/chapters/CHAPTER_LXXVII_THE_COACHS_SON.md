# Chapter LXXVII — The Coach's Son

The training hall was quiet enough that the first sounds of the morning carried across it: the click of a collar settling against a bar, the low movement of the ventilation above the rafters, and the rubber soles of Travis's shoes crossing the floor toward the platform. He had arrived before the other members of the group. His father was already there, standing beside the rack with a clipboard tucked under one arm and a towel over his shoulder. To most of the men who trained at the hall, Mr. Kaufman was simply Coach Kaufman: exacting, patient, difficult to distract once a session had begun. To Travis he was also the man who had taught him to ride a bicycle, who had waited outside a school auditorium after a performance, who knew the difference between his silence when he was concentrating and his silence when he was angry. The two roles had occupied the same life for so long that neither of them always noticed when one had begun to crowd the other.

“You're early,” Mr. Kaufman said.

“So are you.”

“I work here.”

“I train here.”

His father glanced at the empty platform. “Then let's see what you brought in today.”

Travis set his bag down and began his warm-up. He had been working toward a stronger, more consistent lift for several months. The numbers were not secret, but he had stopped wanting them to be the first thing discussed whenever he entered the hall. Every session seemed to begin with a question about whether he had improved, whether he could add weight, whether the last attempt had been a step forward. He knew his father meant to help. He also knew that a coach could make a person feel measured even when the clipboard remained closed.

Mr. Kaufman watched the first movement without speaking. He corrected the position of Travis's feet with a small gesture, then asked him to repeat the setup. Travis did. The second repetition was steadier.

“Better,” his father said. “Keep that position when the weight comes up.”

“I know.”

“I know you know. I'm asking you to do it.”

Travis looked at him. The answer had come out sharper than he intended. Mr. Kaufman lowered the clipboard.

“What's going on?”

“Nothing.”

“That's not an answer.”

“It's the answer I have right now.”

The coach nodded once. He did not press. Travis finished the warm-up, loaded the bar, and began the first working set. The movement was sound, but he rushed the final repetition. His father called the set there, before the next attempt could become a matter of pride. Travis stepped away and sat on the bench, breathing hard.

“You stopped me.”

“Yes.”

“I had another one.”

“You had another one if you wanted to turn the last repetition into a different lift.”

Travis looked down at his hands. “You always say that.”

“Because it's still true.”

“Sometimes I want you to let me work it out.”

Mr. Kaufman rested the clipboard on the rack. “I do let you work things out. I also have a responsibility to stop a set when the movement changes enough that the risk is no longer worth it.”

“I know what your responsibility is.”

“Then tell me what yours is.”

Travis looked up. The question irritated him because it was fair. He had expected another instruction, perhaps a reminder that effort did not excuse poor technique. Instead, his father had left the question with him.

“To know when I'm done,” Travis said.

“And?”

“To know why I'm doing it.”

Mr. Kaufman waited.

“And to decide what I want from the session without having to prove something every time I walk in.”

The hall seemed to grow quieter around them. A door opened at the far end and someone wheeled a stack of plates toward the storage bay, but neither man turned.

His father nodded. “That's reasonable.”

Travis had prepared himself for an argument. The absence of one left him uncertain.

“Is it?” he asked.

“Yes. But I need you to tell me when the goal changes. I can't coach a target you haven't given me.”

“I don't always know until I'm here.”

“Then we can start by asking. You don't have to arrive with a complete answer.”

Travis leaned back against the wall. “Sometimes I don't want Coach Kaufman.”

His father looked at him carefully. “Who do you want?”

“My dad.”

The answer did not sound as simple once it had been spoken. Mr. Kaufman took the towel from his shoulder and folded it across the bench. For a moment he seemed to be considering whether he should say something as a coach or as a father. Then he sat beside Travis, leaving a little space between them.

“All right,” he said. “I'm here.”

They remained there while the hall continued its morning routine. Mr. Kaufman asked whether Travis had slept. Travis said he had, though not especially well. They spoke about the family car, a leaking tap, and a film Travis had been meaning to watch. His father listened without turning each answer into a lesson. The conversation moved slowly, and that was what made it useful. Travis began to feel that he had returned to the room rather than been brought back under examination.

After a while Mr. Kaufman stood and picked up the clipboard.

“Do you want to finish the session?”

“Yes. But I want to change it.”

“Tell me how.”

“I want the next sets lighter. I want to work on the pause at the bottom, and I don't want you calling out every repetition. Let me tell you what I notice first.”

His father considered the request. “That works. I'll watch the movement. You give me your account between sets, and I'll add anything that materially changes the next attempt.”

“Deal.”

The session resumed. Mr. Kaufman stood at the side of the platform, attentive but quieter. Travis completed the sets with less weight and more control. Between them he described what he felt: where his balance shifted, when his breath became difficult to coordinate, and how the pause changed his confidence in the movement. His father offered one correction about the bar path and then let him try again. The next repetition was better, not because someone had demanded more from him, but because he understood what he was trying to do.

When the group arrived, they found the two men reviewing the session sheet. Mr. Kaufman had written the work down in his usual compact hand. Travis took the pencil and added a note beside the final set: **repeat the same load; do not increase until the pause stays consistent**.

His father read it. “That's a good call.”

“It's my programme.”

“It's your training. I'm still your coach.”

“And my dad.”

Mr. Kaufman smiled. “That part doesn't go on the sheet.”

“No?”

“No. The sheet is for the work. The rest is for us.”

They packed the plates away together. Travis carried the smaller collars to the storage rack while his father checked the platform and returned the clipboard to its hook. Nothing in the hall had changed in any visible way, but the arrangement between them had shifted. Mr. Kaufman would still coach him. He would still correct him when the movement required it, and Travis would still sometimes dislike hearing the correction. But the son would be allowed to speak before the coach decided what the session meant.

At the door, Travis paused.

“Are you coming home for lunch?”

“I was planning to.”

“Not as Coach?”

His father picked up his bag. “I'll try to leave the clipboard here.”

Travis laughed. “Good.”

They walked out together into the cool morning. The hall remained open behind them, ready for the next group, and the session record stayed on its hook with the new note in Travis's handwriting. It did not describe the whole relationship. It only preserved what they had agreed to do next.

SETH: There's the difference, Nick. The coach can see a movement that needs correcting. A father has to see the son who is doing it, and remember that the boy in front of him is allowed to become a man with his own judgement.

NICK: And the son has to be able to ask for both. He can want instruction without surrendering his own account of the work. He can want his father without having to earn the visit by lifting well.

SETH: The next session is already on the calendar, but it isn't the only thing they've got.

NICK: No. Lunch is waiting, and this time nobody is bringing the clipboard to the table.
