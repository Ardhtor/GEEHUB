# Chapter LXXV — The Table After Rain

The rain had stopped during the night, but the common room still smelled of wet timber. A narrow line of water had found its way beneath the eastern window and darkened the boards along the wall. Someone had set a folded towel against the sill. The large table stood in the middle of the room, pulled a little away from its usual place so that the floor could dry. Luke arrived first with two more towels and a mug balanced in one hand. He stopped when he saw the table.

“Who's been using it?”

“Everybody,” Andrew called from the doorway. He was carrying a small tool case and looked pleased with himself. “Which is why it has decided to become a moving object.”

The table shifted a fraction when Andrew touched its corner. One leg had loosened where the lower brace met the frame. The movement was slight, but the sound travelled through the room: a dry click, then a faint complaint from the wood. Luke set the mug down. He put his palm against the table and tested it without leaning his weight into it. “Nobody sit here until we know what's happening.” Tyler came in behind Andrew, already looking at the joint. “It's not the leg. The brace is walking out of the socket.”

“You saw that from the door?”

“I heard it.”

Andrew crouched beside him. “I heard a table asking for attention.”

“It asked for a repair.”

“Same thing, in this room.”

Chase entered without interrupting them. He looked at the towel, the dark floorboards, the table's new position, and the small gap between the brace and the frame. His eyes moved once around the room as if checking the order in which the night had happened. He took the folded note from the sill and read the short message someone had left: *Water came in. Floor is drying. Table moved. Don't put weight on the east corner.* “Who wrote this?” he asked.

“I did,” Luke said. “I didn't know how far the water had travelled.”

Chase folded the note along its existing crease. “Good. It says what happened. It doesn't pretend the floor is dry.” Tyler had already taken a ruler from the tool case. He measured the distance from the brace to the socket and wrote the number on the back of an old envelope. Andrew put one finger against the loose joint. “Don't push it yet,” Tyler said.

“I wasn't pushing.”

“You were about to.”

“I was considering pushing.”

“That is what you call it when you don't want to admit you've started.”

Luke smiled. The laugh was small, but it settled the room. Andrew lifted both hands and backed away from the joint with an exaggerated expression of innocence. Gabe arrived while Tyler was checking the underside. He had changed out of his outdoor clothes and brought a clean shirt folded over one arm. He paused at the threshold when he saw the gathering around the table.

“Did it happen again?”

“The table?” Andrew asked. “Or me?”

“The table.”

“Then yes.”

Gabe crossed the room and looked at the joint. He had learned to notice how a gathering changed when something broke. The first few seconds were often more revealing than the repair itself. Luke had moved close enough to steady the frame. Tyler had narrowed the problem to one connection. Andrew was waiting for a chance to try something. Chase was preserving the account. The table had become the centre of their attention, but none of them was treating the others as an audience.

“It's interesting,” Gabe said, “how quickly everybody gets a different job when something moves.”

“It's not interesting,” Tyler replied. “It's loose.”

“That's the material fact. I'm talking about the room.”

“I know what you're talking about.”

Gabe looked at him. “Do you?” Tyler paused, then glanced at the others. “Yes. I do. I just don't think the room needs to be repaired before the table.” Andrew laughed. “That's the most Tyler thing you've said all week.” Joseph came in carrying a paper bag from the kitchen. He set it on the clear end of the table, then noticed the note and moved it to the windowsill.

“I brought bread,” he said. “I thought we might have breakfast here.”

“Not yet,” Luke said. “The east corner isn't safe.”

Joseph looked at the table. “It was here when the room was still being furnished. There was a split in this same brace. We thought we'd replaced the whole piece.” Tyler checked the underside again. “We replaced the fastener. The brace is original.” “Then the table remembers more than we do,” Andrew said. Chase looked up. “The table remembers the repair. We have the note.” Joseph smiled. “That's what I meant.” The distinction mattered to Chase. He did not correct him. He turned the envelope Tyler had used so that the measurement could be read, then placed the note beside it. One record described the present gap. The other recorded the earlier repair. They were related, but not interchangeable. Gabe watched the two pieces of paper settle beside each other. “That's the part I keep thinking about. The old state doesn't disappear because something changes. But it doesn't get to dictate what the new state has to be.”

Tyler held the brace in place while Luke checked the frame. “Agreed. But we still have to know what we're dealing with.”

“Of course.”

Andrew opened the tool case. “And we need to know whether we're allowed to have breakfast before or after the table becomes a table again.” “After,” Luke said.

“Unreasonable.”

“Safe.”

Andrew leaned back against the cabinet. He was ready to start, but Luke's answer had made the pause real. He looked at the others and waited. It was a small adjustment, and Chase noticed it. Andrew did not fill the space with another joke. He let Tyler finish measuring. The repair took longer than any of them expected. The socket had widened slightly, and the old fastener had pulled against the grain. Tyler proposed a small wooden shim, cut to the measured gap. Luke held the frame without forcing it. Andrew trimmed the shim, then offered it to Tyler for inspection. Chase wrote down the order of the work. Gabe collected the fragments and placed them in a shallow tray, keeping the old fastener rather than throwing it away.

Joseph remained near the kitchen end of the room. He did not hover. He opened the paper bag and set out the bread on a board, then found the jam and a small dish of butter. When Luke looked over, Joseph raised the knife.

“I'm not putting anything on the table until you say it's ready.”

“Good,” Luke said. “Keep that side clear.”

“I remember.”

The phrase made Luke turn. For a moment he looked as though he might answer with a practical instruction. Then he understood that Joseph was not asking him to explain the table. He was recalling the earlier room, the first repair, the breakfast they had eaten after moving in. Luke nodded.

“I remember too.”

Tyler fitted the shim. Andrew held the light. Luke steadied the frame. Chase watched the socket as the pieces came together. Gabe stood back far enough to see the whole table rather than only the joint. Joseph kept the food covered. When the fastener was seated, Tyler asked everyone to move away. He measured the gap again, then checked the table diagonally. Andrew wanted to test it immediately. Luke told him to wait until the frame had settled. Andrew rolled his eyes, but he waited. The first test was gentle. Tyler pressed down at the corner while Luke watched the opposite leg. The table held. They repeated the test from the side, then from the middle. Nothing shifted.

“Now,” Tyler said, “we can put weight on it.”

Andrew placed both hands on the tabletop and leaned forward. “Not all your weight,” Tyler said.

“I am being scientific.”

“You are being theatrical.”

“Both can happen.”

Luke shook his head, smiling. “Just sit down.” Andrew drew out a chair and sat. He looked around at the others as if he had been granted an honour. Gabe took the chair beside him. Joseph brought the bread. Chase moved the note and envelope to the shelf rather than leaving them among the plates. Tyler remained standing until he had checked the brace one last time. “You can sit too,” Luke told him.

“I know.”

“Then sit.”

Tyler sat. For a while they ate. The conversation moved away from the table. Andrew told them about a shortcut he had tried on the walk back the night before and how it had turned out to be longer than the ordinary route. Tyler asked why he had expected it to be shorter. Andrew said that the route had looked shorter on the map, which prompted Tyler to ask whether he had measured the map or merely looked at it. “Here we go,” Chase said. Andrew pointed at him with the bread. “You were waiting for that.”

“I was waiting for you to finish.”

Gabe laughed. Tyler tried not to, then gave in. Joseph asked Andrew to draw the route on the back of the envelope. Andrew did, and Tyler corrected the scale. The correction made the drawing worse before it made it better. They argued about the bend by the old fence, then decided to walk it together later and settle the matter in person. Luke listened, eating slowly. He noticed that Gabe had chosen a seat where he could see the whole group. He noticed that Tyler had stopped checking the table. He noticed that Chase had put the folded note on the shelf rather than in his notebook. He did not ask about any of it. He let the room be ordinary.

After breakfast, Andrew began clearing the plates. Luke reached for the same stack. “I've got these,” Andrew said.

“I know.”

“Then let me.”

Luke hesitated, then passed him the plates. It was not a significant sacrifice, but it was a different kind of trust. Andrew took them to the kitchen. Joseph followed with the breadboard. Tyler collected the tools. Chase put the old fastener in the labelled tray. Gabe stood for a moment beside the table and rested his hand on the repaired corner. The table did not move. He looked toward the others. “I like that it can be used again without pretending it was never damaged.” “That's the point of a repair,” Tyler said from the doorway.

“Sometimes,” Chase added, “the repair is the part worth remembering.”

Andrew called from the kitchen. “And sometimes the point is that we can have breakfast.” Luke laughed and went to help him. Joseph was already washing the board. Tyler returned to put the ruler away. Chase left the note on the shelf, where it could be found if the floor darkened again. He liked the table best when the others had stopped inspecting it. The repaired corner was easy to see if a person knew where to look, but the broad surface had returned to its usual work: holding plates, cups, notebooks, a folded map, and the small objects people put down while they were talking. Luke had moved the table back toward the centre of the room, though he left a little more space on the eastern side so that the longer bench could be pulled out without scraping the wall. The change was slight. The room felt different because the men had agreed on it rather than because anyone had announced a new arrangement. Gabe rested his forearms on the tabletop and watched Andrew carry the last basket of plates through the doorway. Andrew's shoulders had widened since the previous week's practice, and the sleeves of his shirt had climbed toward his upper arms. He walked with the loose confidence of a man who knew where every chair stood, but he turned sideways when he passed the narrow cupboard. The building was familiar to them, though their bodies were not staying still long enough for familiarity to mean that nothing had to be adjusted.

Joseph returned from the kitchen with a second pot of coffee and a plate of sliced pears. He set them at the centre of the table, then noticed that Tyler had left his ruler beside the breadboard. He moved the ruler to the windowsill before it could be covered in jam. Tyler saw him do it and gave a small nod. Their friendship often appeared in these quiet exchanges: Joseph knew which objects Tyler wanted kept in order, and Tyler knew which conversations Joseph preferred to finish without an interruption. Neither needed to praise the other for remembering. The room had become easier to share because its inhabitants had learned the small practical grammars of one another's lives.

**JOSEPH:** “We should keep this table here for supper.”

**TYLER:** “It still needs a second inspection after the room dries.”

**ANDREW:** “You can inspect it during supper.”

**TYLER:** “No.”

**ANDREW:** “You can inspect it before supper.”

**TYLER:** “That's more likely.”

Luke looked toward Gabe. “Do you want to stay?” Gabe had been about to leave. He looked at the doorway, then at the chair beside him. “Yeah. I can stay.” Luke did not ask why he had nearly gone. He pulled out the chair and sat across from him. The two men had once treated silence as something to be filled quickly, especially when one of them had noticed the other looking uncomfortable. Now they had become better at leaving a little room around a feeling. Gabe's shirt pulled across the chest when he leaned back, and Luke noticed the latest change in his shoulders. He wanted to say something, but he waited until Gabe looked up.

**LUKE:** “Your shirt's getting tight again.”

**GABE:** “I know.”

**LUKE:** “Want me to bring you the spare one from my room?”

**GABE:** “Not yet. I want to see how long this one lasts.”

Luke smiled. “All right.” There was affection in the exchange, but also a growing respect for each man's right to decide what his body meant. Gabe had enjoyed being noticed during his growth, and he still did. He simply wanted the pleasure to remain his rather than become a duty to supply the room with another event. Luke had learned to notice without turning every change into an announcement. His own body was growing too. When he reached for the coffee pot, the sleeve of his shirt moved over a broader shoulder and the fabric pulled against his upper back. Gabe saw it and smiled.

**GABE:** “You're not exactly staying the same.”

**LUKE:** “I didn't say I was.”

**GABE:** “Your doorframe's going to complain next.”

**LUKE:** “It already does.”

Andrew heard them and turned from the kitchen. “We should measure the doorframes.” Tyler closed his eyes briefly. “We are not measuring every doorframe today.”

“Not every one,” Andrew agreed. “Just the interesting ones.”

“That's all of them to you.”

“Now you're understanding the method.”

The laughter came easily. It did not conceal the work or turn it into a performance. The men could joke because the table had held, the food was ready, and the immediate problem had been answered. A little later, they brought the long bench from the wall and placed it along the window side. Its legs were thick oak, and the seat had been widened when the first residents began to outgrow the original chairs. It made a soft scraping sound across the boards, then settled with a low thump. Luke checked the feet. Andrew sat at one end. The bench held. Tyler pressed down at the other end and watched the joint. It held again.

**ANDREW:** “We should name it.”

**CHASE:** “It's a bench.”

**ANDREW:** “That's not a name.”

**CHASE:** “It's the correct description.”

**GABE:** “Call it the East Bench.”

**JOSEPH:** “That's not bad.”

**TYLER:** “It needs a maintenance label, not a title.”

Andrew looked offended. “It can have both.” Chase smiled and took the small card from the shelf. He wrote EAST BENCH on one side and the installation date on the other. Tyler added the load rating and the name of the workshop that had rebuilt it. The card was not a monument. It was there to tell the next person what the bench was, who had worked on it, and when it should be checked again. Andrew wanted to add a small drawing of a baseball. Chase allowed him to put it in the corner. Tyler objected that the baseball was not part of the bench's construction. Andrew replied that it was part of the bench's social history. Joseph said that was probably true. The argument ended with the drawing remaining.

The room filled through the afternoon. Two residents from the upper floor came down to collect books, then stayed to ask about the repaired table. One of them had grown enough that his old jacket no longer closed properly. Luke showed him where the tailoring station had left a measuring card. The man did not need a new jacket immediately, but he wanted to know whether the shoulders could be altered without changing the waist. Daniel, who had been passing through with a box of fittings, offered to look at the seams later. The conversation took place without anyone treating the man's new size as a public event. They talked about the garment, the fabric, the cost of replacing it, and whether the old jacket could be kept for work. The change was physical, but the decision belonged to the person who wore it.

Gabe watched this from the end of the table. He had begun to understand that being seen did not have to mean being singled out. A room could notice a change and continue to include the person who had changed. It could also fail, of course. Sometimes people stared too long, or spoke as if a man's earlier body had become a joke. But the table had become a place where the group could make a correction without humiliating anyone. That mattered more to him than he had expected. He looked at Luke, who was helping the visiting resident fold the measuring card, and felt a familiar warmth in his chest. Luke had not become less attentive as he grew. He had become more exact about where to direct the attention.

By the time the light moved off the window, the common room had returned to its usual sounds. Someone was reading in the alcove. Andrew was telling a story about the baseball field. Tyler was checking the repaired joint once more, though he had promised not to turn the afternoon into an inspection. Joseph had gone to refill the coffee. Chase was sorting the photographs on his camera, keeping the image of the repair beside the image of the old fastener. Gabe sat at the table and listened. The men were not required to share one subject. They could talk across one another, follow separate thoughts, or let a conversation pass without joining it. The room had become a place where company did not require constant agreement.

When the afternoon meal arrived, they used the table without testing it first. Plates moved across the surface, cups were passed, and the breadboard sat over the repair line without making anyone nervous. The East Bench held three men comfortably. The chairs held the others. Their different heights and proportions changed the way they sat: Luke's knees reached farther beneath the table; Gabe's shoulders filled the chair back; Tyler's longer hands made the cup appear smaller; Andrew leaned forward with the energy of a man ready to leave for the field; Chase sat with his camera beside him but did not reach for it; Joseph made room for the dish at the centre. It was a practical arrangement, not a tableau. The men ate, argued about the route to the old fence, and decided to walk it together after the rain had dried.

At the end of the meal, Tyler placed his notebook away. The table had passed the test. There would be another inspection after the next wet week, and the floor would need checking once the boards had dried fully. For now, the room could be used again. That was the most meaningful result. Nobody had to keep talking about the repair. They could return to their ordinary lives and let the table do its work. Gabe pulled out a chair and sat for another minute, not waiting for anyone to comment on the table or on him.

At the window, Joseph and Chase took a moment to look at the floor where the rain had entered. The towel Luke had placed there was damp now, and a faint line remained in the grain of the boards. Chase asked whether the old fastener should be placed beside the repair note or stored separately. Joseph said it should be stored where the next person could find it, but not displayed as if it were a trophy. Chase liked that distinction. He had begun to understand that an archive could preserve something without making the object perform its importance. He put the fastener into a shallow tray, wrote the date on a small card, and left space for the next inspection. Joseph watched him write, then asked whether he had kept the photograph of the first table arrangement.

“I kept it,” Chase said. “I haven't decided what it means yet.”

“That's all right.”

“Most people want the meaning first.”

“Most people don't have to keep the record.”

Chase smiled. “You make it sound like a difficult job.”

“It can be.”

Across the room, Gabe and Luke were moving the chairs into their usual places. Gabe carried two at once, his wider frame making the task easier than it had been the month before. Luke took the longer bench. Their movements were unhurried, and they did not need to discuss who should carry what. When the bench reached the wall, Gabe stepped aside so Luke could settle the far leg. Luke checked the floor, then looked up.

“Good?”

“Good.”

Gabe glanced at the repaired table. “It's strange how much a room changes when one thing stops wobbling.” Luke laughed. “You mean the table?”

“I mean the table.”

“Right.”

They both knew there was more in the sentence, but neither pressed it. The morning had been enough. They could return to the rest of the conversation another day, and the table would be there to hold whatever came next. The rain had left a mark beside the window. The joint had been repaired. The old fastener remained in its tray. The room had not returned to precisely the same arrangement, and nobody tried to make it.

Before anyone left the room, Chase returned the old fastener to its tray and wrote a note about the repaired corner. He did not label the table as newly restored. He called it serviceable, with a date for the next inspection. Joseph read the note and approved. The table would be used for supper, for letters, for the occasional board game, and for the small domestic arrangements that made the common room feel inhabited. The men had made no grand agreement about what the repair meant. They had simply learned that a shared object could carry its history without making everyone repeat it aloud. When Andrew came back from the kitchen with another loaf, he put it in the centre of the table as though the morning's work had never interrupted the meal. Tyler checked the brace once more, then joined them. The repaired table had returned to use, which was the best kind of ending it could have had.

By afternoon, the table had become ordinary again. That was the result they had wanted. Its history remained in the note, the measurement, the small piece of wood fitted into the socket, and the way six men had learned a little more about one another while making it usable.

**SETH:** The table has been repaired, and the men have resisted the urge to turn it into an allegory.
