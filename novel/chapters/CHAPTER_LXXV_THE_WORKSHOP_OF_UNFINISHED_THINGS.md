# Chapter LXXV — The Workshop of Unfinished Things

The first thing Andrew lost was the pencil. He had placed it behind his ear while carrying a length of timber from the outbuilding, then forgotten it was there and spent several minutes searching the workbench, the floor, and the pockets of his trousers. Tyler watched him without offering assistance. The workshop had been open for three days, and already its organisation was under pressure from the men who were using it. The tool register had been filled in, the benches had been assembled, and the first cabinet stood against the eastern wall. Yet every surface had acquired a new collection of objects: a measuring tape, a half-empty tin of screws, a folded drawing, two cups, a piece of sandpaper, and a small mechanical assembly whose purpose nobody had yet identified.

“You're wearing it,” Tyler said.

Andrew stopped searching. “What?”

“The pencil.”

Andrew reached behind his ear and found it. He looked at Tyler. “You could have said something earlier.”

“I wanted to see how long it would take.”

“That's not a useful experiment.”

“It produced a result.”

Luke came in carrying a crate of wood offcuts. “If you're finished testing one another, there's room for these beside the saw.”

Andrew took the crate. The workshop was acquiring its own rhythm, a combination of purposeful work and interruptions that gradually became part of the method. Nobody was expected to be good at everything. Tyler could measure and document with unusual precision, but he sometimes needed reminding that a drawing was not the same as a finished object. Andrew had strength and confidence, but he could move too quickly when a task required patience. Luke was good at noticing what a room needed before anybody had asked for it. Kirk had an instinct for the structure of an object and the way its separate parts depended on one another. Joseph could see how a finished piece might belong in the larger building, and he had begun to understand that this ability was most useful when he stayed close to the actual work.

The first man had given them a simple instruction: do not make the workshop a museum. The men had interpreted this as permission to use the room freely, but they were beginning to discover that freedom required maintenance. A tool left on the floor could injure somebody. A measurement not recorded could waste an afternoon. A piece of timber used without checking its condition could split under pressure. The workshop needed rules, though the rules had to remain in service of the work rather than becoming an institution that existed to preserve its own authority.

Tyler had therefore divided the room into working areas. The eastern wall held the main bench and hand tools. The northern end contained the saw and heavier equipment. The window table was reserved for small assemblies and models. The cabinet held fasteners, fittings, and labelled containers. A clear central aisle allowed long pieces of timber to pass through the room. The archive shelf stood near the entrance, where drawings and records could be consulted without interrupting the work. The arrangement was not perfect, but it was legible. Anyone entering the room could understand where to begin.

Joseph and Kirk were working on the cabinet. It had been designed to hold the small artifacts collected from earlier rooms in the House, but they had agreed that it should also accommodate objects whose meaning was not yet known. The drawers were different depths. Some had shallow compartments for keys, washers, fragments of paper, and small pieces of metal. Others were deeper, with removable dividers for larger objects. A narrow shelf at the top would hold temporary deposits: things brought in by somebody who wanted them recorded but had not decided whether they belonged in the archive.

“There's too much space in this drawer,” Joseph said.

Kirk checked the dimensions. “It's supposed to be deep enough for the wooden pieces.”

“Then the divider should move.”

“It can move.”

“Wouldn't it be better if it could be removed entirely?”

Kirk looked at him. “You want the cabinet to be adaptable.”

“I want it to remain useful when we don't know what we're going to put in it.”

Kirk considered the suggestion, then drew a new line on the plan. “That will work.”

Joseph smiled. The exchange was small, but he noticed that he was no longer waiting for Kirk to approve every idea before he allowed himself to develop it. Kirk, in turn, did not treat a disagreement as evidence that Joseph was ignoring him. They were finding a more ordinary way to collaborate. It was not effortless. It required them to listen, revise, and sometimes admit that the other person had seen something they had missed.

Nick arrived with a portable speaker and placed it on the window table. Seth followed with a notebook.

“We're recording the workshop,” Nick said.

Tyler looked at the speaker. “Why?”

“Because it's a place where things happen.”

“That describes most rooms.”

“Most rooms don't have Andrew trying to cut a straight line.”

Andrew looked up from the saw. “I can hear you.”

“That's part of the recording.”

Seth opened his notebook. “We want to capture the sound of the place as it develops. The tools, the voices, the pauses, the mistakes. Not a polished demonstration. Something that shows how the room actually works.”

Luke nodded. “Keep the microphone away from the dust.”

Nick moved the speaker. “There. Technical excellence.”

The recording began with the ordinary sounds of the workshop: a drawer sliding shut, the scrape of a chair, the measured tapping of a mallet, Andrew asking where the square had gone, and Tyler explaining that it was on the bench where Andrew had left it. The microphone caught the rhythm of the saw and the low hum of the ventilation system. It also caught a conversation between Joseph and Kirk about the drawer dividers, which Nick decided to leave in the recording because it revealed more about the work than a formal explanation would have done.

Seth listened through the headphones. “This is better than a narration.”

“Of course it is,” Nick said. “The room is doing the narration.”

The recording became the first entry in the workshop's sound archive. It was not music, though the men began to notice that the room had a rhythm of its own. Different tools produced different kinds of sound. The mallet gave a low, deliberate knock. The hand plane made a long, even whisper against the wood. The drill interrupted the room with a higher mechanical note. When several men worked at once, the sounds overlapped without becoming chaotic. There were periods of silence, too, when somebody was measuring or studying a joint. The recording preserved these intervals. It showed that the workshop was not simply a place where activity occurred. It was a place where attention moved between people and materials.

By the end of the week, the cabinet was nearly complete. The men had sanded the surfaces, fitted the drawers, and applied a protective finish. Kirk had insisted that the handles be simple and easy to replace. Joseph had added a narrow space for a written record of each deposited object. Tyler had prepared a catalogue with blank fields rather than invented categories. Luke had made a small padded tray for fragile items. Andrew had fitted the cabinet to the wall, making sure it could not tip when several drawers were open.

The first object placed inside was the brass washer. Joseph brought it down from his room and set it on the temporary deposit shelf. Kirk watched him.

“Are you putting it in the archive?”

“For now.”

“Why?”

“Because it belongs to the history of the House.”

Kirk considered this. “It belongs to our history too.”

“Yes.”

“Don't let the cabinet take it away from us.”

Joseph looked at him. “It won't.”

He wrote a short entry on a card: brass washer, recovered from the original chair assembly; retained by Kirk; deposited temporarily by Joseph. He left the date blank until Tyler could confirm the correct record format. The object was not assigned a grand title. It did not become a key to the House or a symbol that the men were required to understand. It remained a small piece of brass with a known history and a place in the cabinet. Kirk took it out again before they left the room. He put it in his pocket.

The first man arrived later that afternoon. He inspected the cabinet and the workbench, then looked at the recording equipment on the window table.

“You have made a room that can remember its own work,” he said.

Tyler nodded. “That's the intention.”

“Then remember the people as well as the objects.”

“We have names on the records.”

“Names are a beginning. Remember who repaired the drawer, who changed the plan, who found the timber, who noticed the light was wrong, and who came back the next day to finish something.”

Seth wrote the sentence in his notebook. Nick looked at the cabinet.

“That's a very long label.”

“It is not a label,” the first man replied.

The distinction stayed with them. The archive could record objects and events, but the workshop's continuity depended on the men who used it. A cabinet could survive its makers, but it could not preserve their judgment by itself. The next person needed to understand not only what had been made but why a particular choice had been made, what had failed, and what could be improved. The men began adding brief work notes to the records. They did not write elaborate accounts of every task. They recorded the decisions that might matter later.

As the room grew more useful, men from other parts of the House began visiting. Some brought broken objects. Others wanted help building shelves, repairing a chair, or making a frame for a drawing. One brought a model whose parts had been assembled in the wrong order. Tyler examined it, Andrew offered a solution, and Kirk discovered that the central piece had been reversed. They corrected it together. The model was returned to its owner with a note explaining the repair. Nobody treated the task as beneath them. A working institution was built from the willingness to do small things properly.

Joseph noticed that the workshop had begun to change the way the men spoke to one another. They asked for help more directly. They became more willing to show an unfinished attempt. A man could bring in something that did not work without being treated as incompetent. The others would examine it, identify the difficulty, and suggest a method. There was still pride, and sometimes irritation, but the room made it easier to separate a failed piece of work from the worth of the person who had made it. That was not a small change. It allowed the men to improve without needing to disguise every mistake.

Kirk was particularly good at identifying where an assembly had gone wrong. He could look at a joint and see the order in which the pieces had been fitted. He could recognise when pressure had been applied unevenly or when a measurement had been copied from the wrong edge. He explained these things without turning the lesson into a performance. Joseph watched him work with another man on a damaged chair. Kirk removed the loose joint, cleaned the surfaces, and showed the man how to refit it. He did not take over the task. He let the other man complete it.

When they were finished, Joseph asked him, “You enjoy teaching?”

Kirk wiped his hands. “I enjoy seeing somebody understand what they're doing.”

“That's different.”

“Yes.”

Joseph nodded. He understood that Kirk had a life and a set of interests that existed independently of their relationship. He wanted to know more about them, not because knowing would make Kirk easier to keep, but because they were part of the man he had chosen to be near. He asked whether Kirk would show him how to repair the chair joints properly. Kirk said he would, but warned him that the first attempt would probably be untidy.

“That's fine,” Joseph said.

“You say that now.”

“I'll say it tomorrow too.”

Kirk smiled. “We'll see.”

That evening, the workshop closed later than usual. The men had finished a set of repairs and were cleaning the benches. Tyler checked the tool register. Andrew swept the floor. Luke collected the cups and wiped the window table. Seth saved the sound recording. Nick made a copy for the archive and another for the House's shared listening room. Joseph and Kirk stood by the cabinet, checking that the drawers closed properly.

The room was not complete. There were still tools to acquire, shelves to build, and methods to improve. The cabinet would eventually need another drawer. The bench surface would require maintenance. The ventilation system was louder than Tyler liked. Andrew wanted a heavier vice. Luke wanted a better light above the small assembly table. The men had made a place that could accommodate these requests without needing to settle them all immediately.

The first man stood at the entrance. “What will you make next?”

Andrew looked around. “A better vice.”

Tyler said, “A second workbench.”

Luke suggested, “A proper storage rack.”

Nick wanted, “A recording booth.”

Seth looked at him. “Not in this room.”

Joseph glanced at Kirk. “A chair.”

Kirk raised an eyebrow. “For whom?”

“Anyone who needs one.”

The first man nodded. “Then you have understood the room.”

They switched off the lights and left the workshop. In the corridor, Joseph and Kirk walked together. Their shoulders did not touch, but neither had moved away. The brass washer was in Kirk's pocket. The cabinet held the record of its temporary deposit. The two facts could coexist without competing.

At the end of the corridor, Kirk stopped.

“Are you coming to breakfast tomorrow?”

Joseph looked at him. “Yes.”

“Good. I want to start the chair.”

“I'll be there.”

Kirk continued toward the stairs. Joseph watched him go, then turned toward the kitchen to help Luke with the remaining dishes. The House had become a place where people could make something, put it down, and return to it later. The unfinished things were not failures. They were invitations to continue.

In the workshop, the last recording remained saved on the archive drive. It held the sound of the men working, talking, correcting one another, and laughing. It was not a record of a perfect day. It was a record of a room beginning to belong to the people who used it. Tomorrow there would be another recording, perhaps another repair, another argument about measurements, and another object that needed a place in the cabinet.

The work would continue because the men had made a place for it to continue.
