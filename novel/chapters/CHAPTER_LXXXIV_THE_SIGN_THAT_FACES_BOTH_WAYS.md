# Chapter LXXXIV — The Sign That Faces Both Ways

The temporary arrow at the Redwood junction had survived its first night. It was a simple board on a weighted stand, set just before the path divided, with a dark blue arrow pointing back toward the Athletic Commons reception desk. The facilities crew had placed it where the manager requested, then returned before opening to check that it had not shifted in the rain. It was not the finished sign, and nobody in the Commons treated it as one. The permanent panel still needed a drawing, approval, a mounting detail, and a check from both directions. The floor marking was still a trial. The bench clearance had been measured, but the furniture had not yet been moved. The small incident had produced a list of ordinary work, and the ordinary work had to be completed in the right order.

Tyler arrived with the revised drawing rolled beneath his arm. He had added the reception desk as a return point, but he had also changed the shape of the map. His first version had shown the buildings as a compact plan. The second showed the sequence a visitor actually encountered: pool entrance, café terrace, glass doors, Redwood junction, and the path back. It was less elegant as a campus diagram and more useful as a walking instruction. A visitor did not need to understand the whole grounds. He needed to recognize the next decision before he reached it.

Andrew was waiting beside the temporary stand with a small recorder in his hand. He had brought it to test the sound of the floor marking, though no one had approved sound as a navigation method. He had placed two narrow sample strips on a dry section of the covered passage and was walking over them in different shoes. The first strip made almost no sound. The second produced a faint change beneath the heel. Andrew listened with the seriousness of a man evaluating an instrument.

“It has a little click,” he said when Tyler came over.

“It is a floor strip,” Tyler replied.

“It has a little click.”

“Does the click identify the direction?”

“No.”

“Then it is not a directional feature.”

Andrew looked down at the strip. “It could still be a useful secondary observation.”

“It could. But the sign has to work without it.”

Andrew put the recorder away. He was not offended. He liked the moment when an idea was narrowed enough to become testable. The sound could remain in his notes as an observation, not a claim that the path had acquired a voice. He moved the recorder to his pocket and helped Tyler hold the drawing flat against the wooden bench.

Dorian came from the west court with Gabe and Luke. He had chosen a loose charcoal shirt that gave his shoulders more room than the blue travelling shirt, though its fabric still followed the breadth of his chest and upper back. His arms hung naturally at his sides, but the width of his frame made the passage look narrower when he entered it. He noticed the temporary board first, then the floor samples, then the drawing on the bench. He had become accustomed to looking at a building as a set of relationships between fixed measurements and moving bodies. The first thing he now checked was not whether he could pass through a space, but whether the space remained usable when other people were moving through it too.

Gabe was carrying two coffees. He handed one to Dorian and kept the other for himself. It was a small exchange, but it was no longer the careful, uncertain gesture of their first meeting. They had spoken twice since the western inspection, once about the bridge report and once about the route between the dormitory and the gym. Neither had tried to force familiarity. Gabe had discovered that Dorian did not need to be addressed as a monument, and Dorian had discovered that Gabe could be funny when he was not busy measuring himself against someone else. There was still some reserve between them, but it had become an ordinary reserve rather than a contest.

“Is that the permanent version?” Dorian asked, looking at the drawing.

“Not yet,” Tyler said. “It is the first version that shows the return route clearly. We need to test the sightline before it goes to facilities.”

Dorian stepped to one side so that he would not block the path. “From here?”

“From here, and from the other side. We also need to see what it looks like at the height of someone who isn't already looking for it.”

Luke studied the drawing. “The map is smaller than the one at reception.”

“It should be,” Tyler said. “It is answering a smaller question.”

Gabe pointed to the arrow. “The old one tells you where you can go. This one tells you how to come back.”

“That is the point,” Tyler said. “A first-time visitor needs both.”

Joseph arrived with the Commons manager and a facilities technician. He had asked that the review take place before the lunchtime rush, when the main entrance would be busy enough to reveal circulation problems but not so crowded that the team would obstruct people. The manager carried a folder containing the temporary incident note, the bench measurement, and the list of requested changes. No personal details from the young man's report appeared in the folder used for the public facilities review. The manager had kept the restricted record separate, as the procedure required.

They began at the reception desk. Tyler stood where a visitor would first see the glass doors and held up a paper mock-up at the proposed sign height. Chase walked to the outer path with his camera but waited until the manager confirmed that the approach was clear. He took a photograph from the point of view of a person entering the building, then another from the path looking inward. The first image showed the sign against the bright reflection of the pool windows. The arrow was legible, but the words beneath it were too small. The second image showed that the map could be read from the path, though the reception symbol needed more contrast.

“People will look at the arrow before they read the text,” Chase said. “The arrow is clear. The small words disappear.”

Tyler adjusted the layout. He enlarged the return-point symbol and removed two labels that did not help someone choose a direction. The map became simpler. The manager preferred it immediately, but Tyler asked to keep the decision provisional until it had been viewed from the opposite side and under the afternoon light. He did not want a sign approved because the room in which it had been drawn was bright and quiet.

Dorian stood at the edge of the glass entrance while the others worked. His reflection appeared faintly over the blue pool windows, layered with the moving water and the pale walls of the hall. The broad outline of his shoulders made the reflected doorway seem smaller than it was. He watched a group of men come through with gym bags, then move toward the changing rooms without consulting the map. They knew the building. A visitor would not. That distinction had been at the centre of the earlier incident, and it was now visible in the ordinary movement of the Commons.

A voice behind them said, “That's the man.”

Dorian turned. The father from the Redwood incident had entered with his son. the young man was wearing a blue sweatshirt and carrying the same small backpack. He saw Dorian at once and raised his hand. Dorian returned the greeting with a nod, keeping his position beside the entrance rather than approaching before the father had a chance to decide whether to stop.

The father came forward. “We came back for swimming. He wanted to show me where he met you.”

the young man stood beside him, looking first at Dorian and then at the temporary arrow. “It's there now,” he said.

“It is,” Dorian replied. “The staff put it in after your visit.”

The father read the sign and looked toward the path. “That would have helped.”

“It should help,” Tyler said, joining them. “We're checking the permanent version today. The temporary one is only there while we make sure the placement works.”

The father nodded. “Would it be useful to know where we got confused?”

“Yes,” Joseph said. “Only if you have time. We don't need the details of the incident again. We're trying to understand what the signs should show.”

The father explained that he had entered through the pool side and assumed the Redwood Walk would bring him back toward the car park. the young man had remembered seeing trees through the glass and thought the path might be a shortcut. The difficulty was not that he had ignored a clear instruction. The map at the entrance showed the grounds in a way that made sense to regular users, but it did not show the relationship between the pool doors and the return point. The father had gone looking in one direction, the young man had gone in another, and each had believed the other was nearby.

Tyler listened without interrupting. He changed one label on the drawing from “Main Building” to “Reception / Help Desk.” Joseph suggested that the sign should include a small instruction: “Need help? Return to reception.” The manager agreed, though he wanted the wording checked for clarity and translated into the additional languages commonly used by campus visitors. the young man looked at the paper and asked whether the arrow meant that the reception desk was behind him.

“It does,” Tyler said. He turned the mock-up so that the young man could see it from the path. “When you're standing here, the arrow points back through those doors.”

the young man followed the arrow with his eyes, then turned and looked at the glass entrance. “So I go that way.”

“That's right.”

“And if I come from the pool?”

“Then the sign needs to face you too,” Tyler said.

the young man looked at the opposite side of the board. “This side is blank.”

Andrew smiled. “That's what we're fixing.”

The father laughed, then looked at the facilities technician. “He has a point.”

“He does,” the technician said. “The permanent panel can be double-sided. We were already considering that, but the sightline test will tell us whether the mounting position works.”

Gabe had been watching the young man study the sign. He leaned toward Dorian. “He noticed the thing we missed.”

Dorian looked at the empty back of the temporary board. “He was the person who needed it.”

There was no accusation in the answer. Gabe heard the distinction. A committee could produce a complicated map and still miss the question that mattered to the person standing at the junction. the young man did not need an account of the entire campus. He needed to know whether the arrow pointed toward the place where his father could be found.

The manager asked the father whether he would be comfortable walking the route with his son while the team watched the sign from a distance. The father agreed. He kept the young man beside him, and they began at the pool entrance. Tyler remained near the temporary board with the revised mock-up. Joseph watched the flow of people through the doors. Chase took photographs only of the empty route and the sign, never of the family. Dorian stayed near the reception side, where the young man could see him if he chose to look back.

The father and son walked toward the Redwood path. At the junction, the young man stopped before the temporary arrow and turned his head. The board was easy to see from the pool side, but the arrow was not quite aligned with the doors. the young man pointed toward the glass wall rather than the reception desk. Tyler saw it and marked the angle on the drawing.

“That's the problem,” he said. “The arrow is technically pointing toward the building, but it isn't pointing toward the entrance.”

The facilities technician moved the paper mock-up a few degrees. the young man looked again. This time the arrow led directly toward the doors. He turned and began walking back. His father followed, and they reached the reception desk without hesitation.

The test was repeated from the opposite direction. The temporary board had no printed message on its rear face, so the team held the second mock-up beside it. The return point was now visible from both directions, but the map itself needed to be mirrored rather than merely repeated. Tyler had not wanted to assume that a double-sided sign could use the same orientation on both faces. The technician confirmed that the panel could be printed on each side with the correct directional relationship, and that the mounting bracket could be turned without weakening the support. The design would need a formal drawing and a check of the fixing points before manufacture.

While the technician measured the post, the father thanked the team for making the change. He said that the young man had been reluctant to visit again at first, not because he disliked the Commons, but because he remembered how frightening it had been to lose sight of him. the young man had changed his mind when he heard that the new arrow was being made. He wanted to see whether it would help someone else.

Dorian listened. “It's good that you came back,” he said. “You don't have to use the path today if you don't want to.”

“I want to,” the young man replied. “With Dad.”

“That's a good way to do it.”

The father looked at Dorian. “He talks about you as the very large man who didn't make him feel silly.”

Dorian turned toward the young man. the young man had begun examining the seam along his own backpack strap. “You weren't silly,” Dorian said. “You were trying to find your dad. That was a reasonable thing to want.”

the young man looked up and smiled. It was a small, unguarded expression. Dorian did not try to extend the moment into a promise or a special arrangement. The father thanked him, and the family continued toward the pool. the young man looked back once from the glass doors and waved. Dorian raised his hand in return.

The facilities review continued after they had gone. Tyler's revised drawing now showed the sign as a two-sided panel, with the reception symbol enlarged, the return arrow aligned to the actual entrance, and the instruction placed where it could be read at ordinary walking speed. The technician recorded the proposed fixing height and bracket dimensions. Chase attached the sightline photographs to the work file, with the family excluded from the images. Joseph wrote the operational note: the permanent sign must be legible from both approaches, must not obscure the existing campus map, and must direct a visitor to a staffed return point rather than merely toward a building. The floor strip remained a secondary trial. Andrew could keep testing the sound, but no one would depend on it.

The bench clearance was measured next. The bench was not in itself a hazard, but a display stand had been placed too close to one end, narrowing the space where a person could stop with a bag or wait beside a younger visitor. The manager had already agreed to move the display. Luke checked the resulting gap with the facilities technician and confirmed that a wheelchair, a mobility aid, or two people standing side by side could use the space without blocking the doors. The measurement was added to the work order. It was not enough to make a space accessible on paper; the route had to remain clear after the café opened, after a delivery arrived, and after the evening crowd began to gather.

Gabe helped move the display stand. It was lighter than he expected, and he carried it to the wall without dragging its feet across the floor. Dorian lifted the other end of the bench just enough for the technician to place a protective pad beneath it, then lowered it carefully. They worked without drawing a crowd. The jobs were small, but they changed the entrance in a way that would matter to the next visitor. The bench could now be approached from either side. The doors opened freely. The sign could be read without standing in the stream of people entering the pool.

When the review was finished, Joseph gathered the men near the reception desk. The permanent sign was not yet approved, and the floor marking had not passed its durability test. The temporary arrow would remain for the week. Facilities would produce the double-sided drawing, the manager would check the wording, and the team would return at two different times of day to confirm that the panel remained visible in changing light. The bench clearance had been completed and recorded. The display stand had been moved. The work order would stay open until the manufactured sign had been installed and its two approaches checked.

Andrew asked whether the completed sign could have a small sound marker built into the post. Tyler looked at him.

“No.”

“I hadn't finished.”

“You were going to say a bell.”

“A very small bell.”

“The sign is not a musical instrument.”

“It could be both.”

Gabe laughed. Dorian looked at the post and considered it for a moment. “A bell would make people look for the bell,” he said. “The arrow should be enough.”

Andrew accepted the answer more readily than he might have a week before. “All right. No bell.”

They left the reception area together. the young man and his father were somewhere inside the pool complex now, following the ordinary route they had intended to take on their first visit. The new sign would not be finished by the time they left, but the temporary arrow was clearer, and the doorway was easier to see. The father had been able to return without having to explain the whole story. the young man had been able to test the sign without being made the subject of another report. The campus had learned something from the event without making the family carry the burden of teaching it.

On the walk back to East Dormitory, Gabe fell into step beside Dorian. Their shoulders were nearly level with the upper rail of the covered passage, though Dorian still occupied more of the available width. His shirt pulled slightly across the back when he reached to hold the door for Tyler. Gabe noticed the movement, then noticed that Dorian had noticed him noticing.

“You're still getting bigger,” Gabe said.

“Yes.”

“Does it ever stop being strange?”

Dorian took a moment before answering. “The measurements become familiar. The consequences keep changing.”

Gabe nodded. “That's a good answer.”

“It isn't always a comfortable one.”

They walked a little farther. The path opened beside the cedar landing, where the trees made the light seem darker even at midday. Gabe asked whether Dorian had always been careful around younger visitors. Dorian said that he had learned to be careful around people who did not know him, because his size could make a simple approach feel like a sudden event. Younger visitors were more direct about showing when they were frightened, but that did not make the responsibility belong to them. He tried to give people room to decide whether they wanted him closer.

Gabe thought about the first time he had seen Dorian at the western bridge. He had mistaken the attention around the giant for a judgement about himself. He had assumed that being noticed was a resource another man could take away. The Redwood incident had shown him something different. Dorian could be the largest person in a room and still choose not to dominate it. He could be noticed without asking for admiration. He could leave the space around another person intact.

“I think I understand you a little better,” Gabe said.

Dorian looked across at him. “I think I understand you a little better too.”

Neither of them tried to define the friendship. They continued toward the residence buildings, where the others had already begun arguing about lunch. Joseph wanted a quiet table. Andrew wanted to visit the workshop and check the sound sample. Tyler wanted the drawing finished before the facilities office closed. Chase wanted to catalogue the photographs and remove the duplicates. Luke asked whether anyone had remembered to bring the western report back from the reception folder. The day had divided into tasks again, each one small enough to complete and connected enough to change the next.

At lunch, the men sat around the long table beneath the south windows. The conversation moved from the sign to the western bridge, then to a proposed meal at the café and a repair request for the dormitory laundry. Gabe sat beside Dorian without making a point of it. Their chairs were wide, the table had enough clearance beneath it, and the repaired oak surface held the weight of the dishes without complaint. The men had become accustomed to accommodating their changing size, but accommodation was no longer only a matter of furniture and doorways. It was also a practice of attention: noticing what a person needed, asking before deciding, and allowing the answer to change the plan.

Dorian continued to grow, and the Commons continued to measure the spaces around him. The building had not learned to prevent every emergency. It had learned to make help easier to find.
