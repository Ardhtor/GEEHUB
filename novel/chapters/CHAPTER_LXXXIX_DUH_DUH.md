# Chapter LXXXIX — Duh Duh!

The following Sunday began without a drawing in sight. Dorian had made a point of leaving the survey folder in his room, and when Gabe met him beneath the cedar arcade he checked both of Dorian's hands before accepting the coffee. The inspection was so deliberate that Dorian held his palms out, empty, like a man being searched at a very polite entrance.

“Nothing,” Dorian said.

“No folded plan in your jacket?”

“No.”

“No measurements hidden in the coffee?”

“I didn't put any in.”

Gabe looked at the lids, then at him. “Duh.”

“Duh.”

They walked past the Athletic Commons and took the covered passage toward the older part of Northgate, where the university's print room occupied a long timber building joined to the map library by a glass corridor. The room had once handled course packets, exhibition posters, and the small publications made by student societies. Its high windows admitted a cool, diffuse light, and the old presses stood along the western wall like a row of quiet machines waiting for a reason to move. A broad central table had been cleared, the paper cabinets had been labelled, and a set of rolling boards offered a place to arrange images and pages without fixing them permanently. The smell of cedar shelving, paper dust, ink, and the faint warm-metal scent of the binding equipment made the room feel different from the clean administrative offices nearby. It had the settled atmosphere of a place where an idea could become an object.

Gabe had suggested it the evening before. He knew the print room supervisor, and he had been thinking about how much of their recent work existed in separate places: a drawing in the survey office, notes in someone's pocket, a chapter in the manuscript, a photograph in a source folder, and a conversation that could be remembered only by the men who had heard it. He did not want to turn their Sunday into an administrative meeting. He wanted to find somewhere they could make something together and leave it where the next person could see it. The print room was already part of Northgate, already connected to the map library and the workshops, and already equipped for paper, binding, layout, and practical display. It was not a new institution. It was a local point of production waiting to be used again.

Dorian looked across the broad table. “This is better than another café.”

“It's warmer than the survey office.”

“And nobody can ask us about the bridge.”

“Someone will. They always do.”

“But we don't have to bring it.”

Gabe smiled and set down the paper bag he had carried from the bakery. “That's the agreement.”

A man was already standing beside the paper cabinet, examining a sample sheet against the window. He was broad through the chest and shoulders, with a thick, athletic build that looked entirely at ease in the room. His open overshirt revealed a densely hairy chest, the dark growth curling below the collar and spreading across the upper line of his pectorals. The morning air had followed him in through the glass corridor; beneath the thin fabric, his nipples were lively and plainly responsive to the coolness, giving his otherwise relaxed appearance a little unselfconscious animation. He did not fuss with the shirt or try to conceal himself. He held the paper up to the light, turned it, and then caught sight of Gabe.

“There he is.”

Gabe raised a hand. “Tyler.”

“Which one?” Dorian asked.

The man laughed. “That's going to be a problem, isn't it?”

“Apparently,” Gabe said. “Dorian, this is Tyler Boeskin. Boeskin, Dorian Vale.”

Boeskin put the paper down and came around the table. His handshake was warm and direct. “I've heard about you.”

“Good things, I hope.”

“Mostly accurate things. The size gets mentioned a lot.”

Dorian smiled. “It does that.”

“I'm not complaining. It makes introductions easier. People don't have to ask which Dorian.”

Gabe gave Boeskin a look. “You were meant to be helping with the print room.”

“I am helping. I'm identifying the people who have arrived.”

Boeskin had the easy manner of a man who could enter a room without asking it to make space for him, but who noticed when somebody else needed some. He had been using the print room for team notices and event posters, and he knew which shelves still held good stock, which binding tools needed a replacement blade, and which old press was worth preserving. He was not the Tyler who worked with measurements and evidence on the western crossing. The distinction was obvious once the two men were in the same room: the first Tyler inspected a result until its limits became clear; Boeskin made people comfortable enough to start. He was happy to be called Boeskin, and within ten minutes the name had settled.

“You two are working on something?” Boeskin asked.

“Trying to,” Gabe said. “We want a place where a chapter, its notes, the maps, and the images can meet before they get separated again.”

“Then stop standing in the doorway.”

He pushed the central table's lower shelf open with his foot and began moving the boxes beneath it. There were paper weights, clips, a cutting mat, rolls of drafting film, a small speaker, and several empty archival trays. He cleared one section for current work and another for material that had been reviewed but not yet accepted. On the long wall he positioned two rolling boards: one for the current sequence, the other for the open questions that should not be settled merely because a page needed to end. He found a clean card for the top of each board and wrote in large block letters: CURRENT WORK and STILL OPEN.

Gabe watched him. “You had this in mind already.”

“I had a table in mind. You supplied the rest.”

Dorian moved one of the larger boards closer to the window. The wheels rolled smoothly, but the board's top edge sat too low for him to read comfortably while standing. He adjusted it, then checked that the base remained stable. Boeskin noticed and fetched the taller mounting brackets without being asked. The three of them made small changes until the room had a clear route through its centre and the table could be approached from all sides. Nothing was built around Dorian alone; the arrangement simply made the work usable for men of different heights and reach.

“That's the thing,” Gabe said as he looked over the room. “We don't need another platform that promises to produce everything. We need one place where the actual work can be handled.”

Boeskin nodded. “The files can live where files live. The room is for seeing what belongs together.”

“And for noticing what is missing,” Dorian said.

“Exactly. If a page says there's a map, the map should be on the board. If a character changes, the earlier version shouldn't disappear. If somebody makes a good suggestion, give him credit. And if something's only an idea, don't print it like it's fact.”

Gabe looked at Dorian. “We have found our editor.”

“I thought we were finding breakfast,” Dorian said.

“You can have both.”

Boeskin opened the bakery bag and found three pastries. He took one, offered the others, and leaned against the table while they ate. His shirt remained open at the throat, the chest hair plainly visible whenever he moved, and the cold air in the room gave his nipples an animated prominence beneath the thin cotton. He made no effort to turn it into a performance. When Gabe's eyes briefly registered the detail, Boeskin caught the look and grinned.

“Cold room,” he said.

“Very technical explanation,” Gabe replied.

“It's an engineering campus. Everything gets an explanation.”

Dorian laughed. The humour made the first half-hour easy. Boeskin was not introduced as somebody who needed to earn permission to exist among them. He was already a mate of Gabe's, and by the time the food was finished he had become a welcome presence at the table. His body was striking, but he did not spend the morning asking anyone to confirm it. He could accept a compliment, return one, and turn back to the practical business at hand without letting admiration become the only thing available to discuss.

The first material they laid out was not a new chapter. It was a sequence of six printed cards: the Redwood entrance, the two-sided sign, the western crossing, the Evening of Presence, the first dinner at Il Ponte, and the Sunday breakfast that had ended with the second kiss. Each card carried a date, a location, the people involved, and one sentence describing what had changed. Gabe had made the sequence deliberately simple. He wanted a visitor to understand the order without reading the entire manuscript, and he wanted a writer to see at once which details were supported by the record and which remained open.

Boeskin read the cards carefully. “The bridge is still closed?”

“Yes,” Dorian said. “The drainage, joint, and restraint work have to be completed and reviewed.”

“Then the card should say closed. Don't write ‘awaiting completion’ if it needs a barrier. Somebody will read the softer phrase as permission.”

Gabe picked up the pencil. “That's right.”

“I'm not the other Tyler,” Boeskin added. “But I know what happens when a sign sounds friendlier than the actual rule.”

Dorian nodded. “That's a useful distinction.”

They revised the card. The correction was small, but the whole row became clearer. The first five cards showed the route from an emergency handled properly to a growing friendship and then a deliberate attraction. The sixth showed Dorian and Gabe spending time together without needing a public announcement. Nothing had become a formal partnership; nothing needed to be hidden. The new point of production would preserve the sequence as it stood, while leaving the next page free to become something else.

Boeskin studied the card about the Evening of Presence. “You have a lot of men here who like being looked at.”

“Some do,” Gabe said.

“Some like looking,” Dorian added.

“And some like getting on with their day.”

“All three belong,” Gabe said.

Boeskin gave a satisfied nod. “Good. A room gets boring when it only has one kind of man in it.”

His own interest in masculine presentation was obvious. He was comfortable in his build, enjoyed clothes that fitted properly, and appreciated a man who could stand well without constantly checking whether the room had noticed. He liked humour and physical confidence, but he was not interested in ranking every man who entered. He had spent enough time around athletic groups to know that a loud opinion could fill a room while saying very little. The best company, in his view, was the company that could appreciate a man's appearance and still remember what he had come there to do.

Gabe asked him how he had come to know the print room so well. Boeskin described making posters for a local strength club, then helping the map library with a large-format display for a campus open day. He liked the material side of production: choosing the paper, testing the ink, aligning the image, making sure a page could be handled without falling apart. He had learned to use the binding equipment from the supervisor, and he preferred a physical mock-up to an abstract promise that a layout would work. He did not claim to be a designer. He knew how to make a piece of work usable, and he knew which people to ask when the job required a specialist.

“That's exactly what we need,” Gabe said.

“Don't make me director of anything.”

“Not even the pastries?”

“Especially not the pastries.”

They laughed. Across the room, the old press made a brief mechanical sound as the supervisor tested its rollers. The sound travelled through the timber floor and stopped. Outside, the cedar branches moved against the high windows. The print room seemed to hold a different kind of quiet from the library: less silence, more expectation. There were machines here, and paper, and enough open table for a thought to become a thing that another person could pick up.

Dorian and Gabe moved to the wall board. They had agreed that the next Sunday would be free of engineering drawings, but that did not mean they had to avoid making something. Gabe pinned a blank page beneath the six cards and wrote the title at the top: DUH DUH! It looked almost too large in his handwriting. Dorian stood beside him, his shoulder just behind Gabe's, and read the words.

“Two of them,” Dorian said.

“One wasn't enough.”

“Apparently.”

Gabe turned, and they were close enough that the space between them felt less like distance than a choice. Boeskin noticed, looked briefly toward the paper cabinet, and began checking the binding clips with exaggerated concentration. He was friendly, but he was not going to turn somebody else's private moment into a performance.

Dorian lowered his voice. “You want this one on the wall?”

“I do.”

“Then it's staying.”

Gabe leaned in and kissed him. It was familiar enough now that neither had to decide what to do with his hands, but still new enough to make the room briefly recede. Dorian's palm rested at Gabe's waist. Gabe touched the front of Dorian's jacket. When they separated, the title remained pinned above them, a small piece of evidence that an ordinary afternoon had acquired its own name.

Boeskin returned with a bundle of clips. “Should I put a little frame around that?”

Gabe looked at him. “You really can't help yourself.”

“I could make it tasteful.”

“Duh.”

“Duh duh,” Boeskin answered, smiling.

The phrase took hold at once. It was not a private code that excluded everyone else; it was a small joke that had become a shared sound. Dorian repeated it, and Boeskin put the clips down with a laugh. For a moment the three men stood around the wall board, looking at the title as though it had been a much more serious production decision than it was.

Then they worked. The print room supervisor showed them how to make a small test run of the six cards. Gabe adjusted the type size, Dorian checked that the sequence could be read from both sides of the table, and Boeskin tested the paper stock against the binding rings. They did not print the entire manuscript. They made a six-card pilot and a single title sheet, enough to see whether the local arrangement could support the work. The test sheets came out clean. The edges aligned, the text remained legible, and the cards could be moved without losing their order. The supervisor recommended a heavier stock for the final display and showed them where to store the reusable boards.

The physical pieces did not replace the digital record. They made it easier to see. The manuscript remained in the repository, and the cards pointed back to the exact chapters and records that supported each event. An idea not yet accepted could be pinned to the open-questions board without being mistaken for established history. If a page changed, the date and source could be checked. The print room was the local point where a person could stand among the work, compare its parts, and decide what should happen next. It was a production space, not a second canon.

Seth and Nick were watching from the gallery above the corridor. The upper windows looked down across the timber room, the long table, and the three men gathering their first printed set. The old presses stood in shadow behind them, while the window light moved across the paper and made its surface appear almost silver.

**SETH:**

There it is. Not another grand machine announced before anyone can use it. A room, a table, a few working boards, and three men who know how to put a thing in front of them.

**NICK:**

And Boeskin has turned up with enough personality for the whole place. That's a good mate. He can appreciate a body, appreciate a joke, and still remember the bridge is closed.

**SETH:**

The other Tyler will want that distinction written down.

**NICK:**

It is. One Tyler checks the measurement. Boeskin gets the room moving.

When the test run was finished, Gabe stacked the six cards in their proper order and placed the title sheet on top. Dorian checked the shelf where the current work would remain. Boeskin wrote a small note on the storage label: CURRENT SEQUENCE — REVIEW BEFORE REPRINT. Beneath it he added the date and the names of the three men who had handled the first set. It was a practical record, neither grand nor temporary.

They left the print room together. The evening had cooled, and the cedar corridor held the smell of damp wood from the morning rain. Boeskin walked with them as far as the Athletic Commons, talking about a poster he wanted to make for the next strength-club evening. He invited them both to come by and offered to show Dorian the reinforced paper stock he used for large-format prints. Dorian accepted the invitation. Gabe said he would come if Boeskin promised not to appoint him to a committee.

“No promises,” Boeskin said. “You're already on the pastry committee.”

“That's not a real committee.”

“It's the most important one.”

At the doors, Boeskin gave Gabe a friendly shoulder bump and shook Dorian's hand again. “Good to meet you properly.”

“You too,” Dorian said.

Boeskin headed toward the gym, his open shirt shifting as he walked, chest hair still visible above the dark fabric. He turned once to call back, “Duh duh!” and disappeared through the doors laughing at his own joke.

Gabe watched him go. “He's a good mate.”

“He is.”

“You like him.”

“I do.”

“Good. I want you to know my friends.”

Dorian turned toward him. “I want that too.”

They took the longer way back through the cedar arcade. Their pace was unhurried, and neither made a point of filling the quiet. Near the end of the passage, Gabe slipped his hand into Dorian's. They had spent the day building a small local production point, but the thing that mattered most was not the board or the printed cards. It was that Gabe had brought someone into his ordinary life and watched Dorian welcome him without treating the introduction as a test. The relationship had widened by one friend, not been reduced to a private pair.

At the entrance to East Dormitory, Gabe stopped. “Next time, we should make something that isn't about us.”

“Agreed.”

“Something for the campus.”

“Let's ask Boeskin what needs printing.”

Gabe smiled. “Duh.”

“Duh duh.”

Their kiss was brief, warm, and entirely unhurried. Then Gabe went inside, and Dorian continued toward his rooms. On the wall of the print room, the six cards remained in order beneath the new title. The bridge was still closed. The relationship was still taking shape. Tyler Boeskin had become part of the circle. And Northgate had acquired a place where the work could be handled nearby, with its sources visible and its next movement still open.
