# Chapter LXXXI — Room for Quiet and the Dormitory Decathlon

The next morning began with a noise that sounded, to Gabe, like a very large man trying to fold a wardrobe in half. It came from the bed frame, somewhere beneath the mattress and just beyond the reach of his hand, and it repeated whenever he shifted his weight. The room was still cool from the night. Rain had left a silver film on the window, and the early light fell through it in a long rectangular patch across the oak floorboards. His old training shirt lay over the back of the desk chair, its shoulder seams stretched into pale ridges; on the wardrobe door, the small mirror showed a man whose frame had become broader again during the night, with heavier arms, a deeper chest, and a posture that seemed to have acquired another degree of height. He sat on the edge of the mattress, listened to the frame complain, then laughed to himself. The building had been making accommodations for him for weeks, and now the bed had decided to submit its own formal objection.

He was still laughing when Luke knocked. The knock was gentle but unmistakable, three firm taps followed by the slight pause Luke used when he wanted to be heard without assuming that the door would open. Gabe looked at the frame, then at the door, and called him in. Luke entered carrying a tape measure and a folded sheet of paper. He had already changed into a dark sleeveless shirt and loose training shorts, and the upper part of his frame filled the doorway in a way that had become increasingly difficult to overlook. His shoulders were wider than the week before; the shirt sat close across his back, and his arms had acquired a fuller weight that changed the angle of his elbows when he walked. He looked at Gabe first, then at the bed, then back at Gabe. He did not begin dismantling anything.

**LUKE:** “Is it the bed, or are you trying to get the room to applaud?”

**GABE:** “The bed has a strong opinion about me.”

Luke smiled and set the tape measure on the desk. The exchange was their usual way of approaching a problem: a joke first, then the actual question, then the practical work. Luke had learned that Gabe did not always want a problem solved at the speed of the first available tool. Sometimes he wanted a witness. Sometimes he wanted help. Sometimes he wanted both, but in that order. Gabe had learned that Luke could be trusted to remain useful without making himself the hero of the repair. Their friendship had become more comfortable as their bodies became less so. The room itself made the point: the bed was too short, the desk chair was becoming narrow, the wardrobe rail sat lower against Gabe's hand than it once had, and the old training shirt looked like something borrowed from a smaller version of a familiar man.

Gabe stood. The mattress released a long, relieved creak. His feet settled flat on the boards, and he stretched both arms overhead. The movement drew the fabric of his current shirt across his chest and shoulders; the seams tightened, then relaxed as he lowered his elbows. He had gained again, not in one sudden leap but through the slow physical accumulation that had become part of his mornings. His chest sat farther forward, his shoulders spread another fraction, and his thighs pressed more heavily against the loose shorts. He rolled one shoulder and felt the familiar warmth of a body finding its new resting position. Luke watched long enough to understand the change, then looked down at the frame.

**LUKE:** “Do you want me to measure the bed first, or do you want me to help move it?”

**GABE:** “Measure it. Then tell me what you think. Don't just decide for me.”

**LUKE:** “All right.”

Luke measured the mattress, the inner frame, the distance from the headboard to the wall, and the clearance beside the wardrobe. Gabe held the opposite end of the tape. Their hands met briefly at the metal hook, and Luke shifted the tape so Gabe could keep a steady grip. There was no need to make a ceremony out of it. The measurements showed that the frame was still structurally sound but no longer long enough for Gabe's present height. The mattress had compressed at the foot, and the headboard was beginning to sit too close to his shoulders. They could extend the platform with a matched insert, reinforce the centre rail, and move the wardrobe by a few inches. It was a repair rather than a replacement, which suited them both. Gabe wanted to keep the frame because it had been there when he first moved into the room. Luke understood that the old furniture could carry history without being allowed to dictate the next arrangement. A second knock sounded at the door, and Daniel appeared with Tyler just behind him. Daniel carried a pattern tube from the workshop and a folded sketch of the frame extension. Luke had sent a message after taking the first measurements; Daniel had offered to check the joint design, while Tyler had volunteered to inspect the centre support. They were not there to take over. They had brought another pair of eyes and the right tools, and they waited for Gabe to show them what he wanted preserved.

**GABE:** “I don't want a completely different bed.”

**DANIEL:** “We can keep the headboard and replace the side rails. The extension can bolt into the old frame without covering the original joint.”

**LUKE:** “That sounds right.”

**TYLER:** “We'll check the centre support before we put the mattress back.”

**GABE:** “I knew you were going to say that.”

**TYLER:** “Then you can save us both time and agree.”

Gabe laughed. Tyler smiled. The work proceeded. Andrew carried the oak extension from the rack, and Luke held the frame steady while Daniel marked the new length. The fresh timber had a warm, resinous smell beneath the surface dust. When the saw cut through it, a clean ribbon of pale wood curled away from the blade and dropped onto the floor. Gabe ran his fingers over the new edge. It felt smooth and firm, with a grain that was slightly lighter than the old rail. The difference would remain visible. He liked that. The extension would not pretend that the bed had always been this long. It would show where the frame had changed and still belong to the same piece of furniture.

Tyler checked the centre support, then asked Gabe to stand beside the frame while the others tested the alignment. Gabe's shoulders had widened again, and his shirt was beginning to pull beneath the arms. He could feel the fabric against his back when he leaned forward. Luke noticed but did not interrupt the work. Andrew stood at the opposite end, holding the spare rail. Daniel guided the fastener through the predrilled hole. Chase took one photograph of the joint and asked Gabe whether he wanted it kept. Gabe said yes, but privately. Chase nodded and returned the camera to his side. There was a quiet satisfaction in the fact that the frame could be changed without being discarded, and that the men who had assembled it once could work on it again without treating the old version as a failure.

The bed passed its first load check. The centre rail held, the side joints remained square, and the headboard stayed where Gabe wanted it. They moved the frame back upstairs, adjusted the mattress, and fitted a wider set of sheets from the linen store. Gabe lay down for a moment, then sat up with a broad grin. His feet no longer approached the lower edge, and his shoulders had room to settle against the headboard. Luke stood beside the bed, looking pleased in the unguarded way that Gabe had wanted the previous day.

**GABE:** “That's better.”

**LUKE:** “Good.”

**GABE:** “You can say you fixed it.”

**LUKE:** “We fixed it.”

Gabe reached up and took Luke's hand. Their palms met with a solid warmth, fingers wrapping around one another in a familiar clasp. It lasted only a few seconds, but both men understood it. Gabe was grateful for the repair; Luke was glad to have been asked into it; neither needed to turn the moment into a speech. They left the bed ready for the evening and went back downstairs to the workshop.

The old service landing had been cleared for the Room for Quiet conversion. The maintenance team had removed the partition the previous afternoon, leaving a rectangular space with a shallow recess, a narrow window, and an old ventilation duct that had been sealed during the last renovation. The floorboards were darker near the wall, where stored objects had shielded them from the light. A thin layer of plaster dust remained along the skirting, and the air had the dry, slightly stale smell of a room that had not been used for years. One brass hook remained on the inside of the doorframe. The old paint was a muted green, worn smooth near the handle. Beyond the window, the covered walk and a small section of the garden could be seen between the buildings. It was not a grand room. That was part of its appeal.

Chase stood in the doorway for a long time. He had been the person who proposed the private archive folder, and he had been careful not to confuse his preference for preserving things with the needs of other residents. Now he looked at the empty room and seemed to recognise something in it. A space that did not demand a performance. A room where the door could close, the light could be adjusted, and a man could sit without being asked what he was thinking. Joseph noticed that he had gone quiet, but did not ask him to explain. Instead, he walked into the room and checked the window latch.

**JOSEPH:** “It's a good view.”

**CHASE:** “It's a small view.”

**JOSEPH:** “Small can be enough.”

Chase looked at the window again. The glass had a faint ripple in it, so the garden beyond appeared to move slightly when the wind disturbed the leaves. He imagined a chair by the wall, a shelf of books, and a lamp with a warm shade. He did not imagine a screen or a camera. The room could contain quiet without needing to represent quiet as a visual theme. He told Joseph that he would like to try it once the work was finished. Joseph said that sounded reasonable. There was no pressure to make it a personal retreat or to declare it his. The building could offer the room to everyone, and Chase could choose to use it.

The work required more than a chair and a lamp. Daniel inspected the ventilation duct and found that the old grille could not be reused. Tyler checked the maintenance drawing and marked the likely route for a new vent. Luke measured the doorway and the available wall clearance. Andrew proposed a built-in shelf that could double as a seat. Tyler reminded him that the room needed a chair that could be moved. Andrew called this a failure of imagination. Daniel said that a movable chair was useful because a person could change the arrangement without asking permission from the furniture. That argument pleased Joseph, who suggested that the room should have a table just large enough for a book, a mug, and nothing else.

The men set to work. The vent cover was removed; the duct was cleared; a new grille was measured and fitted; the electrical circuit was checked; the wall was patched where the partition had been fixed; and the old green paint was cleaned rather than covered. The work had a steady rhythm. Tyler measured, Daniel marked, Andrew carried, Luke held, Joseph organised the tools, and Chase recorded the parts that would need to be checked again after the room had been used. Gabe came in later with the extended bed-frame offcut, which was too good to waste. Daniel suggested using it for the new shelf, preserving the same oak grain in a different room. Gabe agreed, provided the shelf did not become a memorial to his bed.

**ANDREW:** “We can call it the Gabe Shelf.”

**GABE:** “Absolutely not.”

**TYLER:** “The shelf will be labelled with its material and installation date.”

**ANDREW:** “The Gabe Shelf.”

**CHASE:** “The record will show that the proposal was rejected.”

**JOSEPH:** “And the shelf will still be a shelf.”

The laughter carried out into the corridor. The room was beginning to acquire a different smell: fresh plaster, warm timber, and the faint clean scent of the new vent drawing air from the covered passage. The narrow window admitted a pale strip of daylight. The floorboards no longer looked abandoned; they looked ready to be used. The men moved the chair in and out, checked the door swing, and tried the lamp from different corners. The light settled warmly over the oak shelf and the green wall. Chase stood in the centre of the room and listened. The sound of the corridor was muted, not absent. He could hear a distant footstep, the movement of a door, and a brief laugh from the common kitchen. It felt like being inside the Institute without being inside the crowd.

**CHASE:** “I think it works.”

**JOSEPH:** “Then sit.”

Chase sat in the chair. It was wide, stable, and upholstered in a dark fabric that felt cool at first and then warmed beneath him. The chair's feet rested evenly on the floor. The small table stood beside it; the oak shelf ran along the wall; the vent moved a quiet current of air across the room. Chase placed his notebook on the table and did not open it. He simply sat. Joseph remained in the doorway, giving him enough privacy to enjoy the room without making a show of leaving. The moment was brief, but it changed the meaning of the old landing. It had become a place where a person could be present without being required to contribute.

The room still needed a final inspection, a door seal, and a small plaque that would identify its purpose without turning it into a monument. The men agreed that the plaque should simply say ROOM FOR QUIET. No motto, no slogan, no heroic inscription. Andrew suggested a small arrow pointing toward the chair. Tyler objected that the room was visible from the corridor. Andrew said the arrow was for people who had forgotten how doors worked. Joseph laughed and let the matter go. The plaque would be fitted the next morning, once the paint had fully dried.

By mid-afternoon, the Dormitory Decathlon had become the next major event. Andrew had expanded the original route into a cooperative circuit with seven stations. Each station would require at least two residents, and no one would be timed. The point was to practise moving through the East Wing with bodies that were growing, equipment that was heavy, and spaces that had been built for more than one kind of use. The circuit would begin in the common room, pass through the shoe store and laundry corridor, continue to the study room and Map Room, descend to the workshop, and finish on the roof terrace. The kitchen would remain a refreshment stop, not a timed station. The shower suite would be left private. The stairwell would be a walking route with a handrail, not an obstacle.

**ANDREW:** “Seven stations. Six men. One building. No prizes.”

**TYLER:** “That's not a decathlon.”

**ANDREW:** “It is now.”

**DANIEL:** “What are the tasks?”

Andrew unfolded his cereal-box plan. It had grown into a large sheet of paper covered in arrows, diagrams, and small sketches. The common room station involved moving a set of modular cushions into a pattern shown on a card. The shoe room required the team to load the new pull-out rack and return the right pairs to their marked bays. The laundry station involved guiding a full cart around two padded corners without striking the wall. The study room station required the men to assemble a flat-pack reading desk using only the tools in the tray. The Map Room station was a search for the old service landing on the original building plan. The workshop station involved moving a long oak beam from the rack to the cutting table. The final station on the roof terrace required the team to arrange six chairs and a table so that every man had room to sit without blocking the door.

Tyler looked at the plan and began removing the things that could create unnecessary risk. The laundry cart would be empty for the test. The beam would be a light practice length rather than the full structural piece. The stairs would not be used as a timed obstacle. Nobody would be asked to lift beyond his capacity or continue after a fatigue limit. Andrew objected that Tyler was turning the decathlon into a maintenance induction. Tyler replied that a maintenance induction was how a building survived people having fun in it. Joseph suggested that the tasks should reward communication as much as speed. Luke said that the course should have at least one point where the men had to stop and let another person choose the next move. Chase asked whether the rules could be written in a way that made it clear that a resident could skip a station without being treated as having failed. Daniel suggested that the shoe rack task should include a check of the new dividers.

**SETH:** “This is the part of the sports movie where somebody unveils an elaborate course and the sensible friend spends the next ten minutes removing the dangerous bits.”

**NICK:** “It's a cooperative course. The obstacle is that Andrew has designed it.”

**SETH:** “Which is why Tyler has arrived with a notebook and a second opinion.”

Andrew put one hand over his chest. “I am being attacked in my own chapter.”

Chase did not look up. “The narrator has a point.”

The first run began in the common room. The modular cushions had been rearranged into an uneven pattern along the sofa, with a card showing the intended final arrangement. The men had to move them without blocking the door or covering the low storage cabinet. Luke and Gabe took the larger sections; Andrew and Joseph handled the smaller cushions; Tyler checked the plan; Chase watched the clearances. It was not difficult, but the task became more involved when Gabe grew another fraction through the shoulders and found that the space between the sofa and the bookcase was tighter than he expected. Luke noticed and moved the table out of the way. Gabe shifted the cushion through at an angle. They did not need to force it. The task was complete when the room had a clear route through it and every cushion sat in the pattern on the card.

Andrew declared the first station too easy. Tyler said that was the point. Andrew said a course should contain surprise. Tyler replied that surprise was not the same as difficulty. Joseph suggested a surprise station later. Andrew looked pleased. Tyler looked suspicious.

The shoe room had a different atmosphere. Its floor carried the faint rubber smell of the soles stored on the lower racks, and the air moved steadily through the drying vents. The new pull-out shelf was not installed yet, but Daniel had built a prototype with removable dividers and a low-friction runner. He placed it on the test frame and loaded it with pairs of shoes chosen to represent different weights and widths. Andrew wanted to test it by pulling the drawer open in one dramatic movement. Tyler told him to use the handle and keep the motion controlled. Daniel checked the dividers, then asked Luke to stand at the far side and watch for twisting. The drawer slid out smoothly. The dividers held. The men returned the shoes to their labelled bays, placing the largest pairs on the lower shelves and leaving the centre aisle clear.

**DANIEL:** “It needs a stop at the end. Otherwise someone will pull it too far.”

**TYLER:** “Agreed. A removable stop, so the drawer can be serviced.”

**ANDREW:** “A dramatic stop.”

**DANIEL:** “A mechanical stop.”

**ANDREW:** “We have different visions.”

Luke tested the drawer with one hand. “It works.”

Daniel looked pleased. The design had begun as a practical response to shoes that no longer fit their owners, but the group was turning it into a shared feature. Daniel and Tyler had become good collaborators because each could see what the other was trying to protect. Daniel cared about the person who used the shoe; Tyler cared about the system that would keep the storage working. Neither had to abandon his way of thinking. Their arguments made the design more useful.

The laundry station was the first one to make Andrew laugh hard enough that he had to lean on the wall. The empty cart was wider than the corridor by a small margin, and its wheels had a habit of pulling to the left. The men had to move it around two padded corners and park it beside the clean linen shelves without striking the wall. Luke pushed from the rear; Gabe guided the near side; Joseph kept the folded towels from sliding; Chase watched the clearance; Tyler called out the turn; and Andrew, who had insisted that he could handle the front, tried to steer while looking backward. The cart drifted toward the wall. Gabe caught the handle. Luke stopped the rear wheels. Nobody was hurt, and the cart came to rest with its front wheels neatly inside the marked bay.

**TYLER:** “You were looking the wrong way.”

**ANDREW:** “I was checking the team.”

**GABE:** “You were checking the wall.”

**ANDREW:** “The wall was very close.”

Chase laughed. The sound bounced off the tile and travelled into the next room. The laundry smelled of warm cotton and detergent, with the industrial dryers humming softly behind the door. Steam gathered along the upper windows, and a clean stack of towels sat on the long folding table. The men put the cart away and folded the towels they had disturbed. The course did not reward them for finishing quickly. It rewarded them for leaving the space ready for the next resident.

The study room station was quieter. The room had adjustable lamps, broad desks, acoustic panels, and shelves of reference books. A flat-pack desk lay in pieces on the floor. The instructions were printed on a single card, with the parts arranged by number. Daniel read the steps, Tyler sorted the fasteners, Chase held the side panel, and Luke tightened the bolts. Andrew began to assemble the top before the frame was square. Tyler stopped him. Andrew argued that the desk would settle into position once the top was attached. Daniel disagreed. Joseph looked at the instructions and pointed to the next line. The group rebuilt the frame in the correct order. It took longer, but the finished desk did not wobble.

**CHASE:** “The lesson is that the instructions were right.”

**ANDREW:** “The lesson is that we should have better instructions.”

**TYLER:** “The instructions were clear.”

**ANDREW:** “They were written by a desk.”

Joseph smiled. “The desk seems pleased with itself.”

The Map Room station involved the old service landing. The men had to locate it on the original plan and compare the dimensions with the newly exposed room. Tyler placed the drawing on the table and aligned it with the modern plan. Chase read the labels aloud. Daniel measured the wall recess. Andrew searched the plan for an old cupboard that no longer existed. Luke found the old ventilation line. Gabe noticed that the partition had hidden the door swing. The discovery was not a magical one; it was the result of several men looking at the same drawing from different positions. The old landing had been there all along. It had simply been covered, forgotten, and then found again.

The men stopped at the doorway. The room now smelled of fresh timber and clean air. The new vent drew a light current through the space, and the oak shelf had been fitted along the wall. The chair sat near the window. The plaque had not yet been installed, but the letters had been cut and were resting on a cloth beside the door. The men had given the room a purpose without making it a shrine. It was not a place for people who had failed to enjoy the common room. It was a place for anyone who wanted to spend time without being drawn into every conversation.

Chase stood in the doorway. He had spent years observing other men and keeping records of their actions. He was learning to recognise when he needed a space where nobody expected him to notice anything. Joseph stood beside him. He did not ask whether Chase wanted company or solitude. He waited.

**CHASE:** “I think I'll use it tonight.”

**JOSEPH:** “All right.”

**CHASE:** “You don't have to come with me.”

**JOSEPH:** “I know.”

Chase looked at him. “You can wait outside if you want.”

Joseph nodded. It was a small adjustment, but it showed the way their friendship had changed. Joseph could remain available without assuming that his presence had to fill every room. Chase could ask for quiet without turning it into rejection. Their relationship had become more precise because they had learned to state what they wanted.

The workshop station required the men to move a length of practice timber from the rack to the cutting table. The beam was lighter than the oak used in the bed repair, but it was long enough to require coordination. Andrew wanted to carry the front. Tyler wanted to take the back. Daniel suggested that Luke and Gabe should take the middle supports because their height made the grip more comfortable. Chase checked the route. Joseph opened the workshop door. They lifted together and moved the timber through the space without striking the bench or the tool cabinet. The beam settled onto the cutting table with a dull, satisfying thud.

The final station was the roof terrace. The wind was mild, and the wet stone had begun to dry. The chairs were heavy, with broad wooden arms and cushions that smelled faintly of cedar when the covers were lifted. The group had to arrange six chairs and a table so that all of them could sit without blocking the door. At first Andrew placed the table in the centre. Tyler pointed out that the route to the storage chest would be blocked. Gabe moved one chair closer to the parapet. Luke shifted another toward the covered section. Daniel tested the clearance. Joseph brought out the blankets. Chase looked at the arrangement from the doorway and suggested that the chairs should not be placed in a perfect circle. Some men liked to face the view; others preferred to face one another.

They moved the table twice. The final arrangement was slightly asymmetrical, with a clear path from the stair door to the covered section and enough room for each man to sit comfortably. It was not a perfect diagram. It was a usable place. The sun had come through the clouds, and the terrace smelled of wet stone, cedar, and the small evergreen shrubs along the parapet. The men stood together for a moment, looking out over the campus. The gymnasium roof shone in the light. The dining hall windows reflected the sky. The dormitory's long brick walls were darker where rain had run down the mortar.

**ANDREW:** “We need a winner.”

**TYLER:** “No.”

**ANDREW:** “A champion.”

**JOSEPH:** “No.”

**ANDREW:** “A medal.”

**CHASE:** “No.”

**GABE:** “A pancake.”

Andrew stopped. “Now we're talking.”

The men returned to the common kitchen. Andrew made a fresh batch of batter, larger than the previous one but less architectural in ambition. He spread it across the griddle and waited until the edges began to bubble. The butter hissed softly. The smell filled the room, mixing with coffee and the faint sweetness of maple syrup. Luke brought plates. Gabe carried the fruit. Tyler checked the griddle's temperature and insisted that the first pancake be treated as a test. Andrew said the first pancake was always a sacrifice to the gods of breakfast. Chase asked whether the gods had signed the maintenance form. Joseph laughed and placed the syrup on the table.

They ate at the repaired table, which held the plates without moving. The new bed was ready upstairs. The quiet room had its chair, light, shelf, vent, and plaque. The shoe rack had a working prototype. The laundry cart had passed its route. The map had identified the hidden landing. The workshop timber had been moved without damage. The terrace had a usable arrangement. None of these achievements was world-shattering. Together, they made the dormitory a better place to live.

Gabe leaned back in his chair. The shirt across his chest had tightened again. He could feel the fabric resting against his shoulders, and the sleeve had shifted higher on his arm. Luke noticed and smiled. Gabe saw him.

**GABE:** “Don't.”

**LUKE:** “I didn't say anything.”

**GABE:** “You were going to.”

**LUKE:** “I was going to ask if you wanted another shirt.”

**GABE:** “I do, actually.”

Luke nodded. “I'll bring one up.”

The ease of the exchange pleased Gabe. He did not have to turn the growth into a performance, and Luke did not have to pretend he had failed to notice it. The two men could be close without making a contest out of who had changed more. Across the table, Andrew was telling Tyler that the obstacle course needed a second version with a baseball checkpoint. Tyler said it would need a proper plan. Andrew said he already had one. Tyler asked whether the plan had been tested. Andrew said the plan was the testing. Chase looked at Joseph. Joseph shook his head, smiling. Daniel had begun sketching the final shoe rack, and the table was once again covered in small pieces of paper.

After lunch, Chase used Room for Quiet for the first time. He took a book from the reading room, a mug of tea, and his notebook, though he left the notebook closed. The door clicked softly behind him. The vent carried a small current of fresh air through the room, and the lamp warmed the green wall with a pale amber glow. He sat in the chair and listened. The common room could be heard faintly through the wall, but the sounds had lost their edges. He could hear Andrew laughing, the low movement of chairs, and the distant call of someone crossing the courtyard. It was enough to know that he was not alone. It was also enough to be alone.

Joseph waited outside for a moment, then went downstairs. He did not knock. The room had been built to make that possible. It gave Chase a place to return to himself without requiring another person to interpret the choice. The door remained shut, and the Institute continued around it.

Seth and Nick were heard again from the upper gallery, where the afternoon light had begun to move across the oak beams.

**SETH:** “A new room, a new bed, a shoe rack, a fully functioning laundry cart, and a decathlon nobody won. It's almost as if the point was to live here.”

**NICK:** “The writers have been trying to tell us that for eighty chapters.”

**SETH:** “Perhaps the audience is finally ready.”

**NICK:** “The audience is waiting for the next pancake.”

**SETH:** “Then we know what the next chapter needs.”

That evening the men gathered on the roof terrace. The temperature had dropped, and the cedar cushions felt cool beneath their hands. Luke brought a blanket for Gabe. Andrew brought the notebook with the new course design. Tyler brought a list of revisions. Daniel brought a drawing of the shoe rack. Chase arrived after a while, carrying no notebook at all. Joseph made room for him at the table. They sat together, talking about the day and the next things they might build. Their bodies continued changing, slowly and independently. The terrace chairs were deep enough to hold them, but the men could feel the frame of the seats beneath their weight. Their shoulders occupied more space. Their legs extended farther beneath the table. Their hands looked larger around the mugs. The physical changes were no longer interruptions. They were part of the life they were making.

The Dormitory Decathlon had begun as Andrew's joke, and it had become a way of learning the building. The men discovered that they could move through the East Wing without racing, that a task could be shared without becoming a competition, and that a good room could give a person company or privacy according to what he needed. They had not solved every practical problem. The shoe rack still required a final load test; the laundry vent needed a seasonal check; the old service landing would require a review after several weeks of use; and the bed frame would need another measurement when Gabe's growth settled. These were not failures. They were the next entries in a maintenance ledger.

The last thing Andrew did before going to bed was tape the revised course map beside the pancake photograph in the common room. Tyler added the current route version number. Chase added the date. Joseph wrote the next communal supper beneath it. Luke added a note that the quiet room should remain unbooked unless someone specifically requested it. Gabe wrote one more line beneath the whole arrangement: **WE CAN MAKE ROOM.**

The phrase looked familiar. It had been engraved above the Institute's entrance for years. But in the dormitory it had acquired a more immediate meaning. It meant extending a bed without discarding its headboard, building a place where a man could sit without speaking, making a shoe rack that could be repaired, and allowing a friend to notice a change without asking him to perform it. It meant that the men could become larger without becoming less available to one another.

The corridor settled into its night rhythm. A shower ran on the second floor. The laundry finished its last cycle. The kitchen lights dimmed. In Room for Quiet, Chase closed his book and turned off the lamp. He left the chair where it was. Down the corridor, Gabe lay on the extended bed and felt the mattress support his full length. Luke had brought him the larger shirt, and he had folded it neatly over the chair for the morning. The old bed frame had become longer without losing its history. The room had grown with him.

Across the hall, Andrew was already sketching a second version of the Decathlon. Tyler would object to the first course map; Andrew knew that. He was looking forward to it. Daniel had left his shoe rack drawing on the kitchen table. Joseph had taken the last mug downstairs. Chase's quiet room remained available. The men had another day ahead of them.

The next morning, an envelope would arrive from the western survey camp. Dorian Vale would report that the crossing had been remeasured, that the temporary brace had held, and that a new support design was being considered. Tyler would want the source readings. Andrew would ask whether the bridge had a distinct note when Dorian stepped near it. Gabe would wonder whether he was ready to meet him again. Luke would ask whether he wanted company. Joseph would make breakfast. Chase would decide whether the new report belonged in the archive or only in the working file.

The East Dormitory would wake to coffee, warm bread, wet shoes, the smell of cedar, and the sound of a much larger group of men moving through a building that was learning their names. No one had won the Decathlon. Everyone had helped make the place more inhabitable. That was the result worth keeping.
