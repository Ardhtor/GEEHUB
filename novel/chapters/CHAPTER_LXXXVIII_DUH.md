# Chapter LXXXVIII — duh!

Sunday arrived with a bright, cold clarity that made the cedar arcade look newly built. The rain had passed during the night, leaving beads of water along the dark beams and a clean shine on the paving stones. Beyond the Athletic Commons, the western woods rose in layers of green and brown, and the survey office windows reflected the pale morning sky. Dorian had arrived early with two coffees, a paper bag of breakfast sandwiches, and the revised drawings Tyler had sent over the previous evening. He had also changed his shirt twice before leaving his room. The first was too close-fitting at the shoulders; the second pulled across his chest whenever he reached forward. The third was a dark green knit that sat comfortably against his frame and made his eyes look lighter. He had not dressed for an audience, but he had dressed for Gabe. There was a difference, and he was beginning to enjoy it.

Gabe came through the arcade carrying a small box from the bakery. He wore a fitted cream shirt beneath a brown jacket, dark trousers, and boots polished enough to catch the window light. His hair was still slightly damp. He saw Dorian at the table and smiled before he had reached him.

“You brought coffee.”

“Two.”

“I brought breakfast.”

“Then we have accidentally organised a very efficient morning.”

Gabe put the box down and looked at the drawings. “You said coffee first.”

“I did.”

“And then the drawings.”

“They're here.”

Gabe looked from the paper to Dorian and back again. “You really do know how to make a date sound like a site meeting.”

Dorian pulled out the chair opposite him. “I thought you liked the drawings.”

“I do. I also like you.”

“Good. Sit down.”

Gabe laughed as he took the chair. “Duh!”

The word came out so naturally that both men laughed again. It was not an argument, and it was not a declaration. It was the answer to a question neither had needed to ask. Dorian slid one of the coffees across the table. Gabe opened the bakery box: two warm pastries, a small loaf with rosemary, and a pair of dense chocolate cakes wrapped in paper. They ate before opening the drawings, letting the morning take its own pace.

The western crossing occupied the centre sheet. Tyler had marked the lateral restraint assembly in blue pencil, Andrew had added a separate note about the change in resonance during the loaded test, and the drainage survey showed the narrow channel behind the retaining wall with its obstruction circled in red. The drawing was becoming crowded, but the separate evidence was still legible. Dorian explained the proposed sequence: clear the drainage channel, expose and inspect the joint, correct the alignment, then test the revised restraint under changing load. Only after the independent findings had been addressed would the team review whether the full bridge could reopen.

Gabe followed the marks with one finger. “So the bracket isn't the whole problem.”

“No. It was the first thing the changing-load test made obvious. The water and the joint are separate conditions. If we fix one and leave the others, we haven't fixed the crossing.”

“And if somebody says the static test was fine?”

“Then we show them what the static test did and didn't establish.”

Gabe nodded. “You make it sound simple.”

“The explanation is simple. The work isn't.”

He leaned over the drawing, and Dorian shifted the coffee away from the edge before Gabe's sleeve could catch it. Their shoulders touched. Neither moved away immediately. The room was quiet except for the heating system and the distant movement of people arriving at the gym. Gabe's hand remained on the paper, close to Dorian's. He looked up, and for a moment the bridge disappeared beneath the more immediate fact of the man across the table.

“You're looking at me,” Gabe said.

“I am.”

“Not the drawing.”

“No.”

Gabe smiled. “Good.”

Dorian did not make a performance of the answer. He was comfortable with desire being visible. His size had made him conspicuous long before he learned how to dress well, and he had spent enough time being assessed by strangers to understand the difference between attention and intimacy. Gabe was not a crowd. He was a man sitting across from him with chocolate on one finger and a question in his eyes. Dorian reached across the table and brushed the small trace of chocolate away with his thumb. Gabe turned his hand and caught Dorian's fingers before he could withdraw them.

“I like this,” Gabe said.

“The coffee?”

“Obviously the coffee. But this too.”

Dorian let his hand remain there. “I like it too.”

They returned to the drawings when they were ready, and the work benefited from the pause rather than suffering from it. Gabe noticed that the revised inspection platform left too little room for the removable central member. Dorian had been concentrating on the load path and had overlooked the maintenance clearance. They marked the space and added a note for Tyler. Gabe proposed a hinged section that could be secured during testing and opened for inspection. Dorian asked whether the hinge would introduce another point of movement. They worked through the problem together, sketching two alternatives in the margin. The second was simpler: a removable guard panel with a locking pin, stored clear of the load-bearing assembly. Dorian wrote Gabe's initials beside the suggestion.

“You don't have to put my initials on it,” Gabe said.

“I know.”

“It's your drawing.”

“It's our review.”

Gabe looked at the page. He did not hide that the gesture pleased him. “All right. Keep them there.”

At eleven, Tyler arrived with a fresh set of measurements and found them sitting close together over the same sheet. He paused at the door, looked at their hands, then at the two empty coffee cups.

“Should I come back?”

“No,” Dorian said.

“Definitely not,” Gabe added. “We have an engineering problem.”

Tyler glanced at the drawing. “That much I can see.”

“There's a maintenance clearance issue,” Gabe said, turning the sheet toward him. “The guard needs to come away without disturbing the restraint assembly.”

Tyler studied the margin sketch. “That's better than what I had. I was trying to make the whole section hinge.”

“Too many moving parts,” Dorian said.

Tyler nodded. “I'll take this to the workshop.”

He gathered the measurement sheets, then looked at the bakery box. “You two have been here since breakfast?”

“Since coffee,” Gabe said.

“That's practically a formal commitment.”

Gabe looked at Dorian. Dorian looked back. Neither answered immediately.

Tyler grinned. “Right. I'll leave you to your very serious drawing review.”

When he had gone, Gabe leaned back in his chair. “The campus is going to be unbearable.”

“Only if we let it be.”

“You're not worried about people talking?”

“People talk when they're bored. We don't have to organise our lives around it.”

Gabe considered that. “I don't want to hide. I also don't want every time we have coffee to become a public event.”

“Then we don't announce every time we have coffee.”

“Duh!”

Again, the word made them laugh. Gabe seemed pleased to have found a small expression that could hold both affection and impatience. Dorian liked hearing it. It sounded like Gabe: direct, amused, and unembarrassed by the obvious.

By midday, the Commons had become busier. The training floor was full, and the café had begun serving lunch. Dorian and Gabe carried the drawings to the large table near the western windows, where Andrew was comparing the sound-test notes with a set of structural photographs. Andrew greeted them and immediately asked whether they had settled the guard-panel question. Gabe explained the removable design. Andrew said the idea made sense, then asked whether the new panel would rattle under vibration. They added a rubber isolation strip to the note and agreed that Tyler would check the material in the workshop.

The conversation moved easily between technical detail and ordinary campus life. Andrew asked about the restaurant. Gabe said the food had been good. Dorian said the company had been better. Andrew made a theatrical face of disappointment and claimed that Northgate's standards were slipping if a first date could not produce a more detailed review of the pasta. Gabe told him the pasta had been excellent, the beef was generous, and Dorian had ordered enough vegetables for a small expedition. Andrew said that sounded exactly like Dorian.

The remark drew a few looks from the next table. Dorian noticed them, then noticed Gabe noticing him notice. He did not change his posture. He had no interest in pretending that he was unaware of the attention, but he was not going to turn it into a show. Gabe, for his part, looked entirely comfortable. The two men were not performing a relationship for the room. They were simply sitting together, sharing a meal and a set of drawings, with the ease of people who had decided to see one another again.

After lunch, they walked toward the gym. The afternoon session was underway, and the mirrors were bright with movement. Men crossed between the racks and benches in training clothes, carrying towels and water bottles. The room had its own kind of display: broad backs, powerful legs, thick arms, careful posture, and the small rituals of preparation. Some men trained for strength, some for size, some for the pleasure of being seen, and many for several reasons at once. The Evening of Presence had left a residue in the Commons. Compliments were more direct. Men were more willing to admit when they admired another man's physique or style. But the gym remained a place of work, and the strongest men were still expected to rerack their weights.

A man from the afternoon group called Dorian over to ask about the western route. Another complimented Gabe's jacket. Gabe thanked him and asked where he had found the shoes. The conversation lasted a minute and ended without anyone needing to turn it into a contest. Dorian watched Gabe move through the room with growing ease. He was not trying to imitate Dorian's confidence. He was discovering his own.

Near the far mirror, a tall athlete finished a set and stood upright, chest rising with his breath. His training partner gave him a brief appraisal and said, “That's the frame you wanted.”

“Not finished,” the athlete replied.

“Never is.”

Gabe heard the exchange and smiled. “That's going to be the whole campus now.”

“Maybe.”

“Everyone getting bigger, everyone getting dressed up, everyone pretending not to notice who's looking.”

“Some of them won't pretend.”

“Good.”

Dorian glanced at him. “You like that?”

“I like people being honest. I like a man knowing he looks good. I like seeing somebody walk into a room and not apologise for taking up space.” Gabe paused. “I also like knowing that he can make room for someone else.”

Dorian understood the distinction. Physical superiority, when it meant strength, discipline, presence, or the confidence to be seen, could be cultivated without turning into a claim over other people. A man could be proud of his body without treating another body as a lesser thing. He could enjoy being desired without expecting desire to become obedience. The campus was learning to hold those ideas together, though it would not always manage perfectly.

They left the gym and took the western path as far as the closure barrier. The bridge was visible beyond it, its central section still marked for inspection. The new survey stakes stood along the drainage channel, and a strip of orange mesh separated the work area from the public route. Gabe read the notice, then looked at Dorian.

“Still closed.”

“Until the repair and testing are complete.”

“Even if everyone thinks it looks fine?”

“Especially then.”

Gabe smiled. “You really are consistent.”

“It's useful.”

They stood for a moment beneath the trees. Dorian could see the line of the bridge through the branches, the pale structure against the darker ground. The crossing would reopen when the evidence supported it. There was no need to hurry the decision. Gabe stepped closer, slipped his hand into Dorian's, and looked down the path toward the Commons.

“Next Sunday?” he asked.

“Next Sunday.”

“Not a survey meeting.”

“No drawings.”

“Not even one?”

Dorian pretended to consider it. “One small drawing.”

Gabe shook his head. “You're impossible.”

“Yet here you are.”

“Duh!”

He kissed Dorian beside the path, longer this time, with no audience close enough to make it a performance. When they separated, Gabe remained near him. Dorian's hand rested at the back of Gabe's jacket, warm through the fabric. Neither tried to turn the moment into a promise beyond what it was. They had a second meeting planned. That was enough to make the next week feel different.

As they walked back, the Commons came into view through the trees: the gym windows, the café terrace, the cedar arcade, and the survey office where the revised drawings were waiting for Tyler. Men moved through the grounds in small groups, some carrying gym bags, others returning from lunch or heading toward the workshop. The place was becoming more openly confident. Its residents were learning to take pride in their bodies and in the way they presented themselves, while discovering that confidence had to survive ordinary contact: a shared table, a difficult conversation, an apology, a practical correction, a kiss that did not settle every question.

Seth and Nick watched from the upper gallery as Dorian and Gabe came back along the path.

**SETH:**

The first dinner has become a second meeting, and the second meeting has become an afternoon neither man wanted to end early. The drawings were real. The attraction was real. Neither one had to cancel the other.

**NICK:**

Gabe is not trying to become Dorian. Dorian is not trying to collect Gabe. They are two men who like what they see, and who are beginning to find out what else is there.

**SETH:**

And the title?

**NICK:**

Duh.

The revised crossing sketch went to Tyler before the workshop closed. The drainage survey remained open, the bridge remained closed, and the maintenance panel was added to the inspection notes. In the relationship record, the change was smaller and more personal: Dorian and Gabe had now spent two deliberate occasions together, shared a first kiss, and chosen another day. No announcement was made. No label was required. By evening, Gabe had sent Dorian a photograph of the chocolate cakes from the bakery box, with one word beneath it.

*duh!*

Dorian replied with a photograph of the marked-up drawing. Beside Gabe's removable-panel suggestion, he had written: *good catch.*

Then he sent another message.

*Sunday. No drawings.*

Gabe's reply arrived almost immediately.

*We'll see.*

Dorian smiled at the screen. It was not a grand beginning, but it was becoming theirs.
