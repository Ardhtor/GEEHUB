# Chapter LXXXVII — The First Dinner

The dinner had been arranged for Thursday, and by late afternoon the whole of Northgate seemed to know that Dorian Vale and Gabe were going out together. Nobody had been told to keep it secret. Neither man had announced it, either. The information had travelled through the ordinary channels of a residential campus: a remark at the gym desk, a question about whether the late table would be occupied, a passing comment from Luke, and Andrew's insistence that a first date deserved a meal more substantial than whatever could be assembled from the café counter after training. By the time Dorian came down from his rooms, the story had acquired three different versions. In one, he had booked the private dining room; in another, Gabe had challenged him to a food contest; in the third, Tyler had been asked to measure the chairs before they arrived. The truth was quieter and better. They had chosen a small restaurant beyond the cedar arcade, one with a long window facing the wooded road and a menu built around grilled meat, fish, peppers, pickled vegetables, and handmade pasta.

Dorian had dressed carefully. His dark jacket had been altered twice in the shoulders, and the tailor had left enough allowance in the seams for another adjustment if his frame continued to broaden. Beneath it he wore a pale shirt with the collar open. His chest and upper arms gave the fabric a substantial shape without making the outfit look like gym clothing. His trousers had a clean line through the thigh, and his shoes were polished. The changes in his body were still evident even when he was not training: the breadth of his back, the thickness of his neck, the way his hands occupied more space around an ordinary glass. He had become accustomed to noticing these things as practical facts, but tonight he was also aware that he wanted to look attractive. He checked the fit once in the mirror, not to find a flaw, but to make sure the jacket sat as he intended. Then he left.

Gabe was waiting beneath the arcade. He wore a dark rust-coloured shirt, a fitted charcoal jacket, and a narrow silver watch he usually kept for occasions that mattered. He had made an effort with his hair and beard, and he looked composed without seeming stiff. When Dorian approached, Gabe's eyes moved over him openly. There was no attempt to disguise the appraisal, and Dorian did not pretend not to notice.

“You look very good,” Gabe said.

“So do you.”

“I was hoping you'd say that.”

“I was going to say it anyway.”

Gabe laughed. He had expected the first few minutes to feel like an examination, as though one of them would need to prove that the invitation had been a good idea. Instead, Dorian offered his arm with a small, almost formal gesture, and Gabe took it. They walked through the arcade and down the cedar road at an unhurried pace. The lamps had begun to come on beneath the beams. Beyond the path, the woods held the last blue light of the day, and the restaurant windows appeared between the trunks as a warm rectangle.

The restaurant was called Il Ponte, although the owners had kept the old local name above the door. It had started as a family trattoria and had gradually developed a menu that reflected the surrounding district: Italian foundations, Texas portions, roasted peppers, smoked brisket, seafood, pickled jalapeños, and sauces that moved comfortably between the two traditions. The room was not large, but its timber ceiling and deep booths made it feel settled. A long counter ran along one wall, and behind it the kitchen staff moved between copper pans and a wood-fired oven. The host greeted Dorian by name, then looked at Gabe and asked whether they would prefer the window booth. Dorian said yes. The table had been reinforced during a recent renovation, but the work was invisible beneath the dark wood.

They began with olives, marinated artichokes, warm bread, and a plate of pickled vegetables. Gabe chose grilled fish with lemon, herbs, and roasted potatoes. Dorian ordered a large portion of slow-cooked beef with peppers and a side of pasta, then added another dish of grilled vegetables for the table. The portions were generous without being theatrical. When the food arrived, Gabe looked at Dorian's plate and raised an eyebrow.

“You said dinner,” Gabe said. “You didn't say provisions for a surveying crew.”

“I've been on the western road. It changes your sense of what counts as a meal.”

“You were already large before the bridge.”

“True. But now I have a better reason to order the extra vegetables.”

Gabe smiled and served himself. Their first conversation was easy because it did not need to carry the whole meaning of the evening. They talked about the restaurant, the latest work at the crossing, and the revised drainage report. Dorian described the way the water had gathered behind the western retaining wall. Gabe asked whether the new bracket would address the movement Tyler had observed. Dorian explained that the bracket was only one part of the repair; the drainage channel and joint alignment had to be corrected before any new test could be trusted. Gabe listened closely. He was not pretending to understand more than he did, and Dorian appreciated the questions.

After the plates had been cleared, Gabe asked him something that had been waiting between them since the terrace.

“Were you surprised when I said I found you attractive?”

Dorian considered the question. “A little. Not because I thought you couldn't. I just hadn't decided whether you were telling me something you wanted me to know, or whether you were still working it out yourself.”

“I was working it out. I knew I noticed you. I wasn't sure what I wanted to do with that.”

“And now?”

“Now I wanted to have dinner with you.”

Dorian nodded. “That's enough for tonight.”

Gabe looked at him. “You say that as though you're very comfortable with uncertainty.”

“I work around things that aren't finished. A bridge doesn't become safe because somebody wants the inspection to be over.”

“That sounds like a very Dorian answer.”

“It is.”

Gabe laughed, then let the conversation settle. The restaurant had grown busier. A group of men from the print room occupied the far booth, and two members of the rowing club were sharing a bottle of sparkling water near the counter. One of them noticed Dorian and raised his glass in greeting. Dorian returned the gesture. The attention did not interrupt the date. It simply entered the room and passed through it.

Gabe spoke about the Evening of Presence. He admitted that he had enjoyed the presentation walk more than he expected, and that part of the pleasure had come from discovering that he could be looked at without immediately measuring himself against someone else. Dorian said that he had noticed. Gabe asked whether Dorian always felt so certain in public. Dorian said no. He had learned to distinguish confidence from the expectation that every room would respond in a particular way. Sometimes a room admired him; sometimes it was busy with its own concerns. Neither response changed what he had to do when he went home.

“I like being desired,” Dorian said. “I don't want to pretend otherwise. I like knowing that a man has looked at me and decided he wants to keep looking. But I don't want to spend every evening waiting for that to happen.”

Gabe turned his glass slowly on the table. “I think I've done that.”

“You don't have to keep doing it.”

“I know. It's just different when someone says it plainly.”

Dorian rested his forearm beside his plate. The sleeve of his jacket pulled slightly across the muscle, and he adjusted it with a small movement. “You can want attention. You can enjoy it. You can even compete for it if that's the sort of evening you're having. But you don't have to make it the measure of every other part of your life.”

Gabe watched him for a moment. “I think that's what I liked about the way you walked that night. You didn't ask the room to decide who you were.”

“I knew who I was before I stepped onto the platform.”

“And you liked the applause.”

“I liked the applause.”

They both laughed. It was the first time that evening the conversation had become openly flirtatious rather than simply candid. Gabe reached across the table and rested two fingers against Dorian's hand. Dorian turned his palm upward. The contact remained there while they spoke about less consequential things: the café's new menu, a jacket Gabe wanted to have altered, and Andrew's claim that he could identify a man's training history by the way he ordered potatoes. When the bill came, they split it without discussion. Dorian offered to walk Gabe back to the Commons, and Gabe accepted.

Outside, the air had cooled. The road was dark beneath the cedar canopy, but the path lights were working. They walked close together, sometimes brushing shoulders. At the entrance to the arcade, Gabe stopped. He looked toward the gym windows, where the late group were still training beneath the bright white lights. Through the glass, a broad-shouldered man was finishing a set while another stood nearby, waiting to spot him. Their silhouettes moved against the mirrors.

“Do you ever get tired of being looked at?” Gabe asked.

“Sometimes. Usually when people think looking gives them a right to decide something for me.”

“Does it happen often?”

“Less than it used to. People here are learning the difference.”

Gabe nodded. “I want to be looked at. I don't want to be managed by it.”

“Then we agree.”

Gabe stepped closer. “And do we agree that this was a good first date?”

Dorian smiled. “Yes.”

“Would you like a second?”

“I would.”

Gabe kissed him. It was brief and deliberate, a first kiss rather than a promise about everything that might follow. Dorian returned it, then let Gabe decide whether to remain close or step away. Gabe stayed. They stood together for another moment beneath the cedar beams, with the gym's light reaching the edge of the path and the restaurant's warmth still in their clothes.

They did not go upstairs together. Gabe had an early appointment in the print room, and Dorian wanted to review the crossing photographs before the engineering meeting. They agreed to see each other again on Sunday, this time without making the evening an occasion. Gabe would bring coffee to the survey office; Dorian would show him the latest drawings. If the weather held, they would walk the western path afterward, stopping before the closed bridge. The arrangement was ordinary, which made it feel more substantial than a dramatic declaration.

When Gabe went inside, Dorian remained beneath the arcade for a few seconds. He could see the broad line of his shoulders reflected in the glass, the dark jacket, the pale collar, and the face of a man who had enjoyed being wanted. He did not need the reflection to confirm anything. He was pleased with the evening because Gabe had been present in it as himself: attentive, funny, direct, and capable of saying what he wanted without asking to be rewarded for saying it. Dorian turned toward the survey office and walked on.

Seth and Nick had been watching the campus from the upper gallery. The restaurant's windows were visible through the trees, and the two men had seen Dorian and Gabe return separately.

**SETH:**

A first date, then. No grand announcement, no parade through the dormitory, no declaration that the whole campus has to recognise a new couple. Just two men choosing to spend an evening together and discovering that the conversation works as well as the attraction.

**NICK:**

And the attraction is not incidental. They both wanted to be there. They both took care with how they looked. They enjoyed being noticed, and then they made a decision that belonged to them rather than to the audience.

**SETH:**

Dorian is a formidable presence. That hasn't become less true because he sat down for dinner and listened.

**NICK:**

Nor has Gabe become less interesting because he stopped comparing himself with him. The point is not to make one man smaller so the other can feel superior. The point is to let both men become more fully themselves.

The next morning, the campus had already moved on to other matters. The print room needed a new paper delivery, the gym staff were replacing a cable assembly, and Tyler had received another set of drainage photographs from the western approach. The bridge remained closed. The repair sequence was still under review, and no one was permitted to use the temporary brace as a reason to cross. Dorian arrived at the survey office with coffee and the previous night's calm still present in his expression. Gabe sent him a message from the print room: *Sunday. Coffee first. Then the drawings.* Dorian replied that he would be there.

Their first date had not settled the future. It had given them a reason to make another plan. In Northgate, where bodies continued to change and attention could become a powerful social force, that was worth recording accurately. Desire had been spoken aloud. Admiration had become mutual. A first kiss had been exchanged, and a second meeting had been chosen. Neither man had been assigned to the other. They had begun to make room for one another, which was a more useful beginning than any title the campus might have given them.
