# Chapter XCIII — The Lion's Stint

The first notice appeared on the board outside the Redwood residence office, between the revised grounds schedule and a reminder about the western footpath. It was printed on a sheet of old cream paper, although nobody in the office could remember having put it there. The lettering was formal and slightly uneven:

**THE FAMILIAR WILL KEEP HIS STINT WITH THE men THIS AUTUMN.**

Beneath it was a date, a duration, and the seal of the old Redwood household. No explanation followed. The men read it several times, each arriving at a different interpretation. Tyler thought it was an announcement for a visiting fellow. Andrew assumed it was some kind of ceremonial appointment. Gabe wondered whether the word *familiar* referred to a person who had been here before. Dorian, who had been looking at the seal, recognized the lion worked into its centre.

They knew the Golden Lion from the older accounts and from the great residence among the redwoods. He was no ordinary animal and no decorative emblem. He was a living, independent presence: immense, golden, warm in manner, proud without cruelty, and accustomed to being admired without requiring anyone to kneel. The stories described his friendship with the Master, his work with Hephaestus, his visits to the other houses, and his pleasure in a busy household. What none of the men had understood was that the old prophecy concerned them. The Lion would not simply receive them at his residence. He would come to spend a defined period among them.

The appointment began on a clear Monday. The men had gathered near the western entrance when the Lion came along the cedar walk, accompanied by two members of his household and a small party responsible for his care. His gold caught the morning light beneath the redwood canopy. His familiar expression was at once grand and amused, as though he knew exactly how seriously they were trying not to stare. He greeted the group without a speech, asking first whether they had eaten and then whether anyone had remembered to arrange a place where he could sit comfortably.

That practical question broke the ceremony. Andrew laughed. Tyler looked toward the veranda and began measuring the available space with his eyes. Gabe offered the broad bench near the doors, but the Lion inspected it and suggested that they find something built for a little more of him. Dorian led the way to the covered court, where a deep timber seat had been installed for visiting residents. The Lion settled there, tested its strength with a shift of his weight, and declared it acceptable. The men relaxed. Their familiar had arrived, and he appeared perfectly willing to begin with ordinary conversation.

His stint was scheduled to last until the end of the autumn term. The arrangement was not an adoption, a transfer of ownership, or a permanent change of residence. The Lion had other responsibilities and a home of his own. For these weeks, however, his days would be organized around the men: shared meals, guided walks through the grounds, visits to their workshops and recreation rooms, and time set aside for each of them to know him separately. The household staff remained responsible for the practical arrangements around his care. The men were not expected to manage every logistical detail themselves; they were there to become good companions.

They discovered quickly that the Lion was less interested in being treated as a prophecy than in being included. He wanted to know what they were making, which rooms they liked, what they ate after training, and why the covered passage near East Dormitory always seemed to collect damp leaves. He remembered names after hearing them once. He remembered preferences, too, and would ask after a project days after the person who had mentioned it had assumed he had forgotten. His humour was dry and often arrived at the exact moment when a conversation had become too solemn. When Andrew tried to give him a grand tour of the campus, the Lion listened to the introduction of every building and then asked whether the kitchen was any good. Andrew admitted that it was inconsistent. The Lion said that this was the first useful information he had received.

The men developed their own ways of spending time with him. Tyler was interested in the construction of the residence and asked about the old cedar joinery, the bronze fittings, and the way the high gallery carried sound. The Lion took him through the western rooms and showed him where Hephaestus had altered the original supports. Tyler began a careful set of drawings, not of the Lion himself but of the places that had been built to accommodate his life. Gabe preferred the training court. He and the Lion compared routines, talked about the difference between strength and display, and occasionally trained in parallel, each following a programme suited to his own body. Andrew brought conversation and movement; he liked to make the Lion laugh and was delighted whenever he succeeded. Joseph was quieter. He often joined the evening meal late and found that the Lion made room for him without asking him to account for the delay. Dorian became the one who could bring the group back together when their separate interests began to pull the day apart.

The Lion did not distribute his attention as if it were a ration. He could spend an afternoon with Tyler over a building plan and still remember to ask Gabe how his shoulder was recovering. He could laugh with Andrew in the courtyard and later sit with Joseph in the gallery while the house grew quiet. He did not mistake reserve for indifference or loudness for confidence. The men began to understand that being familiar to him did not mean becoming alike. Each relationship had its own shape, and none needed to displace the others.

The first outing beyond the campus took place along the old redwood path. The route passed the western residence, crossed a fern-filled hollow, and climbed toward a clearing where the oldest trees stood far enough apart to let the afternoon light reach the ground. The Lion walked at an easy pace, adjusting to the group's rhythm and leaving room for anyone who wanted to pause. At the clearing he showed them a stone marker bearing the same seal as the notice. It had been placed there during an earlier stint, many years before any of them had arrived. The inscription recorded the names of a previous company of residents, but the final line had been left open.

The Lion explained that the prophecy was never about selecting a permanent favourite. Each stint belonged to its own company. The company would be remembered together, but not because its members had all experienced the same thing. The term would give them time to discover what sort of companionship they could offer and accept. At its end, he would return to his own residence, and the next company would wait for its own season. The open line on the marker was reserved for them.

That knowledge changed the way they treated the remaining weeks. They stopped trying to make every meeting memorable. Some afternoons were ordinary: lunch, a walk, a conversation about a broken hinge, an argument over music in the common room. The Lion enjoyed these days most. He liked a household in use, with people coming and going and no need to turn every encounter into an audience. He attended the men' demonstrations, visited the workshop, watched a game from the edge of the field, and once spent an entire rainy afternoon listening to them disagree about the best route between the dormitories and the dining hall. When the argument became circular, he asked them to walk the routes together. They did, and found that each had been describing a different inconvenience.

Near the end of the term, the men held a supper in the covered court. They did not arrange a farewell ceremony. The Lion had made it clear that he disliked being turned into a monument while still sitting at the table. They served the food he had come to like, brought out Tyler's drawings, and placed the new record beside the old stone-marker inscription. Each man added something: a memory, a small observation, or a detail that would otherwise have been lost. Andrew wrote down the Lion's question about the kitchen. Gabe recorded the training sessions. Joseph described the quiet evenings in the gallery. Tyler included the western joinery. Dorian wrote about the first morning, when the Lion had asked for somewhere comfortable to sit.

On the appointed morning, the Lion left by the cedar walk. He did not vanish into legend. He said goodbye to each man, made one last joke about the veranda bench, and continued toward his own redwood residence with his attendants. The men watched until the trees hid him. Then they returned to the court, where the seat remained in place and the autumn light crossed the floor.

The stint had ended as promised. The Lion was no longer living among them, but the weeks had become part of the way they understood their company. They had learned how to welcome a presence without trying to own it, how to make room for different kinds of friendship, and how an ordinary day could become important without being staged as an occasion. The old notice was placed with their record. Beneath the date, they added the names of the men who had shared the season.

The final line was no longer blank.


The household did not return to its former arrangement all at once. During the first few days after the departure, the covered court continued to be prepared at the hour when the Lion usually came in from the grounds. The staff caught themselves leaving the western doors open, and the men found that they had begun to measure the afternoon by the light on the timber seat. Nobody proposed keeping the place empty as a shrine. The seat was useful, and the court belonged to everyone. But the men were reluctant to move it. Tyler eventually drew a new plan for the room, placing the seat where the Lion had preferred it and showing how the surrounding tables could be rearranged for ordinary use. The plan was adopted. The court became more accommodating than it had been before, not less, and the Lion's absence had quietly improved the place for the people who remained.

Tyler's drawings became the first practical inheritance of the stint. He had started by recording the old joinery and the special supports in the western rooms, but he now expanded the work into a survey of the residence as it was actually used. He marked the wide turns, the sheltered resting places, the paths that stayed dry after rain, and the places where a group could gather without blocking the passage. The Lion had made him look at architecture as a form of hospitality. Tyler had previously been interested in how a building stood; now he was equally interested in whom it allowed to feel at home. The new drawings were pinned in the residence office, where the grounds staff began consulting them before moving furniture or planning gatherings.

Gabe carried the stint into the training court. His conversations with the Lion had unsettled one of his old assumptions: that strength was most visible when it was being demonstrated. The Lion had never made a performance of his size. He moved when movement was useful, rested without embarrassment, and treated care as part of strength rather than its opposite. Gabe adjusted the way he organized the shared sessions. He began asking what each person wanted to improve before setting the day's work, and he stopped treating a missed session as a moral failure. The court became more consistent because the men could return to it without having to explain every interruption. He still liked hard training, and he still enjoyed being strong, but he had become less interested in making everyone prove that they deserved to be there.

Andrew's inheritance was less orderly. He had discovered that he could make the Lion laugh, and for a time he had mistaken that ability for a responsibility to keep every gathering lively. After the departure he tried to reproduce the same atmosphere, filling quiet evenings with stories and invitations. It took Dorian a while to tell him that the Lion had never needed entertaining. He had enjoyed Andrew's company because Andrew was himself, not because Andrew had kept the room in motion. Andrew was embarrassed by the distinction, then relieved by it. He began to let conversations pause. He also began to notice when another person was trying to speak and to leave a little space for them to finish.

Joseph kept the smallest record and perhaps the one he returned to most often. He wrote about the evenings in the gallery, the occasions when the Lion had made room beside him without asking why he had arrived late, and the calm of being allowed to sit without turning silence into an explanation. He did not show the pages to the others. They were not a report for the residence office. They were a private account of a companionship that had made no demands on him. In the weeks after the Lion left, Joseph found himself offering the same unforced welcome to other people. He began leaving the gallery door open when he knew someone might come by, and he learned that a quiet place could be shared without being claimed.

Dorian's part was to keep these separate changes from becoming separate traditions. He arranged for Tyler's drawings to be copied for the grounds team, asked Gabe to explain the revised training schedule at the next residents' meeting, and persuaded Andrew to help host an evening that did not depend on a single person carrying the conversation. He also made sure Joseph's account was included in the private season record only with Joseph's permission. The record was not meant to turn their experiences into a ranking. It was a way of remembering the stint accurately, including the parts that had mattered differently to each of them.

The household changed in less visible ways. The covered court became a place where people could arrive without an appointment. The western walk was maintained more carefully, and the clearing beneath the old cedar was added to the regular grounds route. A small table was placed near the gallery windows for anyone who wanted to read or work while others talked. The kitchen began keeping a better supply of the food the Lion had liked, not because anyone expected him to return immediately but because the men had discovered that a shared meal was easier to arrange when the household remembered what its guests enjoyed. None of these changes was grand enough to be called a new institution. Together, they made the residence more generous.

When winter came, the men returned to the stone marker in the clearing. The final line had been completed, but the inscription now seemed less like a conclusion than a record of a meeting between two periods of the house. They cleaned the moss from the carved seal and added the date of the stint to the residence register. The next appointment was not due for some time. No one knew which company would receive the Lion after them, or what the house would be like when he next came. That uncertainty no longer troubled them. They had learned that a familiar's visit was not measured by how long he stayed, but by how much of the relationship could be carried into ordinary life after he had gone.

The Lion sent a message before the first heavy snow. It was brief, warm, and full of his usual humour. He asked whether the western seat had survived the autumn, whether Gabe was still making everyone work too hard, and whether Andrew had finally learned that a silence did not always need rescuing. He thanked them for the season and said that his own residence had been unusually quiet without their company. The men read the message together in the covered court. Andrew began to answer immediately, then stopped and asked the others what they wanted him to say. They composed the reply together.

By then the court had become theirs in a way it had not been before. Not because the Lion had left it to them, but because they had learned to use it together. His stint had ended; the friendship had not. The next season would bring its own work, its own arrivals, and its own changes to the estate. The men would meet those things with a little more room for one another than they had possessed in autumn.
