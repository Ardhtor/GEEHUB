# Chapter XCIII — The Lion's Stint

The first notice appeared on the board outside the Redwood residence office, between the revised grounds schedule and a reminder about the western footpath. It was printed on a sheet of old cream paper, although nobody in the office could remember having put it there. The lettering was formal and slightly uneven:

**THE FAMILIAR WILL KEEP HIS STINT WITH THE BOYS THIS AUTUMN.**

Beneath it was a date, a duration, and the seal of the old Redwood household. No explanation followed. The boys read it several times, each arriving at a different interpretation. Tyler thought it was an announcement for a visiting fellow. Andrew assumed it was some kind of ceremonial appointment. Gabe wondered whether the word *familiar* referred to a person who had been here before. Dorian, who had been looking at the seal, recognized the lion worked into its centre.

They knew the Golden Lion from the older accounts and from the great residence among the redwoods. He was no ordinary animal and no decorative emblem. He was a living, independent presence: immense, golden, warm in manner, proud without cruelty, and accustomed to being admired without requiring anyone to kneel. The stories described his friendship with the Master, his work with Hephaestus, his visits to the other houses, and his pleasure in a busy household. What none of the boys had understood was that the old prophecy concerned them. The Lion would not simply receive them at his residence. He would come to spend a defined period among them.

The appointment began on a clear Monday. The boys had gathered near the western entrance when the Lion came along the cedar walk, accompanied by two members of his household and a small party responsible for his care. His gold caught the morning light beneath the redwood canopy. His familiar expression was at once grand and amused, as though he knew exactly how seriously they were trying not to stare. He greeted the group without a speech, asking first whether they had eaten and then whether anyone had remembered to arrange a place where he could sit comfortably.

That practical question broke the ceremony. Andrew laughed. Tyler looked toward the veranda and began measuring the available space with his eyes. Gabe offered the broad bench near the doors, but the Lion inspected it and suggested that they find something built for a little more of him. Dorian led the way to the covered court, where a deep timber seat had been installed for visiting residents. The Lion settled there, tested its strength with a shift of his weight, and declared it acceptable. The boys relaxed. Their familiar had arrived, and he appeared perfectly willing to begin with ordinary conversation.

His stint was scheduled to last until the end of the autumn term. The arrangement was not an adoption, a transfer of ownership, or a permanent change of residence. The Lion had other responsibilities and a home of his own. For these weeks, however, his days would be organized around the boys: shared meals, supervised walks through the grounds, visits to their workshops and recreation rooms, and time set aside for each of them to know him separately. The household staff retained responsibility for his care. The boys were not expected to manage a being of his scale by themselves; they were expected to become good companions.

They discovered quickly that the Lion was less interested in being treated as a prophecy than in being included. He wanted to know what they were making, which rooms they liked, what they ate after training, and why the covered passage near East Dormitory always seemed to collect damp leaves. He remembered names after hearing them once. He remembered preferences, too, and would ask after a project days after the person who had mentioned it had assumed he had forgotten. His humour was dry and often arrived at the exact moment when a conversation had become too solemn. When Andrew tried to give him a grand tour of the campus, the Lion listened to the introduction of every building and then asked whether the kitchen was any good. Andrew admitted that it was inconsistent. The Lion said that this was the first useful information he had received.

The boys developed their own ways of spending time with him. Tyler was interested in the construction of the residence and asked about the old cedar joinery, the bronze fittings, and the way the high gallery carried sound. The Lion took him through the western rooms and showed him where Hephaestus had altered the original supports. Tyler began a careful set of drawings, not of the Lion himself but of the places that had been built to accommodate his life. Gabe preferred the training court. He and the Lion compared routines, talked about the difference between strength and display, and occasionally trained in parallel, each following a programme suited to his own body. Andrew brought conversation and movement; he liked to make the Lion laugh and was delighted whenever he succeeded. Joseph was quieter. He often joined the evening meal late and found that the Lion made room for him without asking him to account for the delay. Dorian became the one who could bring the group back together when their separate interests began to pull the day apart.

The Lion did not distribute his attention as if it were a ration. He could spend an afternoon with Tyler over a building plan and still remember to ask Gabe how his shoulder was recovering. He could laugh with Andrew in the courtyard and later sit with Joseph in the gallery while the house grew quiet. He did not mistake reserve for indifference or loudness for confidence. The boys began to understand that being familiar to him did not mean becoming alike. Each relationship had its own shape, and none needed to displace the others.

The first outing beyond the campus took place along the old redwood path. The route passed the western residence, crossed a fern-filled hollow, and climbed toward a clearing where the oldest trees stood far enough apart to let the afternoon light reach the ground. The Lion walked at an easy pace, stopping when the boys stopped and allowing the slower members of the party to set the rhythm. At the clearing he showed them a stone marker bearing the same seal as the notice. It had been placed there during an earlier stint, many years before any of them had arrived. The inscription recorded the names of a previous group of boys, but the final line had been left open.

The Lion explained that the prophecy was never about selecting a permanent favourite. Each stint belonged to its own company. The boys would be remembered together, but not because they had all experienced the same thing. The term would give them time to discover what sort of companionship they could offer and accept. At its end, he would return to his own residence, and the next company would wait for its own season. The open line on the marker was reserved for them.

That knowledge changed the way they treated the remaining weeks. They stopped trying to make every meeting memorable. Some afternoons were ordinary: lunch, a walk, a conversation about a broken hinge, an argument over music in the common room. The Lion enjoyed these days most. He liked a household in use, with people coming and going and no need to turn every encounter into an audience. He attended the boys' demonstrations, visited the workshop, watched a game from the edge of the field, and once spent an entire rainy afternoon listening to them disagree about the best route between the dormitories and the dining hall. When the argument became circular, he asked them to walk the routes together. They did, and found that each had been describing a different inconvenience.

Near the end of the term, the boys held a supper in the covered court. They did not arrange a farewell ceremony. The Lion had made it clear that he disliked being turned into a monument while still sitting at the table. They served the food he had come to like, brought out Tyler's drawings, and placed the new record beside the old stone-marker inscription. Each boy added something: a memory, a small observation, or a detail that would otherwise have been lost. Andrew wrote down the Lion's question about the kitchen. Gabe recorded the training sessions. Joseph described the quiet evenings in the gallery. Tyler included the western joinery. Dorian wrote about the first morning, when the Lion had asked for somewhere comfortable to sit.

On the appointed morning, the Lion left by the cedar walk. He did not vanish into legend. He said goodbye to each boy, made one last joke about the veranda bench, and continued toward his own redwood residence with his attendants. The boys watched until the trees hid him. Then they returned to the court, where the seat remained in place and the autumn light crossed the floor.

The stint had ended as promised. The Lion was no longer living among them, but the weeks had become part of the way they understood their company. They had learned how to welcome a presence without trying to own it, how to make room for different kinds of friendship, and how an ordinary day could become important without being staged as an occasion. The old notice was placed with their record. Beneath the date, they added the names of the boys who had shared the season.

The final line was no longer blank.

— Ardhtor
