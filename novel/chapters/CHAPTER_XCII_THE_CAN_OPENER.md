# Chapter XCII — The Can Opener

The Long Table evening began with a tin of peaches.

It was not meant to be the important part. The men had gathered for the second open table, the one Boeskin had promised to hold again after writing NEXT WEEK TOO beneath the first invitation. There were rolls from the bakery, Pavel's chips—this time cooked evenly, though he denied having changed anything—and a pot of beans that Leon had brought from his flat. The peaches were supposed to go into a bowl for anyone who wanted them.

The can opener was missing.

Boeskin checked the drawer beside the sink. He checked it again, with the careful expression of a man who expected the drawer to apologize.

“It was here last week,” he said.

“Was it?” Gabe asked.

“I opened the tomatoes with it.”

“You opened the tomatoes with the little red one.”

“That was the little red one.”

“This is the little red one.”

Boeskin held up an empty hand.

Dorian stood at the kitchen hatch with a stack of plates. “Maybe we have two little red ones.”

“We do not.”

“You sound certain.”

“I labeled the drawer.”

Gabe opened it. Inside lay a corkscrew, two blunt butter knives, a packet of elastic bands, a small screwdriver, a wooden spoon with a split down the handle, and a folded paper menu from a restaurant that had closed three years earlier.

“Your label says ‘useful things,’” Gabe said.

“Those are useful things.”

“Is the menu useful?”

“It reminds us what we paid for lunch.”

The missing opener had been a plain steel model with black handles. It was not old, valuable, or especially good. It had a little rust mark near the turning wheel and required more effort than anyone thought a can opener should require. Its only distinction was that it had worked last week and was not present now.

That was enough.

The peaches waited on the counter. The gathering waited behind them. The men began trying to reconstruct the opener's last known location with the gravity of investigators revisiting an unexplained disappearance.

“I used it,” Leon said.

“On what?” Gabe asked.

“I don't remember.”

“Then how do you know you used it?”

“Because I remember the handle.”

Pavel looked at him. “You remember a handle but not the can?”

“It was a good handle.”

“It was an ordinary handle.”

“That's your opinion.”

Niko leaned over the drawer. “Maybe the can opener was in the dishwasher.”

“We don't put the can opener in the dishwasher,” Boeskin said.

“Why not?”

“Because it isn't dishwasher safe.”

“Did it say that?”

“It has a wooden grip.”

“It has black plastic grips.”

“Then it may be dishwasher safe.”

This was the first point on which the room divided. Boeskin maintained that the grips were blackened wood. Gabe said they were molded plastic. Leon remembered a black handle but could not testify to its material because, he said, he had been concentrating on the can. Pavel argued that the question was irrelevant because nobody had admitted putting the thing in the dishwasher. Niko turned the dishwasher's lower rack halfway out, just in case.

It was empty except for one spoon and a cup that had fallen onto its side.

Seth and Nick watched from the gallery above the Long Table.

**SETH:**

A missing can opener has stopped an entire kitchen.

**NICK:**

It has not stopped the kitchen. It has delayed a tin of peaches.

**SETH:**

The men have chosen to treat those as the same thing.

**NICK:**

That is how small disputes get their start. A man says, “I know what I saw,” and suddenly the peaches have witnesses.

The tin had a ring-pull tab. Gabe pointed this out.

“It's a ring pull,” he said. “We don't need the opener.”

“Then why are we looking for it?” Leon asked.

“Because it belongs in the drawer.”

“Which drawer?” Pavel said.

“The useful-things drawer.”

“That's where everything goes to disappear.”

“It hasn't disappeared. We know it's in the room.”

“We know a can opener is supposed to be in the room.”

Boeskin set the plates down. “I know which one we're talking about. Black handles. Steel wheel. Slight rust mark. Last week it was beside the sink.”

“Beside the sink is not a storage place,” Gabe said.

“It was drying.”

“It was dry.”

“Then it was being stored while dry.”

Dorian placed the plates beside the peaches. “The tin has a ring pull.”

The matter might have ended there if the ring pull had not been bent flat against the lid. It was visible, but the small tab had been pressed down. Gabe tried to lift it with a finger and stopped when the metal resisted.

“Don't force it,” Dorian said.

“I wasn't.”

“You were about to.”

“I was assessing it.”

“Assess it with a spoon,” Boeskin said. “There's one in the drawer.”

Niko picked up the wooden spoon, examined the split handle, and returned it.

“Not that one.”

Pavel found a teaspoon in a clean cup. Gabe slid its edge under the ring, lifted the tab, and pulled. The lid opened cleanly. The peaches had been freed without the can opener.

No one moved.

“Good,” Leon said.

“Excellent,” Pavel said.

“Now we still need the opener,” Boeskin replied.

The can opener had become a question of order. If it was in the room, it had a place. If it had been borrowed, someone should have returned it. If it had been put away somewhere else, the kitchen should know where. The peaches were open, but that had not answered any of those questions.

Gabe tipped the fruit into a blue bowl. “We can look after supper.”

“We can look now,” Boeskin said.

“It's been missing for ten minutes.”

“It's been missing since last week.”

“Last week you didn't know it was missing.”

“I knew it wasn't where I put it.”

“Where did you put it?”

“Beside the sink.”

“That was the drying place.”

“Then it was the drying place.”

Dorian lifted one shoulder. “I think we are looking for a can opener and an agreement about the word ‘put.’”

This earned a laugh from the men near the door. Boeskin pointed at him.

“You're making it sound petty.”

“It is petty.”

“It can be petty and still be irritating.”

“That's fair,” Dorian said.

The next ten minutes produced three competing accounts. Leon had used a black-handled opener to open beans, then left it by the sink. Pavel had put it in the utensil tray because the counter was wet. Boeskin had removed an opener from the tray because it was not the right one and placed it somewhere safer.

“What does ‘safer’ mean?” Gabe asked.

“Somewhere it wouldn't get knocked into the sink.”

“Where?”

Boeskin frowned. “I don't know. That's why I was looking.”

Pavel folded his arms. “You moved the opener and forgot where you put it.”

“I might have.”

“You might have? We have been discussing whether it has wood handles.”

“I was trying to make sure it didn't get damaged.”

“It is a can opener.”

“It has a wheel. Wheels can get damaged.”

“It's not a cart.”

“It's a tool.”

“It's a cheap tool.”

“Cheap tools still need to work.”

The exchange was turning circular. The men were not angry in any serious way, but they were invested in being the one whose memory proved reliable. Boeskin wanted the kitchen to be orderly. Pavel wanted the blame to land somewhere more specific than the drawer. Leon wanted his remembered handle to count as evidence. Gabe wanted the conversation to stop reproducing itself. Niko wanted to know whether anyone had checked the cupboard above the kettle.

They had not.

He opened it. Two mugs, a jar of tea, a folded apron, and the missing can opener sat on the lowest shelf.

Boeskin stared at it.

“That isn't where it belongs.”

“It's where you put it,” Pavel said.

“I put it there because the counter was wet.”

“Then Pavel put it away.”

“No. I put it there.”

“You said you were looking for it.”

“I forgot.”

The opener was retrieved. No one had damaged it. The rust mark was still there. The black handles were plastic. The turning wheel moved with the same slight stiffness as before. It had not become a better tool by being recovered.

Niko placed it in the drawer.

“Not there,” Boeskin said.

Niko withdrew his hand. “Where, then?”

Boeskin opened the drawer below the counter and found a small plastic organizer with empty compartments. “Here. With the kitchen tools.”

“That drawer has the potato peeler, the thermometer, and the scissors,” Gabe said.

“And the can opener.”

“The thermometer doesn't belong in the same section as the scissors.”

“They're in different compartments.”

“Then label the compartments.”

“They are labeled.”

The compartments had small paper labels, each written in Boeskin's blocky handwriting: PEELER, CUTTING, MEASURE, OPEN. The word OPEN had been placed under the can opener's empty slot.

Pavel read it. “That's clear.”

“Thank you.”

“Too clear,” Gabe said. “Now if it goes missing, the drawer itself will accuse us.”

“Then don't lose it.”

“That's the kind of standard the open table was founded to avoid,” Dorian said.

The group paused. The invitation's claim had been that no one needed to be good at anything to come sit down. The rule did not require them to be good at returning a can opener. It did require the kitchen to remain usable, and it was reasonable to ask a person who moved a shared tool to put it back. The difference was not complicated. It was just easy to obscure when everyone was defending a small point of pride.

Boeskin closed the drawer. “All right. If you move it, put it in the labeled slot.”

Pavel nodded. “And if you move it somewhere safe, tell someone.”

“I did not know I had moved it somewhere safe.”

“Then tell yourself.”

“That is not helpful.”

“It's the best I've got.”

Leon picked up a peach slice from the bowl. “I still think I opened beans with the other opener.”

“Which other opener?” Gabe asked.

“The little red one.”

“We have established there is no little red one,” Boeskin said.

“Then I remember the wrong color.”

“Or the wrong week.”

“Or the wrong can,” Niko added.

Leon considered that. “I can accept the wrong week.”

Pavel took the serving spoon and distributed peaches among the men who wanted them. The fruit was soft and cold from the tin. Nobody congratulated Gabe for opening it with a teaspoon. Nobody asked Boeskin to apologize for forgetting where he had put the opener. The dispute did not need a winner. It needed a place for the tool and an end to the argument.

Later, when the table had emptied, Boeskin checked the drawer once more. The can opener lay in the slot marked OPEN.

“You all right?” Gabe asked.

“Now I am.”

“You were very worried.”

“I was irritated.”

“About the can opener?”

“About people not putting things back.”

“You put it in the cupboard.”

“Exactly.”

Gabe smiled. “I appreciate the consistency.”

Dorian was at the door with the last stack of plates. “Is the opener staying here?”

“Yes,” Boeskin said.

“Then the story has a happy ending.”

“It has an ending.”

The difference mattered to Boeskin, so nobody challenged it.

The open table had been founded on a low bar: come in, sit down, eat something, and do not hurt anyone. The can opener had added a small clause. Shared tools could be misplaced. Men could be forgetful, defensive, and irritating about them. They could be corrected without losing their place at the table. A tool could have a labeled slot without the slot becoming a measure of character.

Next week, the invitation stayed pinned by the menu. Boeskin added one line beneath NEXT WEEK TOO:

PLEASE RETURN THE CAN OPENER TO ITS SLOT.

Pavel read it and added, in smaller handwriting:

EVEN IF YOU PUT IT SOMEWHERE SAFE.

Boeskin crossed that out.

Pavel wrote it again.

By the time the others arrived, the paper was covered in corrections and counter-corrections. The can opener remained in its drawer. The men entered, saw the note, and began arguing about whether “please” was necessary.

That was how the second open table became a regular one.
