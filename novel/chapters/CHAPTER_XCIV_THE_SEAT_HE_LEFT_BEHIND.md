# Chapter XCIV — The Seat He Left Behind

The first winter gathering in the covered court was not planned as a remembrance of the Lion. It was a working evening, called because the residence office needed decisions about the western walk, the training schedule, and the use of the gallery during the colder months. Tyler brought his revised drawings. Gabe brought the training calendar. Dorian brought the list of maintenance requests, and Andrew arrived with a tray of food that he had insisted was enough for everyone, though Joseph quietly brought another loaf because he knew Andrew's estimates were optimistic.

The timber seat remained beneath the western wall. It had been built for the Lion, but Tyler's new arrangement made it useful to the whole household. Two smaller benches had been placed nearby, and the tables could be moved without blocking the entrance. People sat where they liked. Some worked through the plans; others came only to eat and listen. The room no longer depended on a single large presence to give it a centre.

Tyler unrolled the drawing first. He had made three versions of the court: one for a full gathering, one for a small working group, and one for the days when the room needed to remain open as a passage. He explained the changes without treating the Lion's former requirements as an inconvenience. The wide turn near the doors had been retained. The timber seat had been shifted a little farther from the draft. A low shelf now held the maps and the season record, where anyone could consult them without asking the residence office.

“You've designed the room around people arriving,” Dorian said.

“That's what the room is for,” Tyler replied.

“It wasn't always.”

Tyler looked at the drawing again. “No. I suppose it wasn't.”

The comment stayed with them. The court had once been a space that people passed through on their way to somewhere else. During the Lion's stint it had become a place to receive someone whose presence altered the proportions of the room. Now it was a place where the residents could meet without needing a special occasion. The change was modest, but it had survived the departure.

Gabe's calendar produced a longer discussion. He had divided the training sessions by purpose rather than by rank or ability. There were strength sessions, conditioning sessions, technique sessions, and open hours for anyone who wanted to work at their own pace. He had also included recovery days and made it clear that nobody needed to explain an absence unless it affected someone else's arrangements.

Andrew read the page twice. “You've made it difficult to feel guilty.”

“That was intentional,” Gabe said.

“I'm not sure I trust a programme that doesn't make me feel guilty.”

“Then you can keep your old programme.”

“I don't want my old programme. I want to complain about the new one.”

The room laughed. Gabe let him complain, and then explained why the new schedule would make it easier for people to remain consistent. The Lion had helped him see that strength did not need to be constantly announced. A person could train seriously and still recognize that other people had different needs, different histories, and different capacities on a given day. The court would be better if more people could use it over time, not merely if a few people could make the hardest session.

Joseph had not intended to speak at the meeting. He sat near the end of the table, listening while the others worked through the practical details. When Dorian asked whether the gallery should remain open during the evening hours, Joseph said that it should. He suggested a lamp near the window, a small shelf for books, and a sign that made it clear people could sit there without joining a conversation. The idea was accepted almost immediately.

Andrew glanced at him. “You should have said that earlier.”

“I was thinking.”

“You can think out loud.”

“I know.”

There was no tension in the exchange, only a small recognition. Andrew had begun to leave room for other people to arrive at their own pace. Joseph had begun to offer his preferences before someone else guessed them. Neither had become a different person. The difference was that they had learned something about how to make space for one another.

After the meeting, the group stayed in the court. The formal decisions had been made, but no one was in a hurry to leave. Tyler pinned the drawings to the wall. Gabe wrote the first open training hours onto the board. Andrew divided the remaining food and discovered that Joseph had brought more bread than anyone could reasonably eat. Dorian carried the maintenance list back to the office, then returned when he heard them arguing about whether the Lion would approve of the new arrangement.

“He would say the seat is still too small,” Andrew said.

“He said it was acceptable,” Tyler replied.

“He said it was acceptable after testing it.”

“That is how furniture works,” Gabe said.

Joseph smiled. “He would probably ask whether the kitchen had improved.”

That settled the matter. The kitchen had improved, though not enough to prevent another disagreement about the best way to prepare supper. They decided to invite the Lion back for a visit when his duties allowed it. The invitation would not ask him to resume the stint or return to the old arrangement. It would simply offer him a place at the table.

The message arrived several days later. The Lion thanked them and accepted a visit for the beginning of spring. He also sent a note for Gabe, asking whether the new programme included time for doing absolutely nothing. Gabe read it aloud and said that it did, although he suspected the Lion would find the allotted time insufficient. Andrew immediately proposed a longer rest period. Tyler said that Andrew was attempting to turn the training calendar into a holiday schedule. The discussion continued until Dorian wrote both proposals on the board and marked them for review.

The winter went on. The covered court filled and emptied. The gallery lamp was used most evenings. The western walk remained clear after rain, and the cedar clearing became part of the regular route for anyone who wanted a longer walk. The Lion's season was no longer the subject of every conversation, but its effects were present in the way the house worked. The boys had not preserved his visit by keeping everything as it had been. They had preserved it by allowing the place to change.

When spring came, the Lion returned for a single afternoon. He arrived without attendants beyond those responsible for his care, greeted the boys as old companions, and inspected the covered court. He tested the timber seat, examined Tyler's drawings, and asked Gabe whether the training calendar had made the men lazy. Gabe said it had made them more consistent. The Lion considered this and declared consistency a respectable form of ambition.

He stayed for supper. Andrew made him laugh, Joseph showed him the gallery lamp, and Dorian brought out the season record. The Lion read the entries carefully, including the quiet account Joseph had permitted them to include. He did not turn the evening into a ceremony. He asked after the people who had joined the household since autumn, listened to the plans for the western rooms, and complimented the kitchen.

At the end of the visit, he stood in the courtyard and looked toward the cedar walk. The boys knew he would return to his own residence before nightfall. This time his departure did not feel like the closing of a door. They had already learned how to continue without him, and now they knew that a visit could be welcomed without trying to make it permanent.

The Lion paused beside the seat before leaving.

“Keep the place in use,” he said. “A good room should never become a monument to the person it was built for.”

Then he went down the cedar walk, his golden coat bright beneath the new leaves. The boys remained in the court, talking about the next gathering and the work still to be done. The seat was empty again, but the room was full.
