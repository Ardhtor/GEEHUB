# Chapter XCI — The Lowest Bar in Northgate

The invitation was printed on ordinary paper, with a corner bent where Tyler Boeskin had picked it up before the ink was dry. It had no photograph, no dress code, no list of achievements, and no promise that anybody would be interesting. The heading read OPEN TABLE — COME AS YOU ARE. Underneath, in smaller letters, Boeskin had added: “You don't need to be good at anything.” Dorian read it twice at the print-room door.

“You've advertised a very low standard,” he said.

“Across the board,” Boeskin replied. “It's important to be clear.”

Gabe came through the glass corridor with a paper bag under one arm. “What's the event?”

“An evening for men who are not especially good at anything.”

Gabe looked at the invitation. “That rules out half the campus.”

“That's the point.”

They had chosen the Long Table dining room because it was large enough for the men who usually gathered after training and ordinary enough not to make the evening feel like a ceremony. There were scuffed chairs near the window, a long oak surface with two small burns in it, a drinks machine that made a tired mechanical cough, and a noticeboard still carrying a flyer for a lecture that had taken place the previous month. Nothing in the room suggested that anyone had to arrive in perfect condition. A few men came straight from work. One wore a jacket with a missing button. Another had clearly dressed in the dark, judging by the two different socks. A broad fellow in an old football shirt had a damp patch beneath one arm and did not seem particularly concerned about it.

I had been writing the men in these stories toward an ideal without always noticing. They were large, capable, distinct, increasingly articulate about their wants, and often exceptionally good at the thing the scene required. Even their mistakes tended to reveal a hidden strength. I could see the pattern now that it had been named. I had made admission to the world look like a test. A man could be admired for his shoulders, his intelligence, his humour, his history, his discipline, or his capacity to change. The less remarkable man had to wait for a reason to be included.

The invitation unsettled that arrangement. Its standard was not excellence. It was attendance.

The first arrivals made the point without discussing it. One man came in, looked at the food, and asked whether he was allowed to take two rolls. Boeskin told him he was allowed to take four. Another admitted that he had read the invitation twice because he thought it might be a joke about a club he was not meant to join. A third stood near the door until Gabe waved him over and made room without asking him what he did. The men did not arrive as a collection of hidden talents. Some were talkative, some silent, some very ordinary in appearance, and a few appeared to have no subject in common beyond being adult men who had decided to come inside.

Dorian took a chair near the end of the table. His shoulders made the backrest look small, and he shifted the chair away from the wall so that he would not scrape the timber when he sat. A man opposite him glanced at the movement.

“Does that chair hold you?”

“Usually.”

“Usually?”

“Depends how much lunch I've had.”

The man laughed, and Dorian laughed too. That was the first conversation they had. It was enough.

Boeskin brought out a tray of pastries that had been bought cheaply from the bakery near closing time. A few were slightly squashed. One had split along the side and lost most of its filling. He placed them in the middle of the table as though they were a banquet.

“That's the standard,” he said. “Nothing gets rejected for being a bit bent.”

“That's your whole philosophy?” Gabe asked.

“It's a very useful philosophy.”

A man named Leon arrived late, carrying a gym bag with a broken zip. He had the build of a heavyweight rower, but his T-shirt was faded and a little too short, and his hair had flattened on one side. He apologised for being late, then explained that he had gone home to change and become trapped trying to fix the zip. Boeskin offered him a chair and a roll. Leon sat down, ate half of it in three bites, and asked whether anyone had watched the match. Nobody had. He explained the result anyway, getting two of the scores wrong. Nobody checked.

Pavel turned up with a paper plate of chips and announced that he had made them himself. They were unevenly cooked, with a few dark pieces at the bottom. He said the oven was unreliable. Niko took one, chewed, and told him it had a strong personality. Pavel looked pleased. The plate went around the table and was empty in minutes.

I noticed how little the men required from one another. They did not need a good story before they could laugh. They did not need the correct score. Nobody asked Leon to demonstrate his rowing strength or Pavel to explain what had gone wrong with the oven. Their bodies were present, but not under examination. A man could have a powerful chest and a broken bag; another could be soft around the waist and tell a joke no one quite understood. The evening continued without asking either man to resolve the contradiction.

Seth and Nick watched from the gallery above the dining room. The low ceiling lights rested on the table's worn surface, and the broad window held a reflection of the men more clearly than the dark courtyard outside.

**SETH:**

Look at the room. No special entrance, no grand display. One fellow has two different socks, another's brought chips that have seen better days, and the whole table is behaving as though this is exactly where they ought to be.

**NICK:**

That's because it is. You don't have to win a competition to get a chair. Though I do think the man with the broken zip deserves a new bag.

**SETH:**

Not tonight. Tonight we're keeping expectations low.

The men began talking about the campus, but not in any organised way. Someone complained about the showers. Someone else said the new soap smelled like a hospital corridor. A third had bought a television that could not connect to the wireless network and had been using it as a very expensive clock. Dorian offered to look at it, then admitted that he was not especially good with televisions. The man said he was not especially good at setting them up either. They agreed to try after supper.

Gabe asked one of the quieter men whether he was enjoying himself. The man shrugged.

“It's all right.”

“Good.”

“Food's decent.”

“That's the highest review we've had.”

The man nodded and returned to his plate. Gabe did not insist on drawing him into a more revealing conversation. I had been inclined to treat silence as a question that needed an answer. Here it was simply one of the ways a man could spend an evening. He had come. He was eating. He had not been asked to become more interesting.

The invitation's low standard moved through the room in small exchanges. A man admitted he had never finished a book he had started. Another said he did not understand the rules of the sport everyone assumed he followed. Someone confessed that he had been training for months and still could not do a proper pull-up. The table did not become a place of consolation. There was no round of reassurance and no effort to turn each weakness into a strength. They laughed, moved to another subject, and made sure he had enough food.

One of the men looked at Dorian's shoulders and asked whether he had always been that big.

“No,” Dorian said.

“Were you smaller?”

“Quite a bit.”

“Did you train?”

“Sometimes. Not enough to explain all of it.”

The man nodded as though this was a satisfactory answer. “Fair enough. I mostly walk.”

“Walking's good,” Dorian said.

“I'm not very good at it.”

Dorian looked at him. “It's walking.”

They both laughed. There was no hidden challenge in the exchange and no suggestion that the man ought to become a more impressive version of himself. The giant and the ordinary-sized resident had found a conversational level where neither had to perform.

Boeskin moved between the kitchen hatch and the table, collecting empty cups and finding more plates. His shirt was open at the neck, his broad chest visible above the fabric, and he received a few compliments from men who liked his build. He thanked them, made a joke about having chosen the shirt because it was clean, and asked whether anybody wanted the last pastry. He did not turn the attention into a contest. A man could be attractive without the evening becoming a ranking exercise.

Gabe watched him return to the kitchen. “You get on with everyone.”

“I get on with most people,” Boeskin said. “The trick is not asking too much.”

“What if someone is a pain?”

“Then he's a pain. He can still have a chair.”

Dorian considered this. “There must be a limit.”

“There is. Don't hurt people. Don't make them do things they don't want. Don't leave the kitchen in such a state that nobody can use it. Beyond that, I'm not running an academy.”

The answer was so plain that I kept returning to it. A low standard did not mean a world without limits. It meant that the limits were reserved for the things that mattered. Nobody needed to be impressive. Nobody needed to be good-looking. Nobody needed to have a compelling ambition. A man could be annoying and remain welcome. A man could be mediocre and still be wanted. But a refusal remained a refusal, and a shared room still needed to be usable by the next person.

Later, when the men had begun to drift away from the table, Leon returned with a replacement zip pull he had found in the maintenance cupboard. He had not repaired the bag properly; the teeth still separated when he loaded it. He showed the others the new pull and announced that it was a partial success. Pavel said it looked worse than before. Leon told him to fix his own oven. Niko laughed so hard that he knocked his cup over, and three men reached for the napkins. The spill was small. The table was wiped. Nobody turned it into a lesson about responsibility.

Dorian and Gabe stayed behind to help Boeskin stack the plates. Their hands met briefly over the last tray, and Gabe let his fingers rest against Dorian's for a moment. It was a quiet gesture among the ordinary evidence of the evening: crumbs, cups, damp sleeves, an empty paper bag, and the faded noticeboard. Dorian leaned closer and said something too low for the others to hear. Gabe laughed and shook his head. They did not need an audience for it.

I had begun the evening expecting to write a doctrine about lowered expectations. The room gave me something less abstract. It showed that the men did not have to be improved before they could be included. They could begin where they were: with poor chips, old shirts, a broken bag, a failed joke, a quiet mood, a little vanity, and no particular plan for becoming better. The world did not have to celebrate every shortcoming. It only had to stop treating each one as a reason to withhold company.

When the last men left, Boeskin folded the invitation and pinned it beside the Long Table's weekly menu. He wrote a small note beneath it: NEXT WEEK TOO. The paper was ordinary, the ink already beginning to fade where the dining room light reached it. There was no membership list and no test for returning. A man could come back because he liked the food, because he had nobody else to eat with, because he enjoyed Dorian's dry jokes, because Gabe remembered his name, or because he simply had nowhere he would rather be.

The bar had been low. It had also been wide.
