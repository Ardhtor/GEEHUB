# THE COUNTY LINE FEED
## A GEEHUB internet-narrative spinoff

**Status:** active branch; separate from main novel canon until its encounters are reconciled  
**Primary voice:** Bill, an older white Southern man, joined by two older men from the house  
**Format:** episodic environmental broadcast, archive encounter, conversation, response, consequence  
**Premise:** the men discover that the internet is not one place and that its stories do not stay where they are posted. Images, captions, edits, comments, reblogs, arguments, fantasies, jokes, and old memories travel between people. The men enter these narratives as observers first, then learn how to answer them without confusing a public image with the private person who may be behind it.

---

## The first feed

I had figured the internet would look more impressive.

There was a whole room set aside for it, mind you. The Hub had taken the old map room and fitted it with a long desk, three monitors, a wall display, and a chair that looked as if it had been built for a county sheriff. The cables ran down through the floor. A fan turned lazily in the corner. Somebody had put a coffee ring on the desk already, which made the whole operation seem more trustworthy.

The screen was showing a Tumblr archive.

Not a neat catalogue, either. It was a river of pictures and little remarks, some of them no more than a sentence. A man posing under stage lights. Another standing in a gym. A close view of a chest and shoulders. A photograph of a classical soldier carved in stone. Then a landscape, then a joke, then another enormous fellow looking as if he had been built to fill the doorway of a barn.

The posts had traveled a long way. Some had thousands of notes. Some had been passed around so many times that the original source was hard to see.

“Well,” I said, “that's a mighty mixed-up scrapbook.”

Harlan leaned back in his chair. He was a broad old fellow with a silver moustache and hands that looked as if they had spent forty years lifting things other people couldn't move. “Ain't a scrapbook. It's a feed.”

“What's the difference?”

“A scrapbook belongs to somebody.”

I looked at the screen again. “And this belongs to everybody?”

“That's the trouble,” he said. “Everybody thinks it does.”

The Hub brought up a post with a short caption about a bodybuilder's chest. The image was dramatic, the light hard across the upper body, the shoulders wide enough to change the whole outline. Harlan gave a low whistle.

“That's a lot of man.”

“It is,” I said. “But look at the caption. That's somebody's opinion. The photograph is one thing. The words are another. And the fellow in the picture is a third thing altogether.”

Luke had come in quietly. He stood behind us, still wearing the gray tank, his own shoulders making the chair backs look a little undersized.

“So what are we looking at?” he asked.

“Three records,” I said. “What the camera caught, what the poster said, and what the crowd made of it after.”

The Hub separated the post into layers. Original image. Caption. Reblog trail. Tags. Replies. The screen showed how the same picture acquired different meanings as it moved. One person admired the physique. Another made a joke. Somebody else compared it with an older photograph. A fourth person used it as a reference for a fictional transformation.

Harlan pointed at the last one. “There. That's where it starts changing.”

“Not the body,” Luke said. “The story.”

“Both, sometimes,” I told him. “But don't go mixing the two up.”

The machine opened a side panel and labeled it **PUBLIC NARRATIVE**. Beneath it appeared another label: **PERSON NOT FULLY KNOWN**.

I liked that. It was the first sensible thing the machine had said all afternoon.

We spent the next hour following the trail. The men learned the difference between an original post and a reblog, between a caption and a comment, between a source and somebody's interpretation of that source. They learned that a popular image could be old, reposted, cropped, edited, or separated from the name of the person who first shared it. They learned that a photograph could be used as inspiration without becoming permission to invent facts about the man pictured.

Harlan took to the tags. He treated them like labels on drawers in his workshop.

“Big shoulders,” he read. “Classic physique. Stage posing. Transformation. That's useful enough.”

“Useful for what?” Luke asked.

“For finding the next thing.”

The Hub began to assemble a visual study from the public material: silhouette, camera height, light direction, pose, proportions, clothing, and the way the room around a man established his scale. It did not copy the photographs into a new scene. It recorded what could be learned from them and built a separate study for Luke's rig.

That pleased me. A man can admire a thing without claiming to own it.

Then the feed moved.

A post about a bodybuilder gave way to a clip of blue volcanic fire. A caption called it unbelievable. The next post was a marble relief of an ancient soldier. After that came a sunset on Mars.

Harlan frowned. “How'd we get from shoulders to a volcano?”

“Same road everybody else takes,” I said. “One thing catches your eye, then another thing catches the part of your mind the first one woke up.”

Luke watched the blue light run down the screen.

“That's how the worlds connect.”

“Maybe,” I said. “But don't be too quick to call every connection a revelation. Sometimes a thing is just interesting.”

The Hub saved the sequence as an encounter, not a conclusion. It kept the source links, dates where available, captions, visible engagement, and our notes in separate fields. Then it asked whether the men wanted to answer the feed.

Harlan grinned. “Well, I reckon we ought to.”

I expected him to write something grand. Instead he typed:

**That's a fine piece of work. What was the light like when you were standing there?**

The question appeared beneath the post as a draft response, not yet sent.

I looked at him. “You planning to ask the fellow who took the picture?”

“If that's who posted it.”

“And if it ain't?”

“Then I won't pretend it is.”

That was the beginning of our arrangement with the internet. We could look. We could learn. We could make our own work in response. We could leave a question where a question belonged. But we would not turn a stranger into a character just because the screen had made him visible.

The Hub opened a new record.

**ENCOUNTER 001 — THE FEED IS NOT THE PERSON**

And beneath it, in smaller letters:

**NEXT: FOLLOW THE IMAGE BACK TO ITS SOURCE.**

Harlan pushed his chair away from the desk.

“Coffee first,” he said. “Then we'll see who made the thing.”

## Operating rules for the spinoff

1. The internet is an inhabited narrative environment, not a flat reference library. Posts, replies, tags, reblogs, edits, and links form paths the characters can follow.
2. The older men have distinct personalities and expertise. Bill is patient and discerning; Harlan is practical, sociable, and quick to notice craft; other recurring men may disagree, tease, or bring different histories.
3. They may admire, question, misunderstand, investigate, respond, create, and change their minds. Their interactions should produce scenes and consequences, not merely commentary.
4. Keep source material, community interpretation, fictional response, and GEEHUB canon visibly distinct. Do not invent a real poster's motives, identity, consent, or private life.
5. Public images can inform original studies of pose, proportion, lighting, camera, clothing, and composition. Preserve attribution and source links; do not present a reconstruction as the original photograph or video.
6. A feed encounter should leave a durable artifact: a source trail, visual study, written exchange, scene, rig adjustment, or new question.
7. Let the men be funny and opinionated. Their Southern voices should come from rhythm, judgment, memory, and relationships—not phonetic spelling or stock rural caricature.
8. The spinoff can cross into the main novel only when a specific discovery or event is deliberately promoted to canon.

## Episode vectors

- **The Image and the Man:** how a public photograph becomes detached from its subject.
- **The Reblog Trail:** Bill and Harlan follow a post through changing captions and communities.
- **The Old Gym on the New Screen:** an archive of bodybuilding imagery becomes a study of changing ideals and camera language.
- **A Comment Left Open:** the men draft a response, debate whether to send it, and learn that an unanswered message is still an event.
- **The Machine Learns Taste:** the Hub begins distinguishing the user's preferred visual grammar from popularity metrics.
- **The Men Make Their Own Feed:** original GEEHUB scenes and video studies enter the network with provenance intact.
