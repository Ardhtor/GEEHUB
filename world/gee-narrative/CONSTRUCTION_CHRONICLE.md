# GEEHUB — The Construction Chronicle

The chronicle is the living narrative of GEEHUB becoming a working world-making instrument. It is written from inside the construction process, not as promotional copy and not as a retrospective summary.

## Opening entry — The first machine

At first there was a clip: a body in a room, already moving, already carrying its own history. The first task was not to replace it. The task was to discover how a machine could change what happened to that body without losing the person who was there.

The first frame became a witness. The timeline became a measuring instrument. Resolve received the source and gave it a place to remain intact. Around it, GEEHUB began to construct separate spaces for evidence, growth passes, and the final image. The six marks—Baseline, Desire, Growth, Stress, Mutation, New Baseline—were not yet transformations. They were the first grammar by which a transformation could be made accountable.

The machine learned an important distinction early: arranging a process is not the same as performing it. A bin is not a body model. A marker is not a measurement. A curve is not a deformation. The chronicle keeps these distinctions visible because the world must be able to tell the difference between what it has imagined, what it has measured, and what it has actually made.

## The workshop acquires a memory

The repository became the first stable memory of the machine. Codex instructions describe how work should continue. Skills preserve repeatable methods. Resolve scripts establish the editorial room. Dropbox is designated as the exchange space for assets, evidence, bridge records, and exports. None of these rooms is the world by itself. Their connections are the beginning of the world.

GEEHUB's construction is therefore recorded as a sequence of working discoveries. Each entry should identify the artifact created, the capability it actually provides, the evidence that it works, and the next unresolved operation. The story must advance when the machine advances; it must not invent progress to make the story sound complete.

## The governing image

A frame is a surface carrying evidence of a body. A sequence is a body changing through time. A world is the set of relations that makes that change legible: joints, silhouette, weight, fabric, floor, walls, camera, light, sound, and the people who recognize the subject.

The intended sensation is escalation with continuity. Scale may become startling; identity must remain. Growth should be felt in the whole scene, not merely in a swollen patch of pixels. The room may become too small. Fabric may pull. The camera may need to look upward. Yet every consequence must arise from a coherent state, not from arbitrary distortion.

## 2026-11-08 — The mask bench learns to read the image

The workshop no longer treats a PNG header and a matching checksum as sufficient evidence of a usable body mask. A new decoder, `core/mask-pixels.js`, opens the compressed image data and reconstructs its scanlines. It checks the chunk checksums, reads the dimensions, and reverses each of the five standard PNG row filters. Its accepted language is intentionally small: 8-bit grayscale, no interlace, black for background, white for foreground. Palette images, RGB images, alpha masks, soft probability maps, damaged chunks, and oversized dimensions are refused rather than interpreted by guesswork.

The frame worker now reads each mask's bytes once for both hashing and decoding. It measures the foreground area and bounding rectangle of the body and face masks, then lays the two binary regions against the same frame coordinates. A frame can pass the geometric intake only when both regions contain pixels and their foreground does not overlap. This is a concrete check of the producer's face-exclusion claim: the two supplied masks are disjoint. It is not a face detector, and it cannot prove that the upstream model found the correct face or the correct body. The audit therefore separates `pixelDataVerified` and `faceExclusionVerified` from `pixelSemanticsVerified`, which remains false.

The pixel audit is carried into the segmentation sidecar and manifest metadata. The bridge adapter refuses a missing or incomplete audit. During render-job preparation, the local worker decodes the current mask files again and compares the result with the registered audit; a mask that has changed, or an audit whose pixel counts no longer describe the files, cannot enter the queued job. When supplied, the fresh audit is carried into the job's `frameEvidence`. Older registered sidecars without the additive audit summary remain readable, but they do not bypass the fresh local pixel check.

## 2026-11-09 — The mask bench lays down a common triangle field

The decoded body and face masks now provide more than a pair of measured regions. A new instrument, `core/mask-mesh.js`, lays a bounded rectangular grid across the body silhouette and gives each lattice point a persistent row-and-column identity. Every cell offers two candidate triangles. The instrument tests those triangles against every frame in the supplied sequence, retaining a triangle only when the pixel centres it covers are body foreground and protected-face background throughout the sequence. It uses row-prefix counts to make that coverage test exhaustive without walking every pixel in each triangle separately.

The output is a common index field with one topology digest, stable vertex IDs, stable triangle IDs, and a set of source-pixel coordinates for each frame. When the subject shifts, the grid points follow the changing body bounds while their identities remain fixed. The instrument then checks which triangles share edges and counts the resulting components. A mesh is ready only when the common triangles form one edge-connected component and meet the configured minimum count. A fragmented grid returns `disconnected`; a connected but sparse grid returns `insufficient-coverage`. It does not invent a triangle to fill a gap.

The frame-sequence worker now invokes this instrument after the existing file checksum, PNG decoding, binary-value, non-empty-mask, and body/face-disjointness checks. Its audit carries the resulting `maskMesh` separately from `readyForGeometry`, with `meshReady` indicating whether enough common triangles survived. This distinction matters: mask intake can be valid even when a usable common mesh cannot be found.

## 2026-11-11 — The surface acquires a measure of motion

The mesh workbench now measures not only where each vertex is going, but how quickly the deformation itself changes from one source frame to the next. The new temporal pass in `core/mesh-deformation.js` compares each vertex's destination offset with its source coordinate, then measures the change in that offset over the actual timestamp interval. The result is normalized by the tracked body's height, so the limits describe motion relative to the subject rather than an arbitrary number of pixels that would mean something different in every resolution.

The first guard measures deformation velocity; the second compares adjacent interval velocities to estimate acceleration. The defaults are 1.5 body-heights per second and 40 body-heights per second squared, and a caller may provide stricter positive limits for a particular shot. A two-frame sequence can report velocity but cannot establish acceleration, so that field remains explicitly unavailable until a third frame exists. Non-increasing timestamps and non-contiguous frame numbers are refused instead of being divided through or silently reordered.

A sequence that crosses either limit is marked `blocked-temporal-instability`. Every frame plan is also made non-renderable, so a caller cannot take one apparently good frame from an unstable run and bypass the sequence decision. The audit names the interval and vertex responsible for the peak, reports the real elapsed time, and preserves the existing fixed vertex and triangle identities. This is a geometric continuity test, not optical flow and not a promise that motion will look perceptually smooth; tracker quality, semantic mask correctness, clothing response, and depth-aware occlusion remain separate problems.

## 2026-11-14 — The mesh begins to leave a pixel surface

The raster bench now has a CPU path that takes one ready deformation plan and turns its destination triangles into a transparent RGBA body layer. The input is a frame-numbered source buffer, the exact body mask, the protected-face mask, and the source-to-destination correspondence already produced by the growth solver. The rasterizer walks pixel centres inside each destination triangle, calculates the corresponding source position with barycentric coordinates, and samples the original frame with bilinear interpolation in premultiplied-alpha space. Each bilinear tap is filtered through the body and protected-face masks, so the interpolation cannot pull background or face colour across a mask boundary. This gives the mesh a concrete image surface to carry: a translated test triangle samples the expected source pixels, and the baseline plan reproduces the covered pixels without altering the source buffer.

The new instrument checks its own boundaries rather than trusting the earlier plan alone. Source triangles must lie within body foreground; source and destination triangles may not cross protected-face pixels, leave the image, collapse, or reverse orientation. When adjacent triangles touch the same output pixel centre, they must agree on the source coordinate or the rasterizer refuses the overlap. The output is a separate transparent layer with a coverage mask, not a completed frame. It does not contain the untouched background or face layer, and it does not reconstruct the background revealed when the body moves.
